03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time you found a critical bug late in the release cycle.

*Situation:* I was on a payments feature team at a previous company. Three days before launch, I ran exploratory tests on the refund flow.
*Task:* I needed to verify whether partial refunds calculated correctly across different payment gateways.
*Action:* I wrote a focused regression script covering several edge cases the original test plan had missed. I flagged the issue to the PM and dev lead immediately, shared a reproducible test case, and proposed a targeted fix scope so we did not have to delay the full release.
*Result:* The dev team patched the specific gateway logic in one day, we shipped on time, and the refund error rate in production dropped to near zero in the first month.

---

Q: How do you decide what to automate versus what to test manually?

*Situation:* At a previous company, the team had a growing regression suite but kept automating tests that rarely failed while ignoring higher-risk flows.
*Task:* I was asked to review the automation strategy and improve its return.
*Action:* I mapped each test case to three factors: frequency of execution, historical defect rate, and stability of the underlying feature. Tests that ran every sprint, covered critical paths, and had stable selectors went into automation. Edge cases tied to business rules that changed often stayed manual.
*Result:* Automation maintenance time dropped, and the manual team focused effort on flows that actually caught bugs.

---

Q: How do you handle a developer who disputes a bug you raised?

*Situation:* I once raised a defect where a form accepted invalid date inputs. The developer argued it was 'by design.'
*Task:* I needed to resolve the disagreement without escalating unnecessarily or damaging the working relationship.
*Action:* I pulled up the requirement document and the UX spec, showed the expected validation behaviour, and ran the test live in a screen share. I kept the conversation focused on user impact, not blame.
*Result:* The developer acknowledged the gap, fixed the validation the same day, and we updated the acceptance criteria together to prevent similar gaps in future releases.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions. Every 'tell me about a time...' question deserves a clear Situation, Task, Action, and Result. Keep the Situation short (two sentences), spend most time on Action (what you specifically did), and end with a measurable or concrete Result.

The 'why, what, how' structure for technical questions. When asked about tools or processes, explain why you chose that approach, what you did, and how you measured success. For example, if asked about test coverage, state why coverage matters to you, what metric you track (line coverage, branch coverage, risk-based coverage), and how you have acted on the data.

The 'impact then detail' structure for bug stories. Lead with the impact the bug would have had on users if it had shipped, then walk through how you caught it. Interviewers remember the stakes more than the technical steps.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

QA interviews at companies with large QA teams like SKD SE typically assess three things: technical depth, communication style, and ownership mindset.

Technical depth means being comfortable with test design techniques (equivalence partitioning, boundary value analysis, exploratory testing), automation architecture, and handling flaky tests or unstable environments. Be ready to explain decisions, not just list tools.

Communication comes up repeatedly. Candidates report that interviewers ask how you explain defects to non-technical stakeholders, how you write a bug report a junior developer can act on without clarification, and how you push back on shipping decisions without creating conflict.

Ownership mindset separates mid-level from senior QA hires. Interviewers want to see that you care about quality beyond your own test cases, that you have flagged process gaps proactively, and that you think about what happens after your tests pass.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Foundations.
Review test design techniques you have not used recently. Practise writing test cases for a common feature (login, search, checkout) and time yourself. Brush up on whichever automation framework you plan to discuss, whether Selenium, Cypress, Playwright, or Appium, depending on the role's focus.

Week 2: Company context.
Research SKD SE's products and the domain they operate in. Tailor your examples to that domain where possible. Candidates report that showing genuine domain awareness tends to land well in early discussions with the hiring team.

Week 3: Behavioural preparation.
Write out five to seven stories from your work history using the STAR format. Cover: a bug you found at the last minute, a disagreement with a developer, a process you improved, and a time you worked under release pressure. Practise saying them aloud so they sound natural, not rehearsed.

Final days: Mock interviews.
Do at least two timed mock sessions where you answer each question without pausing longer than thirty seconds. Ask a peer to push back on your answers so you practise defending your reasoning under pressure.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Vague bug stories. Saying 'I have found many bugs in my career' without a specific example tells the interviewer nothing. Always prepare one concrete story with a real impact and a clear resolution.

Ignoring the 'why' behind your tools. Listing Selenium, JIRA, and Postman on your resume and then failing to explain why you used each one is a common gap. Be ready to compare tools and justify your choices based on project needs.

Skipping edge cases in live test exercises. If the interview includes a test case writing exercise, candidates often miss boundary values and negative scenarios. Interviewers notice this immediately because it signals shallow test thinking.

Treating QA as a gatekeeper role. Framing all your answers around 'catching bugs' rather than 'enabling quality early' can signal a junior mindset. Talk about how you joined requirement reviews or sprint planning to prevent defects upstream, not just find them later.

Not asking questions at the end. Candidates who ask nothing signal low interest. Prepare two or three genuine questions about the team's current test coverage, release cadence, or tooling decisions.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the SKD SE QA Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report a process that typically includes a recruiter screening call, one or two technical rounds, and a final HR or offer discussion. The exact count can vary by team and seniority level. Confirm the process with your recruiter after the first call so you can plan your preparation accordingly.

What automation skills does SKD SE look for in a QA Engineer?

Based on what candidates report for similar roles, strong familiarity with at least one web automation framework (Selenium, Cypress, or Playwright) is commonly expected. API testing with tools like Postman or RestAssured comes up frequently as well. Be ready to discuss how you structure and maintain a framework, not just write individual test scripts.

What salary can I expect as a QA Engineer at SKD SE?

SKD SE does not publicly report salary figures by role. Using industry bands from knok jobradar, mid-level QA Engineers with 3-5 years of experience are typically in the 9-17 LPA range, and senior engineers with 6-9 years are commonly in the 17-30 LPA range. Your actual offer will depend on your experience, the specific team, and how you negotiate.

Is SKD SE a good company for QA Engineers looking for career growth?

SKD SE has 86 active QA Engineer openings as of July 2026, which suggests an active and expanding QA function. Large QA organisations generally offer more pathways to specialise in automation engineering, performance testing, or security testing. Ask your interviewer directly about team structure and how engineers move into senior or lead roles to get an honest picture.

How do I prepare for a live test case writing exercise?

Pick a feature you use every day (a login form, a search bar, a checkout flow) and practise writing test cases for it in ten minutes. Cover the happy path, boundary values, negative inputs, and integration points with other systems. Read your test cases aloud to check whether someone unfamiliar with the feature could execute them without asking you questions.

What is the difference between a QA Engineer and a QA Analyst role when it comes to interview expectations?

QA Engineer roles typically place more emphasis on automation, scripting, and framework design. QA Analyst roles lean more toward manual testing, test planning, and defect management. For a QA Engineer interview at SKD SE, expect to be assessed on your ability to write and maintain automated tests, not just identify bugs through manual exploration.
