Most Asked Questions

Candidates typically report the following questions across Haptik MLE rounds:

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time you debugged a model that performed well offline but poorly in production.

*Situation:* At my previous company, we built an intent classifier for a retail chatbot. Our offline evaluation showed strong performance on the internal test set.

*Task:* After going live, support tickets showed the model was frequently misclassifying queries about order returns, even though this was a common user intent.

*Action:* I started by logging a sample of real production queries and comparing them to our training data. I found two issues: the training set had been collected from a controlled pilot with formal language, but real users typed short, fragmented messages with spelling variations; and the 'return order' intent had very few training examples relative to its real-world frequency. I augmented the training data using paraphrasing tools and back-translation, re-balanced the sampling weights, and added a lightweight post-processing layer for common misspellings.

*Result:* After retraining and a staged rollout, the misclassification rate for the return intent dropped substantially. The fix also improved overall user satisfaction scores, which we tracked via post-chat surveys.

---

Q: How would you approach entity extraction for code-mixed (Hinglish) input?

*Situation:* A previous project required extracting product names and quantities from messages like 'mujhe ek blue shirt chahiye' mixed with English.

*Task:* Standard English NER models performed poorly because they were not trained on code-mixed text.

*Action:* I evaluated three approaches: a multilingual model (mBERT), a fine-tuned model on a small Hinglish NER dataset we annotated internally, and a rule-based fallback for structured patterns like quantities and sizes. I ran ablation experiments to measure each component's contribution. The fine-tuned multilingual model with rule-based post-processing gave the best balance of recall and precision for our use case.

*Result:* Entity extraction accuracy improved significantly over the baseline English-only model, and the rule-based layer handled edge cases the model missed. This directly reduced the number of clarification turns in the conversation flow.

---

Q: How do you reduce latency for an LLM-powered feature that needs to respond quickly?

*Situation:* We were integrating an LLM for response generation in a customer-service flow. Initial latency was too high for a good user experience.

*Task:* I was asked to bring down median response time without sacrificing answer quality.

*Action:* I profiled the full request pipeline and found the biggest bottlenecks were prompt size and sequential API calls. I reduced prompt length by moving static instructions into a cached system prompt, switched to streaming responses so the UI could display text as it arrived, and parallelised two independent LLM calls that had previously run in sequence. I also evaluated a smaller, faster model for a subset of simple queries.

*Result:* Median latency dropped considerably, and the streaming UI made the experience feel much faster even before the full response was ready. User drop-off during the loading state decreased noticeably.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use STAR for behavioural questions. Situation and Task together should take no more than a couple of sentences. Spend most of your time on Action (your specific choices and reasoning) and Result (a concrete outcome, even if qualitative).

For system design questions, candidates report that Haptik interviewers appreciate a structured walk-through: start with the problem scope and constraints (latency, scale, data availability), then move to component design, then evaluation strategy, then deployment and monitoring. Do not jump straight to model choice.

For ML theory questions, explain the intuition first, then the math if asked. Haptik's work is applied, so connecting theory to a practical trade-off (for example, 'attention is powerful but costly at long context lengths, so for our chatbot we chose...') lands better than a textbook recitation.

For debugging and production questions, structure your answer as: observe (what signal told you something was wrong), hypothesise (what you thought the root cause was), experiment (how you confirmed it), and fix (what you changed and why).

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Haptik builds products used by millions of real users, so interviewers typically look for engineers who can connect ML decisions to business outcomes. A candidate who says 'I chose this architecture because it reduced latency for our users' will typically score higher than one who only discusses benchmark numbers.

NLP depth is non-negotiable. Given Haptik's core product, expect close follow-up on anything you claim about transformers, pre-trained models, or dialogue systems. Be ready to go a level deeper on any technique you mention.

Production mindset matters. Candidates report that Haptik panels pay close attention to how you think about monitoring, retraining triggers, and failure modes. Mentioning experience with A/B testing, shadow deployments, or model drift detection signals seniority.

Collaboration is valued. Many Haptik MLE roles sit at the intersection of research and product engineering. Showing that you have worked closely with product managers or software engineers to ship features, not just built models in isolation, is typically viewed positively.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: NLP and transformer fundamentals. Review the attention mechanism, BERT-style pre-training, and how fine-tuning works. Practise explaining these concepts out loud without jargon. Study intent classification and NER as specific tasks, including handling low-resource and multilingual scenarios.

Week 2: Haptik-specific context. Read Haptik's public engineering blog and any published case studies to understand the kinds of problems their ML team solves. Note the scale (enterprise clients, high-volume conversations) and the product context (customer support, e-commerce, banking).

Week 3: System design and production ML. Practise designing an end-to-end conversational AI pipeline on paper: data collection, model training, evaluation, serving, and monitoring. Be ready to discuss latency optimisation, model versioning, and how you would handle a sudden drop in production accuracy.

Week 4: Behavioural preparation. Write down three to five projects you are proud of and structure each as a STAR story. Prepare stories that highlight: debugging a production issue, improving a model meaningfully, and working cross-functionally. Practise with a peer or record yourself.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Claiming NLP expertise without depth. Many candidates list BERT or GPT on their resume but struggle to explain tokenisation, subword vocabularies, or why positional encodings matter. If you mention a technique, be ready to go one level deeper.

Skipping evaluation in system design. A common gap is designing a great model architecture but not discussing how you would measure success in production. Always include an evaluation strategy.

Using only vague results. Saying 'accuracy improved' without context is weak. Add: what the business impact was, what the sample size was, and whether the gain held up over time.

Being vague about your specific contribution. In team projects, interviewers want to know what you personally built, decided, or fixed. Use 'I' when describing your actions, not 'we'.

Ignoring Indian-language and code-mixed challenges. For a company like Haptik, which serves a large Indian user base, not having a thoughtful answer about multilingual or Hinglish inputs can signal a gap in contextual awareness.

How many interview rounds does Haptik typically have for MLE roles?

Candidates report a process that typically includes a recruiter screening call, one or two technical rounds covering ML fundamentals and coding, a system design round, and a hiring-manager or leadership conversation. The exact number of rounds can vary by seniority and team. Always confirm the format with your recruiter after the first call.

Does Haptik ask coding questions in MLE interviews?

Candidates typically report that MLE rounds at Haptik include at least one coding session, usually focused on data manipulation, algorithm problems relevant to NLP (like string processing or dynamic programming), or implementing a small ML utility. It is less commonly a pure competitive-programming round and more applied. Brush up on Python, NumPy, and pandas alongside your ML prep.

How important is LLM experience for Haptik MLE roles?

Given Haptik's product direction in conversational AI, LLM experience is increasingly valued. Candidates report questions about prompt engineering, retrieval-augmented generation, and LLM evaluation in recent interview cycles. Even if your direct LLM experience is limited, showing you understand the trade-offs around cost, latency, accuracy, and hallucination risk, and have experimented with publicly available models, will help.
