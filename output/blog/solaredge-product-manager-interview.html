01 Overview

Overview

SolarEdge is a global clean-energy technology company best known for solar inverters, energy storage systems, EV chargers and grid-edge monitoring software. It sells primarily through certified installers rather than directly to homeowners, which makes the PM role distinctly different from a typical consumer tech job. Product Manager positions sit at the intersection of hardware, firmware and cloud software. You will be expected to understand field constraints such as heat tolerance, grid regulations and installer workflows, alongside the usual PM toolkit of roadmaps and metrics. Candidates report that interviews typically span three to five rounds covering product sense, technical depth, cross-functional collaboration and domain knowledge in energy or IoT.

Most Asked Questions

  2. SolarEdge inverters generate continuous telemetry data from millions of panels. How would you decide which metrics to surface to installers versus homeowners?
  3. A firmware update causes a measurable drop in inverter efficiency for a specific panel configuration. How do you manage the rollout decision and communicate with affected customers?
  4. How would you prioritize a feature requested by a large commercial client against a reliability fix affecting a smaller group of residential users?
  5. SolarEdge operates in regulated energy markets. Describe how you have worked with legal or regulatory teams to ship a product on time.

  10. Tell me about a product decision you made with incomplete data. What was your process and what did you learn?
  11. How would you reduce churn among installers on the SolarEdge monitoring platform?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: SolarEdge inverters generate continuous telemetry data. How would you decide which metrics to surface to installers versus homeowners?

*Situation:* At my previous company we built an IoT monitoring platform for industrial equipment, with a similar tension: a rich data stream and two very different audiences with completely different mental models.

*Task:* My job was to define what each audience saw so they could act on it without information overload.

*Action:* I ran separate discovery sessions with field service engineers (analogous to installers) and with end customers. Installers needed fault codes, equipment health scores and alert thresholds to triage remotely before dispatching a technician. End customers cared about energy output today, estimated savings and a simple status indicator. I worked with the data team to tag each metric by primary audience and built two distinct dashboard views from the same pipeline. We ran a short pilot with a small group from each audience, gathered structured feedback after two weeks and iterated before full rollout.

*Result:* Support tickets related to remote diagnosis dropped noticeably. Customer satisfaction on the monitoring experience improved in our next quarterly survey. The dual-view model became the platform standard.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you had to align a hardware R&D team and a software team who disagreed on a product direction.

*Situation:* I was PM for a connected hardware product. Midway through a release cycle, the hardware team wanted to delay a key feature by one quarter because it required a new sensor that had not been validated. The software team had already built the backend and did not want to hold the release.

*Task:* I had to make a scope call while keeping both teams aligned and the release date intact.

*Action:* I set up a joint review where both teams presented their constraints. I framed it as a shared problem: what is the smallest version of this feature we can ship safely with current hardware, while leaving the full version for next quarter? We agreed to ship a read-only version using existing sensors, satisfying the software team's timeline while giving hardware time to validate the new component. I documented the decision, the rationale and the Q+1 commitment in the roadmap so there was no ambiguity.

*Result:* The release shipped on time. The full feature launched the following quarter with no rework needed on the software side because we had architected for it from the start.

---

Q: Tell me about a product decision you made with incomplete data.

*Situation:* We were deciding whether to sunset a legacy API version that a small segment of installer partners still used. Usage data was incomplete because some partners had not updated their reporting tools.

*Task:* I had to make a deprecation decision without full visibility into who would be affected.

*Action:* I pulled the data we had, segmented partners by usage pattern and identified that the uncertain segment represented a small share of total API calls. I reached out to partners in that segment with a short survey to self-identify their dependency. In parallel, I worked with engineering to add a deprecation warning directly in the API response so any affected partner would see it immediately. I set a go/no-go checkpoint four weeks out, using survey response rate and new support tickets as signals.

*Result:* The survey gave us enough confidence to proceed. The deprecation went smoothly. One partner reached out post-launch; we gave them a short extension and they migrated quickly.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioral questions. Most SolarEdge PM interviews open with behavioral rounds. Keep Situation and Task brief (two to three sentences each) and spend most of your time on Action, the choices you made and why. Result should include a real signal, even a qualitative one, not just 'it went well.'

Product design: problem before solution. When asked to design a feature, resist jumping to the UI. Start by clarifying who the user is (installer, homeowner, grid operator), what problem they have and why it matters to the business. Candidates report that interviewers respond well to answers that reference real hardware constraints such as certification timelines and field update cycles.

Prioritization: use a simple impact-versus-effort framing. For prioritization questions, frame your answer around customer impact and feasibility given SolarEdge's hardware-software constraints. A simple two-axis grid is easier to explain out loud than complex scoring models. Be ready to defend your assumptions when pushed.

Metrics: name the North Star first. For success criteria questions, name the primary metric first, then supporting metrics, then guard rails. For SolarEdge products, installer retention and system uptime are often more important than the engagement metrics that dominate consumer app answers.

Market entry: customers, channel, regulation. For questions about entering a new market, organize your answer around three pillars: who are the target customers, how does the installer and distributor channel work locally, and what regulatory approvals are needed before you can sell. This structure shows you understand SolarEdge's go-to-market reality.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Hardware and field empathy. SolarEdge PMs work with firmware teams, hardware engineers and field installers. Interviewers want to see that you understand constraints that do not exist in pure-software roles: firmware update risks, certification cycles and the real cost of a field recall. Even with a software-heavy background, show you have thought through these dynamics.

Domain curiosity in energy. You do not need to be an electrical engineer. You should know what a solar inverter does, why monitoring uptime matters for installers and how the residential, commercial and utility segments differ in their needs. Candidates report that informed, specific questions at the end of each round are consistently noticed by interviewers.

Cross-functional clarity. SolarEdge PM roles span hardware R&D, firmware, cloud software, sales, regulatory and customer success. Interviewers typically probe for examples where you drove alignment across functions with competing priorities and tight timelines.

Data-driven but decisive. Interviewers want candidates who know how to use data without hiding behind it. Show that you can make a call under uncertainty, document your reasoning and revisit it when new information arrives.

Structured communication. Because SolarEdge sells through installers, PMs often communicate complex technical decisions to non-technical partners. Interviewers look for candidates who can simplify without losing the precision that hardware decisions require.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: know the product and the market. Explore the mySolarEdge app and the SolarEdge monitoring portal (publicly available on their site). Understand the difference between the installer view and the homeowner view. Read up on how net metering works, what a solar inverter does and why monitoring uptime has direct commercial value for installers. Note any friction you observe as a product thinker.

Week 2: build your behavioral story bank. List five to seven projects where you drove a product decision involving hardware constraints, cross-functional conflict, data ambiguity or a regulated environment. Map each to the STAR format and practise out loud. Two to three minutes per answer is a good target.

Week 3: sharpen product design and metrics skills. Practise designing a feature specifically for the installer segment, SolarEdge's primary B2B customer. Work through how you would prioritize a firmware reliability fix versus a new dashboard feature. Define a clear North Star metric for inverter monitoring and be ready to defend it.

Before the interview. Check SolarEdge's recent product announcements so you can reference current context. Prepare three to four genuine questions per interviewer. Questions about how hardware and software PMs collaborate day-to-day, or how the company handles regulatory timelines in new markets, tend to land well according to candidate reports.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating it like a SaaS PM interview. The most common mistake candidates report is answering every question with a pure-software mental model: A/B tests, sprint velocity and engagement metrics. SolarEdge is a hardware company. Frame your answers around reliability, field constraints and installer workflows.

Not knowing the product portfolio. SolarEdge makes more than inverters. If you have not looked at their energy storage, EV charging and grid services products before the interview, you will be caught off guard by product design questions that reference those lines.

Ignoring the installer channel. SolarEdge does not sell directly to homeowners in most markets. Candidates who focus only on the end consumer miss a critical stakeholder. Interviewers probe whether you genuinely understand B2B2C dynamics and the installer's business model.

Vague behavioral answers. Saying 'I improved team alignment' without specifics is a red flag in any PM interview. Be concrete about what you did, what was difficult and what the outcome was, even if the result was mixed or partial.

Over-indexing on metrics without a point of view. Listing many metrics without explaining which one you would focus on and why signals discomfort with making calls. Always name a primary metric and defend why it is the right one for the specific problem.

Not asking questions. In a company with complex hardware-software dynamics, asking nothing at the end of a round signals low curiosity or poor preparation. Prepare genuine questions based on what you have read about SolarEdge's products, market strategy and team structure.

Frequently asked

How many PM roles does SolarEdge typically have open at one time?

As of mid-2026, knok jobradar tracks 144 open roles at SolarEdge across all functions. The PM-specific count is a subset of this total. Roles span India, Israel, the US and Europe, so filter by location when you apply. Openings can shift quickly, so checking regularly is worthwhile.

Do I need an engineering background to be a PM at SolarEdge?

Candidates report that SolarEdge does not strictly require an engineering degree, but a working understanding of hardware-software systems is a genuine advantage. If your background is in software product management, spend time before interviews learning how inverters work, what firmware release cycles look like and why field monitoring uptime matters commercially. Demonstrating curiosity about the technology tends to matter more than any specific degree.

How long does the SolarEdge PM hiring process usually take?

Candidates report the process typically spans three to five rounds. Rounds typically include a recruiter screen, a hiring manager conversation, one or two product or case rounds and a cross-functional panel. End-to-end timelines can vary by team and geography, so following up after each round is a reasonable approach.

Is solar industry knowledge required before the interview?

You are not expected to be a solar engineer, but interviewers do expect preparation. Know what a solar inverter does, why installers rely on monitoring software and how the residential and commercial segments differ in their priorities. Candidates who ask specific, informed questions at the end of each round consistently report a better interview experience than those who arrive with no domain background.

How is a SolarEdge PM role different from a typical software PM job?

SolarEdge PMs manage products with physical hardware components, which means firmware release cycles, certification requirements and field update risks are real constraints you have to plan around. The primary customer in most markets is a professional installer, not the end consumer. You will work across hardware R&D, firmware and cloud software teams at the same time, which requires a broader cross-functional range than most pure-software PM roles demand.
