02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk me through a cloud architecture you designed.

*Situation:* My team ran a monolithic e-commerce app on a single server. During sale events, the site would slow down badly and sometimes drop requests.

*Task:* I was asked to redesign the infrastructure on AWS to handle peak traffic without over-provisioning year-round.

*Action:* I split the app into three tiers using an Application Load Balancer, auto-scaling EC2 groups, and an RDS read replica. I set CloudWatch alarms to trigger scaling policies and moved static assets to S3 with CloudFront in front.

*Result:* The next sale event ran without a single downtime alert, and the team saw a noticeable drop in monthly compute costs compared to the old fixed-server setup.

---

Q: Describe a cloud incident you resolved.

*Situation:* A production Kubernetes cluster on GKE started dropping requests intermittently late one night while I was on call.

*Task:* I had to identify the root cause and restore service quickly.

*Action:* I checked pod logs first, then node metrics. One node had a memory leak in a sidecar container. I cordoned that node, drained workloads to healthy nodes, and patched the sidecar image.

*Result:* Service was fully restored within the same shift. I wrote a post-mortem and added memory limits to all sidecar containers to prevent a repeat.

---

Q: How have you used Terraform in production?

*Situation:* My previous project had infrastructure managed partly through the console and partly by old scripts. Nobody had a clear picture of current state, so changes were risky.

*Task:* I led the effort to bring all resources under Terraform and give the team a single source of truth.

*Action:* I wrote modules for VPC, compute, and database layers, imported existing resources, set up remote state in S3 with DynamoDB locking, and added Terraform plan to the CI pipeline so every PR showed an infrastructure diff.

*Result:* Accidental drift dropped to near zero, and new environments that used to take a full day could be created in under an hour.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the right structure for every experience question. Keep Situation and Task brief, spend most of your answer on Action (what you personally did), and always close with a concrete Result. Even a qualitative outcome ('the team saw far fewer incidents') is better than no result at all.

For technical design questions, use a top-down structure: start with requirements, name the key components, explain why you chose each over alternatives, and flag the trade-offs you considered. Interviewers value structured thinking as much as the final answer, because it signals you can explain your work clearly to client stakeholders who may not be deeply technical.

For 'what would you do if...' questions, switch to a present-tense walkthrough: state your approach, name the specific tools or services you would use, and describe the outcome you would aim for.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Artech places engineers directly with client teams, so interviewers are checking whether you can operate independently from day one. They typically look for:

  • Hands-on depth. Can you explain not just what a service does, but why you chose it over alternatives?
  • Incident experience. Have you actually debugged production problems, not just read about them?
  • Cost awareness. Cloud spend is a real concern for clients. Candidates who naturally factor in cost trade-offs tend to stand out.
  • Clear communication. You may work with client stakeholders who are not deeply technical, so explaining complex topics simply is a real skill here.
  • Security mindset. IAM, least-privilege access, secrets management, and network segmentation should come up in your answers naturally, not only when asked directly.
06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Start at least one week before your interview.

  1. Map each requirement in the job description to a specific project from your own experience. This makes STAR answers faster to recall under pressure.
  2. Practise at least five STAR answers out loud. Saying them aloud catches gaps that silent rehearsal misses.
  3. If you have not touched cloud infrastructure recently, spin up a basic VPC with a load balancer and auto-scaling group on AWS or Azure to refresh hands-on recall.
  4. Review Kubernetes fundamentals: pods, deployments, services, and resource limits.
  5. Be ready to explain how Terraform remote state and locking work, since this topic comes up frequently.
  6. Prepare two or three questions about the specific client project or team you would be joining. This signals genuine interest and helps you evaluate whether the role is the right fit.
07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

  1. Staying too high-level. Saying 'I used AWS' without naming specific services or explaining your choices is a common reason candidates do not move forward after a technical round.
  2. Skipping the Result. STAR answers without a concrete outcome feel incomplete. Always close with what actually happened.
  3. Treating security as a separate topic. Weave IAM and network security into every design answer rather than waiting to be asked specifically.
  4. Not asking about the client or project. Artech typically places you with a specific client, and asking nothing about that context can signal low interest in the role.
  5. Over-claiming certifications. Listing certifications you cannot back up with hands-on recall hurts credibility. Be ready to explain the details behind every line on your resume.
  6. Generic soft-skill answers. Phrases like 'I am a fast learner' without a supporting example waste time. Use specific stories from your own work instead.

Candidates report two to four rounds in total. The process typically starts with a recruiter screen, followed by one or two technical rounds, and a final discussion with a hiring manager or client team. The exact number can vary depending on which client project you are being considered for.

Does Artech test coding, or is the focus on cloud architecture?

For Cloud Engineer roles, candidates report that the focus is primarily on cloud architecture, infrastructure tooling (Terraform, Kubernetes, CI/CD), and past incident experience rather than competitive coding. Some rounds may include a short hands-on scenario or a design problem. Prepare for both, but weight your time toward architecture and operations questions.

Which cloud platform should I focus on for this role?

Job descriptions for Cloud Engineer roles at Artech typically mention AWS most often, with Azure appearing regularly as well. If you have depth on one platform, lead with that and be ready to explain how core concepts like VPCs, IAM, and load balancers map across platforms. Breadth matters less than depth here.
