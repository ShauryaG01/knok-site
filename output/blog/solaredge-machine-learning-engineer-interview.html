02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly in SolarEdge ML Engineer interviews, based on what candidates report:

  1. Time series anomaly detection. 'Walk me through how you would detect faults in inverter output data. What model family would you choose and why?'
  1. Energy yield forecasting. 'How would you build a next-day solar energy production forecast? How do you handle missing sensor readings?'
  1. Feature engineering for IoT data. 'What features would you extract from raw telemetry to build a predictive maintenance model?'
  1. Model drift and monitoring. 'How do you know when a deployed model is no longer performing well? What signals do you watch in production?'
  1. Imbalanced data. 'Fault events are rare in inverter data. How do you train a useful model when your positive class is a tiny fraction of your dataset?'
  1. ML system design. 'Design a pipeline that ingests real-time inverter telemetry and triggers a maintenance alert within minutes of detecting an anomaly.'
  1. Coding: sliding window. 'Write a function that computes a rolling mean and standard deviation over a stream of sensor readings.'
  1. Model complexity vs. latency. 'Your model is accurate but runs too slowly at the edge device. How do you approach that tradeoff?'
  1. Limited labelled data. 'You have large volumes of unlabelled telemetry but very few labelled fault events. How do you still build a useful model?'
  1. Experimentation in production. 'How would you validate that a new forecasting model is genuinely better than the current one before fully rolling it out?'
  1. Cross-functional communication. 'How have you explained model outputs to a hardware or field engineer with no ML background?'
  1. Domain curiosity. 'What do you find technically interesting about applying machine learning to renewable energy systems?'

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How would you detect anomalies in inverter telemetry data?

*Situation:* In a previous role I worked on IoT sensor data from industrial equipment where normal operation patterns shifted seasonally and varied across device types.

*Task:* I needed to flag abnormal readings in near real-time with a low false positive rate, because every false alert triggered a costly field visit.

*Action:* I built a baseline using an LSTM autoencoder trained on several months of 'healthy' operation data. Reconstruction error above a calibrated threshold triggered an alert. I also layered a hand-crafted rule to catch sharp voltage faults that the neural network sometimes missed. I cross-validated thresholds on a held-out period from a different season to avoid overfitting to one operational regime.

*Result:* False positives dropped compared to the previous rule-only system. More importantly, the team began catching a class of slow degradation faults that had been invisible before, which reduced unplanned downtime.

---

Q: Tell me about a time a model you deployed did not perform as expected in production.

*Situation:* I shipped a demand forecasting model that had looked strong in offline evaluation. Within two weeks of launch, forecast quality had noticeably degraded in a way that was not immediately obvious from our existing dashboards.

*Task:* I had to diagnose the root cause quickly and decide whether to roll back or patch the model.

*Action:* I added distribution-monitoring dashboards on input features and found that one upstream pipeline had silently changed its unit of measurement after a vendor update. I retrained on corrected data, added a unit-sanity check at ingestion, and set up an automated alert that fires when feature distributions shift beyond a defined bound.

*Result:* The patched model recovered to its original quality. The monitoring layer has since caught two more silent upstream changes before they affected model output.

---

Q: How have you explained model outputs to a non-technical stakeholder?

*Situation:* My team built a predictive maintenance model for a client whose field operations team had no data science background and was sceptical of automated recommendations.

*Task:* I needed to present model recommendations in a way the field team would trust and act on, not just review once and ignore.

*Action:* Instead of showing technical metrics like AUC, I reframed the output as: 'This device shows a high likelihood of failure within the next 7 days based on its recent output pattern.' I built a simple dashboard with a colour-coded risk score and a plain-language explanation for each flagged device. I also ran a shadow period where the model ran silently alongside manual inspections, then shared a side-by-side comparison showing how often the model would have flagged the same devices the engineers caught manually.

*Result:* The field team adopted the tool with minimal resistance. The shadow-period comparison was what built their trust, not any technical metric I presented to them.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

The STAR structure (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the safest default for behavioural questions. Keep Situation and Task brief (two or three sentences combined) and spend most of your time on the Action. SolarEdge interviewers reportedly probe the Action step with follow-up questions like 'why that approach over X alternative?' so think through your reasoning before the interview, not in the moment.

For technical design questions, use a think-aloud structure: restate the problem in your own words, state your assumptions, propose a simple baseline, then iterate toward something production-ready. Candidates report that SolarEdge values structured thinking over a polished answer that arrives too quickly and skips the reasoning.

For coding questions, narrate your reasoning as you write. 'I am using a deque here because I need O(1) access to the oldest element in the sliding window' is more valuable than silent, correct code. Ask about constraints (data volume, latency budget, streaming vs. batch) before you start writing a single line.

Domain framing matters. SolarEdge builds energy products. When answering a general ML question, ground it in energy data wherever you can. Something like 'In a solar forecasting context, this would mean...' signals that you have thought about the specific problem, not just the abstract technique. Candidates who do this consistently report more engaged interviewers.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates report, SolarEdge ML interviewers look for a few specific qualities:

Comfort with time series and signal data. Most of SolarEdge's ML problems involve sensor streams, not tabular or text data. You do not need prior solar experience, but you do need genuine hands-on experience with sequential data, sliding windows, stationarity, and seasonality.

Production mindset. SolarEdge ships software that runs on physical devices in remote locations. Interviewers want to see that you think about latency, data quality failures, model drift, and monitoring, not just offline accuracy on a clean benchmark.

Intellectual honesty. If you do not know something, candidates report that saying 'I have not worked with that specifically, but here is how I would approach it' lands better than bluffing. The interviewers are technical and will probe further.

Communication across disciplines. ML engineers at SolarEdge work alongside hardware engineers, field technicians, and product managers. The ability to explain model behaviour in plain terms is tested explicitly during interviews, not just assumed.

Genuine domain interest. Candidates who have read the basics of how solar inverters work, or who can articulate why forecasting renewable energy output is a genuinely hard ML problem, consistently report more positive interview experiences.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Foundations and domain context

Review time series fundamentals: autocorrelation, stationarity tests, ARIMA, and sequence models (LSTM, Transformer variants). Spend a few hours reading how solar power systems work: how irradiance translates to output, what causes inverter faults, and what predictive maintenance looks like in industrial IoT settings. Public engineering blogs from energy tech companies are a good starting point.

Week 2: Coding and system design

Practise sliding window problems, stream processing patterns, and anomaly detection implementations in Python. Build a small project on a public energy dataset. Kaggle has solar energy datasets worth exploring. Implement a forecasting or anomaly detection model, evaluate it properly with held-out data, and document your choices clearly. This gives you a concrete, specific project to reference during interviews.

Week 3: Behavioural rounds and mock practice

Prepare three to four STAR stories covering: a model you built end to end, a production failure or unexpected result, a time you worked with non-technical stakeholders, and a project where you had limited labelled data. Say these out loud rather than just writing them down. Timing yourself helps keep answers focused and prevents rambling.

On the day: Candidates report SolarEdge interviews are conversational in tone. Ask clarifying questions before diving into solutions. Showing your reasoning matters more than arriving at a perfect answer quickly.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating it like a pure algorithms interview. SolarEdge is not primarily testing your ability to solve abstract competitive programming puzzles. Candidates who over-prepare for that format and under-prepare for ML system design and domain questions report being caught off guard.

Generic ML answers with no domain grounding. Saying 'I would use XGBoost' without connecting it to the specific characteristics of sensor telemetry misses the mark. Ground your answers in the energy or IoT context wherever you can.

Skipping the 'why this model' explanation. SolarEdge interviewers reportedly follow up on model choice. If you pick an LSTM, be ready to explain why not a simpler statistical model, and vice versa. Have a real reason, not just a habit.

Not asking about constraints first. In system design rounds, jumping straight to a complex architecture without first clarifying latency requirements, data volume, and deployment environment signals poor engineering judgement to interviewers.

Overclaiming results without caveats. Candidates report that intellectual honesty lands well at SolarEdge. Saying 'precision improved on the internal test set, but we only validated on one product line' is stronger than inflating a result. Interviewers know real projects have limitations.

Not preparing questions to ask. Candidates who have no questions about the team's current technical challenges or ML infrastructure leave a weaker impression. Prepare two or three genuine questions in advance.

How many interview rounds does SolarEdge typically have for ML Engineer roles?

Candidates report a process that typically involves three to five rounds. These commonly include an initial recruiter or HR screen, one or two technical rounds covering ML concepts and coding, a system design round, and a final round with a hiring manager or cross-functional panel. The exact structure varies by team and role level, so confirm the format with your recruiter after you apply. SolarEdge has not publicly standardised round names or sequences.

Do I need experience in solar energy or renewables to apply?

Direct solar experience is not a stated requirement in most job descriptions, and candidates without it do get hired. What matters more is comfort with time series data, IoT sensor streams, and production ML systems. That said, candidates who understand how solar power generation works and can articulate the ML challenges in energy forecasting report stronger interview outcomes. A few hours of domain reading before your first round is a worthwhile investment.
