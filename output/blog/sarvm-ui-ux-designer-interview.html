02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates report these themes coming up most often in Sarvm UI/UX Designer interviews. Expect a mix of portfolio walkthroughs, design thinking exercises, and cross-team collaboration questions.

  1. Walk us through a project from your portfolio where you solved a real user problem. What was your process from research to final delivery?
  2. How do you approach user research when time and budget are limited?
  3. Describe a situation where you disagreed with a product manager or developer about a design decision. What happened and how did you resolve it?
  4. How do you balance visual appeal with usability when the two pull in different directions?
  5. Tell us about a design you shipped that you later felt was not quite right. What would you change today?
  6. How do you design for users with low digital literacy or those picking up a smartphone for the first time?
  7. Walk us through how you create or contribute to a design system. How do you keep it consistent across a growing product?
  8. After a feature goes live, how do you know whether the design is actually working?
  9. How would you approach redesigning a confusing multi-step form to reduce user drop-off?
  10. A stakeholder gives vague feedback like 'make it pop' or 'it needs to look more modern.' How do you handle that conversation?
  11. Describe a time you had to simplify a very complex workflow into something that felt intuitive to a non-technical user.
  12. How do you hand off designs to developers and make sure the final product matches your original intent?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Three full STAR-format answers to help you structure your own responses.

---

Q: Walk us through a project where you solved a real user problem.

*Situation:* I was working on a mobile app for a logistics company where field agents had to log delivery updates. The existing confirmation flow required many taps to complete, and agents were frequently skipping steps or logging information incorrectly.

*Task:* My goal was to redesign the delivery confirmation flow to reduce errors and speed up the process, without requiring agents to be retrained from scratch.

*Action:* I started by shadowing two field agents on their routes to watch how they actually used the app. I found they were almost always working one-handed under time pressure. I redesigned the flow to a single confirmation screen with large tap targets and auto-populated the GPS location. I ran two rounds of prototype testing with agents before handing off to the development team.

*Result:* After launch, the team tracked a meaningful drop in logging errors. Agents reported the flow felt much faster, and the product manager noted fewer support escalations from field supervisors in the following month.

---

Q: Describe a time you disagreed with a stakeholder about a design decision.

*Situation:* During a fintech project, a senior manager wanted to add a large promotional banner to the app home screen. My usability research had shown that users were already struggling to find key account actions on that screen.

*Task:* I needed to push back on the request while keeping the relationship intact and not delaying the product timeline.

*Action:* Rather than simply saying no, I put together a short presentation showing usability test clips where users missed the primary actions. I proposed an alternative: a dismissible nudge card that appeared only once per session, with the promotional message included more subtly. I walked the manager through the user journey and explained the risk of adding more visual weight to an already busy screen.

*Result:* The manager agreed to the nudge card approach. The feature shipped on time and, based on analytics reviewed at our retrospective, the primary action completion rate held steady after launch.

---

Q: Describe a time you simplified a very complex workflow.

*Situation:* I joined a project where an HR software product had a lengthy onboarding wizard. The product team's funnel data showed that new users were abandoning the wizard roughly halfway through on average.

*Task:* I was asked to reduce abandonment so users could actually reach and start using the core product.

*Action:* I audited every step and categorized each one as 'must-have now,' 'can defer,' or 'optional.' I ran a card-sorting session with several HR managers to understand what they genuinely needed on day one versus later. I condensed the mandatory steps significantly and moved optional settings into a 'finish your setup' checklist inside the app. I also redesigned the progress indicator so users could see they were nearly done.

*Result:* In user testing sessions, participants completed the redesigned flow in roughly half the time. The product team reported a clear improvement in onboarding completion in the weeks following launch.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

The STAR method works well for behavioral questions. Lead with the Situation (one or two sentences of context), the Task (what you specifically needed to accomplish), the Action (the bulk of your answer, where you show your thinking and decisions), and the Result (a concrete outcome, even a qualitative one). Keep Situation and Task brief so Action gets the most airtime.

For portfolio walkthroughs, use a 'problem, process, decision, outcome' structure. Interviewers at product-focused companies typically want to hear about the decisions you made and why, not just the final screens. Be prepared to explain what you chose NOT to do and what trade-offs you accepted.

For design challenges or live exercises, narrate your thinking out loud. Start by asking clarifying questions: Who is the user? What does success look like? What constraints exist? This demonstrates structured thinking, which interviewers value as much as the final output.

For stakeholder conflict questions, the 'data-led pushback' frame works well. Show that you respected the stakeholder's goal, gathered evidence such as user research or usability testing, and proposed an alternative that served the business goal without harming the user experience.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates typically report from product-company interviews, Sarvm interviewers are looking for several qualities in particular.

Strong problem framing. Can you clearly articulate who the user is, what they are trying to do, and why the current experience falls short? Candidates who jump straight to solutions without setting up the user problem tend to score lower.

Evidence of research and iteration. Did you test your assumptions? Interviewers want to see that your designs evolved based on feedback, not that you got it right on the first try. Talk about what did not work and what you learned from it.

Clear communication with non-designers. UI/UX Designers work closely with engineers, product managers, and sometimes business stakeholders. Interviewers look for candidates who can explain design decisions in plain language without leaning on design jargon.

Ownership and follow-through. Candidates who tracked what happened after launch, cared about implementation quality, and pushed back when the built product drifted from the design intent tend to stand out.

Curiosity and collaboration. Smaller and mid-size product teams often weight cultural fit heavily. Ask good questions, show genuine interest in the product, and demonstrate that you are easy to work with across functions.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

One week before

Audit your portfolio and select two or three projects that each tell a different story: one where you conducted meaningful user research, one where you navigated a tough stakeholder situation, and one where you simplified something complex. For each project, prepare a five-minute walkthrough and a clear list of key decisions you made and why you made them.

Research Sarvm. Look at their product if it is public-facing, read any recent news, and go through the job description carefully. Note any specific tools, user segments, or product areas they highlight.

Three to four days before

Practice STAR answers out loud for the behavioral questions listed in this guide. Recording yourself and watching it back is uncomfortable but effective. Aim for answers that run two to three minutes.

Prepare questions to ask the interviewer. Good options: 'How does the design team collaborate with product and engineering here?' and 'What does a strong first few months in this role typically look like?'

Day before

Review your portfolio and confirm all links and prototypes are working. Prepare any case study documents or PDF decks you want to reference during the conversation.

Confirm the interview format with the recruiter. Candidates typically report that Sarvm interviews are conducted over video call. Test your audio, video, and screen-share setup in advance.

Day of

Join the call a few minutes early. Keep your portfolio open in one tab and your case study notes nearby. During any design exercise, think out loud. Asking clarifying questions and narrating your reasoning is the point, not arriving at a perfect answer instantly.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Describing outputs instead of decisions. Saying 'I designed the onboarding screen' tells the interviewer nothing. Say instead: 'I chose progressive disclosure for onboarding because testing showed users were overwhelmed when they saw all the fields at once.'

Skipping the user context. A design answer that starts with 'I made it cleaner' without explaining who the user is and what they were struggling with will not land. Ground every answer in a real user problem.

Not knowing your own portfolio. Interviewers will probe specific decisions and trade-offs in your work. If you cannot explain why you chose a particular pattern or what alternatives you considered, it raises doubts about whether the work was fully yours.

Overly passive language. Phrases like 'we kind of explored' or 'the team decided to' make your individual contribution invisible. Use 'I' when describing what you specifically did, and 'we' for decisions made collectively.

Leaving out the result. Every STAR answer needs a result. If you do not know the exact metric, a qualitative observation works fine: 'Users told us in follow-up sessions that the new flow felt much faster' is a valid result.

Rushing design exercises. Candidates who dive into solutions without asking any clarifying questions tend to score lower than those who take a moment to frame the problem first. Ask about the user, the goal, and the constraints before you start sketching.
