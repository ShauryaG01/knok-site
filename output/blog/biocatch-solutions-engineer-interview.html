01 Overview

Overview

BioCatch is a global behavioral biometrics company whose platform analyzes how users physically interact with their devices, including typing rhythm, mouse movement, and swipe patterns, to detect account takeover fraud, new account fraud, and social engineering attacks in real time. A Solutions Engineer at BioCatch sits at the crossroads of pre-sales consulting and technical delivery. You will demo the platform to banks and financial institutions, run proof-of-concept deployments, answer deep integration questions, and help clients build a business case for adoption.

The interview process typically spans three to five rounds. Candidates report starting with an HR or recruiter screen covering background and motivation, followed by a technical round on cybersecurity fundamentals and BioCatch's platform, a customer scenario or role-play exercise, and a final conversation with a senior leader. BioCatch currently has 24 open roles in India, and the Solutions Engineer position is among the more competitive ones given the specialized mix of fraud domain knowledge and client-facing skills required.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These are the questions candidates most commonly report from BioCatch Solutions Engineer interviews. Expect a mix of domain knowledge, client scenario, and behavioral questions.

  1. How would you explain behavioral biometrics to a bank's CISO who has never encountered the term before?
  2. Walk us through how you would design and run a proof-of-concept for a tier-1 Indian bank evaluating BioCatch.
  3. A client's mobile banking team says the BioCatch SDK is adding noticeable latency to their app. How do you investigate and respond?
  4. How do you handle a prospect whose security or legal team raises data privacy concerns about sending behavioral data to a third-party vendor?
  5. Describe a time you turned a complex technical concept into a business case for a non-technical decision-maker.
  6. How would you position BioCatch alongside fraud tools a bank already uses, such as device fingerprinting or SMS OTP?
  7. What do you understand about account takeover fraud, and how does behavioral biometrics address it compared to traditional controls?
  8. A client says your API documentation is unclear and their integration is stalled. What do you do?
  9. How do you prioritize when you are managing an active POC for one client and fielding RFI responses for three prospects at the same time?
  10. What key metrics would you propose to show a bank that BioCatch is delivering measurable ROI after six months of deployment?
  11. Describe your hands-on experience with REST APIs. How would you help a client's engineering team debug a failing webhook integration?
  12. How do you stay current with trends in financial fraud, behavioral analytics, and regulatory changes like RBI guidelines on digital fraud?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How would you explain behavioral biometrics to a non-technical CISO?

*Situation:* In my previous role at a fintech, we were pitching a fraud detection upgrade to the CISO of a mid-sized private bank. She had a risk background, not an engineering one, and had already sat through too many vendor pitches full of jargon.

*Task:* I needed to convey what behavioral biometrics is, why it differs from what they already had, and why it mattered, all in under five minutes without losing her.

*Action:* I opened with an analogy she already owned: 'Your bank can verify that someone knows your password. But it cannot verify that it is actually you typing it. Behavioral biometrics watches the how, not just the what. It learns your natural rhythm and flags anyone who moves differently.' I then showed a one-slide comparison of a genuine user versus a fraudster using a remote access tool, with detection timestamps side by side.

*Result:* She requested a proof-of-concept on their UAT environment within two weeks of that meeting. The concept landed because it was grounded in a risk she already owned, not a technology she had to decode first.

---

Q: How do you manage competing priorities during a live POC?

*Situation:* I was leading a POC for a large cooperative bank while simultaneously handling RFI responses for two new prospects, all within the same two-week window.

*Task:* The POC client needed daily support and data analysis. The prospects had submission deadlines I could not miss. I had no additional headcount.

*Action:* I blocked my mornings exclusively for the POC, since that client's integration team worked India hours and needed real-time answers. I batched the RFI work into late afternoons, templatized the sections that repeated across both responses, and flagged one low-priority section to my manager to co-author so I could focus on the differentiating content.

*Result:* The POC moved to commercial negotiation. Both RFIs were submitted on time. My manager later used my templatized structure as a starting point for the team's shared RFI library.

---

Q: How would you build an ROI story for a bank client after six months?

*Situation:* A regional bank had gone live with a fraud detection platform six months earlier. The fraud team valued the alerts, but the CFO was questioning the renewal budget and wanted concrete numbers.

*Task:* I needed to build a credible ROI story using data the client already owned, without making claims the data could not support.

*Action:* I pulled fraud loss data from before and after go-live from their team, mapped it against detection events, and calculated fraud prevented as a monetary value. I also quantified the reduction in manual review cases, translating analyst hours saved into a cost figure. I presented this in a two-page executive summary with the CFO's preferred metric front and center.

*Result:* The renewal was approved, and the bank expanded the deployment to two additional product lines in the following quarter.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For 'explain complex technology' questions: Start with the business problem the technology solves, not the technology itself. Use one analogy the listener already owns, then show one concrete example. Only introduce technical depth after you have their interest. BioCatch questions are full of these, so practice explaining behavioral biometrics using bank-familiar risks like account takeover fraud, mule accounts, and social engineering before your interview.

For 'handling objections' questions: Follow a three-step pattern. First, validate the concern genuinely ('That is a fair concern and we hear it often'). Second, provide data or a reference customer example. Third, offer a specific next step that lowers their risk, such as a scoped data privacy review or a limited-data POC. Avoid getting defensive or pivoting too quickly to product features.

For 'prioritization' questions: Name your framework explicitly. Candidates who say 'I triage by impact and urgency' and then show how they applied it in a real situation score higher than those who list generic productivity tips. For a Solutions Engineer role, interviewers want to see that you protect live client commitments first, since a stalled POC can directly kill a deal.

For 'metrics and ROI' questions: Always anchor to the client's own data. Interviewers are wary of candidates who cite industry benchmarks without connecting them to what the specific client can actually measure. Propose two to three metrics: a fraud outcome metric (losses prevented, false positive rate reduction), an operational metric (analyst hours saved), and a customer experience metric (friction reduction for legitimate users).

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

BioCatch interviewers typically look for a combination that is harder to find than it sounds.

Domain credibility: You do not need prior behavioral biometrics experience, but you should understand account takeover fraud, the limitations of OTP and device fingerprinting, and why banks struggle to balance fraud prevention with customer friction. Candidates who have worked in fintech, banking technology, or cybersecurity pre-sales start with a clear advantage.

Technical depth without arrogance: Solutions Engineers talk to both CISOs and junior developers. Interviewers test whether you can shift register fluidly. They are not impressed by jargon. They want to see that you can explain an SDK integration clearly to a developer and then walk the same topic back up to a strategic risk conversation with a head of fraud.

Client empathy: The role involves heavy POC management, which is inherently ambiguous and often involves client teams under deadline pressure. Interviewers watch for candidates who treat client frustration as useful information rather than as an obstacle.

Commercial awareness: This is a pre-sales-adjacent role. Candidates who can articulate how a technical decision affects deal velocity or renewal probability stand out from those who think only about the engineering side.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating it like a generic tech interview: Solutions Engineer interviews at BioCatch are not algorithm or data structure tests. Candidates who arrive without knowing what behavioral biometrics is or how it applies to bank fraud typically do not advance past the technical round.

Over-explaining the technology: A common pattern is candidates who love the science behind behavioral data and go very deep on how the models work, losing the interviewer in the process. The goal is to show that you match depth to audience, not that you can give a detailed technical seminar to anyone in the room.

Skipping the result: Candidates often describe a strong situation and action but forget to name the outcome. In a pre-sales-adjacent role, results are the whole point. If you saved the deal, say so. If the POC converted to a purchase, say so. Numbers from your own work are always valid to include.

Being vague about client pushback: Many candidates say 'I handled the objection professionally.' Interviewers want to know exactly what the objection was, what you said in response, and what happened next. Specificity separates a good story from a great one.

Ignoring the Indian regulatory context: BioCatch works with Indian banks operating under RBI oversight. Candidates who can reference local compliance context, even briefly, signal that they understand the real sales environment rather than just the product in the abstract.

Frequently asked

How many interview rounds does BioCatch typically have for a Solutions Engineer role?

Candidates report three to five rounds in total, though the exact structure can vary by hiring team. A typical sequence includes an HR screen, a technical domain round, a customer scenario or role-play exercise, and a final leadership conversation. Some candidates report an additional panel round with cross-functional stakeholders from product or customer success.

Do I need prior behavioral biometrics experience to apply?

Not necessarily, but adjacent domain knowledge is important. BioCatch typically looks for candidates who understand fraud patterns in digital banking, how traditional controls like OTP and device fingerprinting work, and why those controls fall short against certain attack types. Candidates who cleared the technical round without a behavioral biometrics background commonly had fintech, cybersecurity pre-sales, or banking technology experience instead.

Is there a coding test or take-home assignment in the process?

Candidates rarely report a traditional coding test for this role. The technical round is more commonly a discussion of integration concepts, API familiarity, and how you would guide a client's engineering team through an SDK deployment. That said, expect detailed questions about REST APIs, webhooks, and how you would debug a real integration problem from first principles.
