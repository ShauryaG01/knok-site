01 Overview

Overview

Intangles is a Pune-based vehicle intelligence company that builds connected fleet platforms for OEMs, fleet operators, and automotive service networks. Their products handle real-time telematics, predictive diagnostics, and driver behaviour analytics, so Product Designers here work on data-heavy dashboards, mobile apps for drivers and fleet managers, and complex information architectures.

As of July 2026, knok jobradar shows Intangles has 26 open roles across functions, and the broader Product Designer market in India lists 393 openings. The interview process typically runs two to four rounds: an initial HR screen, a design portfolio review, one or two design exercise or case-study rounds, and a final culture or leadership chat. Candidates report that Intangles interviewers lean heavily on how you handle ambiguous, data-rich problems rather than pixel-perfect visual craft.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates who have interviewed at Intangles report questions that test your ability to design for non-consumer, data-heavy enterprise contexts. Expect these:

  1. Walk us through a project where you had to visualise complex data for a non-technical user.
  2. How do you decide what information to show on a fleet dashboard when the data set is very large?
  3. Describe your process when a product manager's requirement and engineering constraints pull in opposite directions.
  4. How have you designed for users who are on the move, often on low-end Android devices in poor network conditions?
  5. Tell us about a time you had to redesign a feature after user research showed the original design was wrong.
  6. How do you approach designing alerts and notifications that must not fatigue the user but still communicate urgency?
  7. Give an example of how you used analytics or usage data to make a design decision.
  8. How do you handle a stakeholder who pushes back on a design recommendation you strongly believe in?
  9. Walk us through how you would design the onboarding flow for a fleet manager who has never used a telematics product before.
  10. How do you collaborate with engineers during the build phase to make sure the shipped product matches your intent?
  11. What design system or component library practices have you followed, and how did you contribute to or maintain one?
  12. How do you balance speed of delivery with design quality when deadlines are tight?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through a project where you had to visualise complex data for a non-technical user.

*Situation:* I was the sole designer on a logistics startup's internal ops tool. The operations team, mostly field supervisors with no data background, needed to track delivery performance across hundreds of routes daily.

*Task:* My job was to turn a raw database export (with many columns, updated every hour) into something a supervisor could scan in under two minutes at the start of each shift.

*Action:* I started by shadowing three supervisors for a week to understand what decisions they actually made each morning. I mapped those decisions to just five key metrics, cut everything else to a drill-down layer, and used colour coding and iconography that matched the traffic-light mental model they already knew. I ran two rounds of usability testing with paper prototypes before touching Figma.

*Result:* Supervisors reported (in a follow-up session two months after launch) that their morning handover time dropped noticeably. The PM cited this in the company all-hands as a measurable win for the design team.

---

Q: Describe your process when PM requirements and engineering constraints pull in opposite directions.

*Situation:* On a mobile app for a B2B SaaS product, the PM wanted a real-time location map with live vehicle trails for the driver app. Engineering said the battery and data cost on Android Go devices made continuous GPS streaming impractical.

*Task:* I needed to find a solution that satisfied the PM's core user need without overloading the device or the backend.

*Action:* I facilitated a three-way session where I first got the PM to articulate the underlying user need (drivers and dispatchers needed to confirm a vehicle was on the right route, not see its exact position every second). Then I proposed a 'last known location plus route deviation alert' pattern. I sketched five concepts, ran them past two power users over a quick call, and presented the preferred option with annotated trade-offs to both the PM and the engineering lead.

*Result:* The compromise shipped in the next sprint. Battery consumption came in well within the target as reported by engineering in a post-release review. The PM was satisfied because the core dispatcher need was met, and the team avoided a costly rewrite later.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you used analytics or usage data to make a design decision.

*Situation:* A fleet management web app I worked on had a 'Reports' section that the product team believed was heavily used. It had received significant engineering investment.

*Task:* I was asked to redesign the navigation, and I wanted to make sure I was not hiding a popular feature.

*Action:* I pulled six months of Mixpanel data and found that only a small fraction of active users opened the Reports section in any given month. Of those, usage heatmaps showed most only ever used one of the seven available report types. I also ran five user interviews where participants consistently said they did not trust the reports because the data felt stale. Rather than redesigning the nav, I recommended a deeper investigation into data freshness, which turned out to be the real problem.

*Result:* The team prioritised fixing the data pipeline over a nav overhaul. Three months later, report usage climbed to the levels the team had assumed it was already at. It was a good reminder that analytics without context can mislead design decisions.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use these frameworks to structure your answers when you are not sure how to start:

For portfolio walkthroughs: Open with the business problem and who the user was. Then cover your research method, your key insight, the design decision that insight drove, and what you measured after launch. Keep it under four minutes unless asked to go deeper.

For process questions: Use a simple 'Discover, Define, Design, Deliver' spine. Briefly name which activities you ran at each stage and why, then anchor the story to a real project rather than speaking in abstractions.

For conflict or collaboration questions: Name the stakeholder, state the tension honestly, explain how you reframed the conversation around user needs (not personal preference), and share the outcome. Interviewers at product companies like Intangles want to see that you can hold your ground with data while staying collaborative.

For 'how would you design X' prompts: Start by asking two or three clarifying questions out loud (who is the primary user, what device, what does success look like). Then talk through constraints before jumping to solutions. Sketch on paper or a whiteboard if materials are available. This shows structured thinking, which Intangles interviewers specifically value for enterprise dashboard problems.

For data or metrics questions: Be concrete. Name the tool (Mixpanel, Hotjar, Google Analytics), name the metric, and explain why that metric was the right one to watch for the decision at hand.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Intangles builds products for fleet operators, automotive OEMs, and service networks, so their interviewers typically look for a specific profile:

Comfort with complexity. Their core products are data-dense. Interviewers want to see that you can tame complexity through hierarchy and progressive disclosure, not by making things look pretty.

Enterprise and B2B sensibility. Consumer app portfolios are fine as a starting point, but candidates who can show at least one B2B, SaaS, or internal-tools project tend to progress further in the process.

Research-driven decisions. Portfolio reviews at Intangles tend to focus more on 'why did you make that call' than 'what does it look like.' Come ready to explain the research or data behind each major decision.

Cross-functional collaboration. Designers at Intangles work closely with product managers, engineers, and domain experts (vehicle telematics specialists). Candidates who show a history of tight PM and engineering collaboration stand out.

Ownership and pragmatism. Startups move fast and resources are finite. Interviewers want to see that you can ship a good-enough solution quickly, gather feedback, and iterate, rather than holding out for perfection.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know the product and the domain.
Explore any publicly available Intangles demo or product pages. Read their website, case studies, and any press coverage. Understand what telematics means: GPS tracking, vehicle health monitoring, driver behaviour scoring. Watch a few YouTube videos on fleet management software to understand how fleet managers think.

Week 1-2: Prepare your portfolio.
Select two or three projects that best demonstrate data visualisation, enterprise UX, or mobile design for low-end devices. For each project, prepare a crisp four-minute story using this structure: problem, user, research insight, design decision, result. If you do not have a B2B project, take a consumer project and explain the enterprise parallels.

Week 2: Practice STAR answers.
Write out answers to all 12 questions listed above. Record yourself answering three of them. Play them back and check: did you lead with the situation quickly, or did you ramble? Did you quantify the result?

Week 2-3: Design exercise prep.
Candidates report that Intangles sometimes gives a take-home or in-session design prompt. Practice designing a fleet dashboard from scratch in a focused timed session. Focus on: what data is most critical, how to handle alert states, how to design for a user who checks the screen briefly rather than stares at it.

Before the interview:
Prepare three questions to ask the interviewer. Good ones include: 'What does the feedback loop between design and engineering look like on your team?' and 'What is the biggest design challenge the team is working on right now?' These show genuine curiosity about the role.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Bringing only consumer-app portfolio work. Intangles is not building the next Instagram. If every project you show is a social or e-commerce app, interviewers may question your fit for enterprise complexity. Contextualise even consumer projects in terms of data handling, edge cases, and user goals.

Talking about aesthetics over decisions. Saying 'I chose this colour palette because it feels calm' will not land well. Every design choice should trace back to a user need, a research insight, or a technical constraint.

Skipping the clarifying questions. When given an open-ended prompt like 'design a driver app,' jumping straight to solutions without asking about the user, device, or context reads as shallow. Ask first, then design.

Overpromising on timelines in your answers. If you say a project took two weeks, be ready to defend that. Interviewers probe timelines to understand how you manage scope.

Not knowing your own numbers. If you say a redesign improved task completion, know the before and after figures. If you do not have them, say 'we did not measure it formally, which is something I would do differently now.' Honesty beats a vague claim.

Ignoring the mobile context. Intangles serves users in the field, often on Android devices in patchy network areas. If your designs assume high-end hardware and fast internet, flag that you understand this is a constraint and explain how you would address it.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Intangles Product Designer interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically runs two to four rounds. This usually includes an HR or recruiter screening call, a portfolio review with the design lead or a PM, one design exercise (take-home or in-session), and a final round with a senior stakeholder or team lead. The exact sequence can vary by team, so it is worth asking the recruiter at the start of the process what to expect.

Does Intangles give a design assignment, and how should I prepare for it?

Candidates report that Intangles sometimes includes a take-home or in-person design exercise, often involving a dashboard, a fleet-management scenario, or a mobile app flow. The focus is typically on your process and reasoning, not visual polish. Practice talking through your assumptions, constraints, and trade-offs out loud, and do not skip the step of defining the user and their goal before you start designing.

What salary can I expect as a Product Designer at Intangles?

Based on knok jobradar data, Product Designer salaries in India currently range from 6-12 LPA at entry level (0-2 years) up to 36-55+ LPA for Lead and Principal roles. Intangles packages typically align with these bands. Your final offer will depend on your portfolio, years of relevant experience, and the specific team's budget, so it is worth checking comparable offers on Glassdoor or levels.fyi for the Pune market.

Is prior experience in automotive or fleet tech required?

No, candidates report that domain experience is a bonus but not a hard requirement. What matters more is your ability to design for complex, data-rich enterprise products and your willingness to learn the telematics domain quickly. Coming into the interview with a basic understanding of fleet management concepts (GPS tracking, vehicle health, driver scoring) will help you ask better questions and show genuine interest.

How important is having a Figma-based portfolio versus other tools?

Figma is the standard at most Indian product companies and candidates report Intangles teams use it as well. Presenting your portfolio in Figma or as a clean PDF or slide deck is fine. What matters far more than the tool is how clearly you can walk through your decisions. If you use Sketch or another tool, just make sure your files are shareable and your process is well documented.
