02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates who have gone through Vega's ML Engineer interview commonly report questions across these themes:

  1. Walk me through a machine learning project you owned end-to-end, from data ingestion to deployment.
  2. How do you handle class imbalance in a training dataset? What are the trade-offs between oversampling, undersampling, and cost-sensitive learning?
  3. Explain the bias-variance trade-off. How have you managed it in a real project?
  4. How would you design a real-time inference pipeline that needs to serve predictions under low-latency constraints?
  5. Describe your approach to feature engineering for tabular or time-series data.
  6. Once a model is live, how do you monitor it? What signals tell you it is degrading?
  7. Walk us through how gradient boosting works. When would you choose it over a deep learning model?
  8. A model performs well on your validation set but poorly in production. How do you diagnose and fix this?
  9. How would you build a fraud detection or anomaly detection system from scratch?
  10. What MLOps tools have you used? How do you manage experiment tracking, versioning, and reproducibility in your team?
  11. How do you ensure a model is fair and does not encode unwanted bias from the training data?
  12. Design a recommendation or ranking system for a product with a very large user base.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk me through a machine learning project you owned end-to-end.

*Situation:* My team had a customer churn prediction model that had been in production for two years. Its accuracy had degraded significantly because user behaviour patterns had shifted over that period.

*Task:* I was asked to rebuild it from scratch, improve its reliability, and make it production-ready so the growth team could act on its outputs every week.

*Action:* I started by auditing the existing data pipeline and found three feature columns that were leaking future information, which had artificially inflated the original model's reported accuracy. I fixed the data leakage, re-engineered features around usage frequency and recency, and trained an XGBoost classifier with proper time-based cross-validation splits. I containerised the model with Docker, scheduled batch inference via an Airflow DAG, and built a Metabase dashboard so the business team could view outputs without engineering support.

*Result:* The model's precision at the top decile improved meaningfully over the previous version, and the growth team began using the weekly output in their retention campaigns. The pipeline ran without manual intervention for more than six months.

---

Q: A model performs well in validation but poorly in production. How do you diagnose this?

*Situation:* At a previous role, a click-through-rate prediction model showed significantly worse performance two weeks after deployment compared to its offline evaluation metrics.

*Task:* I needed to identify the root cause quickly because the model was directly influencing ad spend allocation.

*Action:* I first compared feature distributions between the training data and live production traffic. I found that one high-importance feature had a schema change in the upstream data source, causing it to be populated with nulls at inference time. I confirmed this was the primary driver by checking feature importance rankings and simulating the null-filled column in a staging environment.

*Result:* I patched the data pipeline within a day and retrained on corrected data. I also introduced automated distribution checks so any future schema-level drift would alert the team before it affected business outcomes.

---

Q: How would you build a fraud detection system from scratch?

*Situation:* At a fintech startup I interned at, the risk team relied on a purely rule-based system that was generating too many false positives and frustrating legitimate users.

*Task:* My project was to design and prototype an ML-based fraud scoring model that could complement the existing rules.

*Action:* I framed this as a highly imbalanced binary classification problem. I used SMOTE combined with cost-sensitive learning to address the class imbalance, engineered velocity features such as transaction count per hour and spend deviation from a rolling average, and trained a LightGBM model. I evaluated using precision-recall curves rather than accuracy, since missing fraud carried a far higher cost than a false flag.

*Result:* The prototype achieved a recall score the business team considered strong at a false positive rate they found operationally acceptable. I presented a deployment roadmap for the senior engineers to take forward into production.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For 'how does X work' questions, use a three-part structure: define the concept in one sentence, explain the mechanics in plain terms, then give a concrete example from your own work. This shows depth and applied thinking without becoming a lecture.

For system design questions, candidates report that Vega interviewers appreciate a structured walkthrough. Start with clarifying questions covering data volume, latency requirements, and update frequency. Then walk through the data pipeline, model training approach, serving architecture, and monitoring strategy in sequence. Jumping straight to model selection before understanding the constraints is a pattern interviewers flag negatively.

For behavioural questions, use the STAR structure: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task brief. Spend most of your time on the Action. Always close with a concrete result. If you cannot cite a specific number, describe an observable outcome: 'the team shipped the feature two sprints ahead of the original estimate.'

For trade-off questions (for example, 'XGBoost versus a neural network'), frame your answer as: 'It depends on the constraints. When the dataset is tabular and interpretability matters, I lean toward gradient boosting because... When I have large volumes of unstructured data and the compute budget to match, deep learning becomes more compelling because...' This signals mature engineering judgement rather than a favourite tool.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Candidates report that Vega ML interviewers are primarily looking for engineers who have shipped models into production, not just built them in notebooks. Knowing the algorithms is a baseline. What differentiates strong candidates is comfort with the entire lifecycle: data quality, feature pipelines, model versioning, serving infrastructure, and monitoring for drift or degradation.

Vega interviewers typically probe for four qualities:

Production mindset. Can you describe how a model you built actually runs in a live system? Do you understand latency, throughput, and failure modes beyond offline accuracy metrics?

Debugging instinct. When something breaks, can you reason systematically about where to look? Interviewers often present an 'underperforming model' scenario specifically to test this quality.

Communication with non-technical stakeholders. ML Engineers at Vega are expected to work closely with product and business teams. Explaining a model's outputs and limitations in plain language is a valued skill, not an optional extra.

Intellectual honesty. Candidates who acknowledge the limits of their knowledge and say 'I would look this up or run a test' score better than those who bluff. A confident wrong answer damages trust for the rest of the conversation.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: ML fundamentals and coding

Revise the core concepts Vega typically tests: supervised and unsupervised learning, regularisation, gradient boosting, neural network basics, and evaluation metrics such as precision, recall, AUC-ROC, and F1. Practise coding ML pipelines from scratch in Python using scikit-learn. Solve 2-3 data manipulation problems daily using pandas and SQL.

Week 2: System design and MLOps

Study how to design ML systems at scale. Cover feature stores, model registries, batch versus online inference, A/B testing for models, and monitoring approaches for data drift and model degradation. Practise talking through a complete design out loud, since articulation is half of what gets evaluated.

Week 3: Behavioural prep and mock interviews

Write out 5-6 STAR stories from your own experience covering projects you owned, failures you recovered from, and situations where you worked across teams. Practise each story aloud. Do at least two timed mock interviews with a peer or using an interview prep platform to sharpen pacing.

Ongoing: Review Vega's publicly available content such as engineering blog posts, LinkedIn updates from their team, and the specific job description for the role you applied to. Tailoring your examples to the domains they actually work in makes a noticeable difference.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping the clarifying questions in system design. Candidates often dive into a solution before agreeing on the constraints. Vega interviewers typically expect you to ask about data size, latency requirements, and update frequency before you start designing.

Talking only about model accuracy. Many candidates focus entirely on offline metrics and ignore the engineering side. Interviewers want to know how you serve the model, how you monitor it, and what your plan is when it breaks.

Overcomplicating the explanation. When asked to explain a concept, candidates sometimes go deep into maths or edge cases in an attempt to impress. A clear, correct explanation of the core idea is worth more than a complex answer that loses the thread midway.

Not quantifying results in behavioural answers. Saying 'the model improved performance' is weak. Even approximate numbers or observable outcomes, such as 'reduced manual review volume by roughly half, based on the team's estimate,' are far more convincing.

Bluffing on tools or techniques you do not know. Interviewers notice this quickly, and it damages credibility for the rest of the conversation. If you have not used a specific tool, say so, then describe how you would approach learning it or what comparable experience you bring.

Ignoring failure scenarios. Most candidates present only the happy path. Vega interviewers respond well to candidates who proactively mention what could go wrong in a system they are describing and how they would handle it.

How many rounds does the Vega ML Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report that the process typically runs across 3 to 4 rounds. What programming languages and tools should I focus on for preparation?

Python is the primary language for ML Engineer roles at most Indian product companies, and this is consistent with what candidates report for Vega. You should be comfortable with scikit-learn, pandas, NumPy, and at least one deep learning framework such as PyTorch or TensorFlow. Familiarity with SQL, Docker, and MLOps tools like MLflow or Weights and Biases is commonly expected for mid-senior profiles.

Some candidates report receiving a take-home case study or coding assignment, though this is not universal across all teams at Vega. If assigned, it typically involves exploring a dataset, building a model, and presenting your reasoning and findings. Treat it as an opportunity to demonstrate production-quality code and clear communication, not just a working notebook.
