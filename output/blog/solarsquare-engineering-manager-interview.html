01 Overview

Overview

SolarSquare is one of India's fastest-growing rooftop solar companies, focused on making clean energy affordable and accessible for homeowners across the country. The engineering team builds across multiple domains: mobile apps for homeowners, IoT device management for solar panels, backend data platforms, and internal tools used by sales and installation teams. With 177 open roles currently listed, the company is in an active growth phase, and Engineering Managers here are expected to move fast without letting quality slip.

The role typically means owning a product squad, setting technical direction for your domain, and working closely with product, design, and field operations. You are accountable for both delivery speed and team health. Candidates report a mix of technical depth checks and leadership scenario questions across multiple rounds. Salary bands from knok's jobradar data show Manager-level roles in the 35-60 LPA range, Senior Manager at 55-90 LPA, and Director at 90-150+ LPA.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for all behavioural questions. Here are three examples tailored to an Engineering Manager interview at a company like SolarSquare.

Q: Tell us about a time you balanced technical debt against shipping a new feature.

*Situation:* Our team was running a mobile app for field installation technicians. The codebase had grown quickly and had no proper error handling for network failures, causing silent failures in the field.

*Task:* Product wanted a new job-scheduling feature shipped within a tight timeline. I had to decide whether to address that debt first or ship the feature and handle it later.

*Action:* I ran a short spike with two engineers to estimate the debt-reduction effort and the risk of deferring it. We found that silent failures were already causing re-work for field staff and had shown up repeatedly in team retrospectives. I proposed a split approach: one engineer would refactor the network layer in parallel while the rest built the scheduler. I adjusted the overall timeline by one week and presented the trade-off to the product manager with clear reasoning and data.

*Result:* We shipped the scheduler on the revised timeline. The network refactor meaningfully reduced reported field failures. Product trusted the team more on future scoping decisions because we showed our thinking transparently.

---

Q: How have you handled an underperforming engineer?

*Situation:* A mid-level engineer on my team was consistently missing estimates and shipping code that needed heavy review. The rest of the team had quietly started picking up the slack, which was creating resentment.

*Task:* I needed to address the performance issue without losing the engineer, who had good domain knowledge, or damaging team trust.

*Action:* I had a direct one-on-one where I named specific patterns I had observed, not vague generalisations. I learned the engineer was struggling with unclear requirements upstream, not with technical ability. I worked with the product manager to improve spec quality for that engineer's tasks and put together a structured plan with clear milestones and regular check-ins. I was transparent with the team only to the extent of saying 'I am working on improving how we hand off requirements.'

*Result:* Delivery consistency improved noticeably over the following weeks. The engineer eventually became the person who pushed for better specs across the whole team. No attrition, no morale dip.

---

Q: Describe a situation where you had to make a technical decision in a domain you were not fully expert in.

*Situation:* I was asked to lead a team building IoT firmware update pipelines for rooftop solar inverters. My background was in backend services, not embedded systems or hardware.

*Task:* I had to make architectural decisions about how firmware updates would be pushed to devices in the field, some of which had intermittent connectivity.

*Action:* I set up knowledge-sharing sessions with the firmware engineers on the team and brought in an external advisor for a short engagement. I focused my energy on the parts where my backend experience applied directly: reliability guarantees, rollback logic, and monitoring. I was explicit with my team about the division of expertise, telling them: 'You are the domain experts on the device side. My job is to make sure the system as a whole is reliable and observable.'

*Result:* We shipped a pipeline that could handle partial connectivity and had automatic rollback on failures. The team appreciated the clarity of ownership. I came out of the project knowing enough about the domain to ask the right questions in future planning sessions.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the standard format for behavioural questions and works well for every leadership scenario question at SolarSquare. Practice delivering each story clearly and concisely before your rounds.

For technical strategy questions, use a three-part structure: first, state the trade-offs you saw; second, explain the criteria you used to decide; third, describe how you communicated the decision and what you learned. Interviewers at engineering-led startups care less about whether you made the 'right' call and more about whether your reasoning was sound and transparent.

For 'how do you measure your team' questions, prepare a concrete answer covering three layers: delivery health (cycle time, on-time rate), code quality (review feedback patterns, production incidents), and people health (retention, growth conversations, team sentiment). Avoid abstract answers like 'I check in regularly.'

For mission-alignment questions, connect your answer to something concrete from your past work. SolarSquare interviewers want to know that you understand the product has real-world impact on energy adoption, not just that you can repeat the company mission statement back to them.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

SolarSquare is a startup in the climate-tech and clean energy space, which shapes what interviewers look for in an Engineering Manager.

Speed with quality. The company is growing fast. Interviewers want managers who can ship reliably without creating fragile systems. Show that you have a framework for deciding when to go fast and when to slow down.

Cross-functional fluency. SolarSquare's engineering touches sales tools, customer apps, IoT systems, and field operations. Engineering Managers here collaborate with non-engineering teams constantly. Candidates who have only worked in pure-product environments may find the scope unfamiliar.

Ownership mindset. Candidates report that SolarSquare values people who treat problems as their own, not as someone else's ticket. Avoid answers that frame you as a coordinator or process manager. Show that you go deep when needed.

Mission fit. This is a company with a clear purpose: making solar energy accessible across India. Interviewers notice when candidates treat it as 'just another startup.' You do not need to be a solar expert, but genuine interest in the problem space matters.

Honest self-awareness. Questions about failures and wrong decisions are common. Interviewers are checking whether you can reflect clearly without being defensive. A well-told story about a mistake you learned from is more impressive than a polished success story with no rough edges.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Research and context-building. Read SolarSquare's public communications, product reviews, and anything written by their engineering leadership. Understand the core product: how homeowners buy, install, and monitor solar systems. Map your past experience to their domain, whether that is IoT, mobile, data pipelines, or field operations tools.

Week 2: Story bank. Write out 8-10 STAR stories covering: scaling a team, handling underperformance, resolving product-engineering conflict, a technical mistake, cross-functional collaboration, hiring decisions, and a time you operated outside your comfort zone. Practice telling each story clearly before your first round.

Week 3: Technical and system design review. Engineering Managers at SolarSquare are typically expected to engage meaningfully in system design discussions. Refresh your thinking on distributed systems fundamentals, mobile-backend communication patterns, and IoT device management. Be ready to reason through trade-offs at a whiteboard level without necessarily writing code.

Before each round: Prepare two or three questions that show you have thought seriously about the role. Strong examples: how the team handles incidents that affect physical installations in the field, what the engineering roadmap looks like for the next few quarters, or how the company thinks about hiring for domain knowledge versus general engineering skill.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Over-indexing on process. Saying 'I introduced agile ceremonies and everything improved' is a red flag at a fast-moving startup. SolarSquare interviewers want to hear about outcomes and judgment, not process for its own sake.

Vague answers to people questions. 'I give regular feedback' is not an answer. Be specific about what feedback you gave, when, and what changed. Interviewers follow up when an answer feels rehearsed but hollow.

Ignoring the hardware-software boundary. SolarSquare's product includes physical devices installed at customer homes. Candidates who only talk about pure software problems and show no curiosity about the IoT or field operations dimension typically do not progress.

Not asking about the role. Candidates who ask no questions, or only ask about compensation, signal low interest. Prepare thoughtful questions about the team, the technical challenges, and how success is measured in the first few months.

Treating mission as a formality. Starting your 'why SolarSquare' answer with phrases like 'I am passionate about clean energy' without backing it up with specifics is unconvincing. Connect to something real: a specific product challenge, the market context in India, or a genuine interest in energy access as a problem worth solving.
