01 Overview

Overview

Housing.com is one of India's leading proptech platforms, built on data-driven property search and a fast-moving engineering culture. An Engineering Manager role here means owning multiple product squads, shipping features at pace, and keeping platform reliability high for millions of property seekers. Housing currently has 188 open engineering roles across levels, which reflects active team expansion. Interview processes typically run across four to six rounds, candidates report, covering a technical screen, system design, engineering leadership, cross-functional collaboration, and a final round with senior leadership. Expect deep dives into how you build and retain engineering teams, manage delivery timelines, and think about technical quality inside a product-driven environment.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How do you maintain code quality and reliability while shipping fast?

*Situation:* At my previous company, we were running a regular sprint cycle but deployment quality was slipping. Rollbacks were happening frequently, sometimes after every other release.

*Task:* I needed to tighten the feedback loop without adding bureaucracy that would slow down delivery.

*Action:* I introduced trunk-based development with short-lived feature branches, mandatory automated test coverage above a team-agreed threshold, and a lightweight pre-merge review checklist. I also ran a one-hour 'quality hour' every Friday where the team reviewed recent incidents together, not to assign blame, but to identify one concrete improvement per sprint.

*Result:* Within a couple of quarters, rollbacks dropped significantly and the team reported feeling more confident shipping. The quality hour became a permanent ritual the team itself owned.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you had to let a strong IC go or manage them out.

*Situation:* I had a senior engineer who was technically exceptional but consistently missed deadlines and blocked others from reviewing their code until the last moment.

*Task:* I needed to either turn around the situation or make a hard decision that protected the team.

*Action:* I started with a direct, private conversation about the impact (not the intent) of their behaviour. We co-created a structured improvement plan with clear milestones and a defined review window. I checked in weekly and gave transparent, documented feedback. At the end of that window, the patterns had not changed materially.

*Result:* We parted ways through a structured off-boarding. The team's velocity and morale improved within the next sprint cycle. The lesson I carried forward: clarity and documentation matter more than waiting for organic change.

---

Q: How do you evaluate the technical health of a team you have just taken over?

*Situation:* I joined a new organisation and inherited a team of engineers working on a payments module that had accumulated significant technical debt.

*Task:* Before making any changes, I needed an honest picture of where things stood technically and culturally.

*Action:* I ran one-on-one conversations with every engineer in the first two weeks, asking what they were proud of and what kept them up at night. I also did a code review of a representative recent feature, looked at incident logs from the past several months, and mapped deployment frequency against rollback rates.

*Result:* Within the first month I had a clear health report: two critical reliability gaps, three areas of strong engineering practice, and one morale issue tied to unclear ownership. I presented this to leadership with a prioritised action plan, which became the team's roadmap for the next quarter.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the default for all behavioural questions. Keep your situation brief (two sentences), spend most of your time on Action (this is what interviewers evaluate), and give a concrete Result. If you do not have specific numbers, describe the qualitative change clearly.

For system design questions, use a structured flow: clarify scope and constraints, estimate scale, design the data model, design the API layer, then discuss trade-offs in your choices. For a proptech platform like Housing, think about search latency, geo-based filtering, and image delivery as recurring concerns.

For leadership and culture questions, use what candidates call the 'impact-then-process' framing: lead with the outcome you achieved or the decision you made, then explain your reasoning. Interviewers at Housing typically want to hear that you think in terms of team outcomes, not individual heroics.

For technical trade-off questions, show your reasoning explicitly. Name the options you considered, the criteria you used (speed, cost, reliability, team skill), and what you would do differently now. Intellectual honesty about past decisions signals maturity.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Housing interviewers typically look for Engineering Managers who can balance product velocity with technical discipline. The company operates in a competitive proptech market where feature releases are frequent, so they want managers with a track record of shipping without sacrificing reliability.

They tend to value clarity of communication. Can you explain a complex engineering decision to a product manager or a business stakeholder without jargon? Candidates report that cross-functional collaboration questions come up in almost every interview loop.

Culture at Housing, as candidates describe it, is outcome-oriented and moves quickly. Interviewers respond well to candidates who show they can make decisions with incomplete information and course-correct fast, rather than waiting for perfect data.

People management depth matters too. Expect at least one round that goes deep on how you hire, give feedback, handle underperformance, and keep engineers growing. Thin or theoretical answers here tend to flag candidates.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Foundation

Read Housing's engineering blog and any publicly available material on their search and recommendation infrastructure. Review system design patterns for high-traffic listing platforms (search indexing, geo queries, image delivery pipelines). Refresh your knowledge of common backend and distributed systems concepts relevant to a consumer-facing proptech product.

Week 2: Behavioural prep

Build a solid bank of real stories from your career, covering key leadership themes: hiring, letting someone go, conflict, technical trade-off decisions, cross-functional alignment, incident response, and team growth. Structure each story in STAR format and practise out loud, not just in your head.

Week 3: Mock rounds

Do at least two timed system design sessions with a peer or mentor. Do one full mock behavioural interview covering leadership and people management questions. Record yourself if possible and review how concisely you answer.

Before each round

Look up the interviewer on LinkedIn to understand their domain. Prepare two to three genuine questions about Housing's engineering challenges that show you have thought about the role beyond the job description.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Talking about your team's wins as if they were your individual wins. Interviewers at companies like Housing probe for this specifically. Say 'we' for outcomes and 'I' only for decisions you personally made.

Being vague on process. Saying 'I improved team velocity' without explaining how you measured it or what specifically changed reads as thin. Always attach a concrete action to a concrete outcome.

Skipping trade-offs in system design. Candidates who present one solution without discussing alternatives tend to score lower. Housing engineering interviewers typically want to hear your reasoning, not just your answer.

Ignoring the product context. Engineering Manager interviews at proptech companies are not purely technical. If you do not show curiosity about the business, the customer, or the competitive market, that tends to be a red flag for cross-functional fit.

Over-preparing for coding. Most EM loops at this level do not include live coding. Spending all your prep time on algorithm practice at the expense of system design and leadership stories is a common mismatch.

Asking no questions or asking about compensation too early. Interviewers note when a candidate has no curious, role-specific questions. Have at least two ready for every round.
