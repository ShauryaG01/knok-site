01 Overview

Overview

The company's core business is IT services, software outsourcing, and end-to-end digital transformation for enterprise clients in sectors like banking, manufacturing, retail, and healthcare. A PM here typically sits at the intersection of client engagement and internal product development, translating a client's business problems into software that actually ships.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates at FPT Asia Pacific typically face questions across three themes: product strategy, client and stakeholder management, and cross-geography execution. Here are the questions most commonly reported:

  1. Walk me through a product you owned end-to-end. What was your most important decision, and how did you make it?
  2. FPT serves enterprise clients across multiple countries. How do you localise a product for different markets without fragmenting your core roadmap?
  3. Describe a time you had to say no to a feature request from a key client. How did you handle the conversation?
  4. How do you prioritise between client-specific customisations and improvements to the shared platform?
  5. Tell me about a product that failed or underperformed. What did you learn, and what would you do differently?
  6. How do you work with offshore or distributed engineering teams to keep delivery quality high and requirements aligned?
  7. You receive a digital transformation brief from a manufacturing client with no clear product scope. Walk me through your first two weeks.
  8. How do you measure success for an internal enterprise tool compared to a customer-facing product?
  9. Describe a time you used data to change direction on a product decision that the team or a stakeholder disagreed with.
  10. How do you manage competing priorities when your engineering team, your sales team, and your client all want something different?
  11. What does a healthy product roadmap look like for a B2B IT services company like FPT?
  12. How do you stay close to the actual end user when your primary contact is the client's IT or procurement team, not the person using the software daily?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time you had to say no to a feature request from a key client.

*Situation:* I was PM for a SaaS logistics platform. Our largest client, who contributed a significant share of revenue, asked for a bespoke reporting module that would have taken the engineering team several weeks to build.

*Task:* I needed to decide whether to build it, redirect the resource, or find a third path, all while protecting the relationship with a client the account team was actively trying to expand.

*Action:* I mapped the request against our backlog and found that three other clients had made similar but not identical requests. I went back to the client with that data and proposed a configurable reporting module that served all three accounts. I offered early access and co-design sessions so they had a real voice in the outcome. I also briefed the account manager honestly on the trade-off before the client call, so we were aligned.

*Result:* The client agreed. The configurable module shipped and was adopted by all three accounts, expanding the platform's stickiness. Candidates report that FPT interviewers respond well to answers that show commercial awareness sitting alongside product judgment.

---

Q: How do you work with offshore or distributed engineering teams to maintain delivery quality?

*Situation:* I was leading a digital onboarding product for a bank, with the engineering team based in a separate country and a firm client deadline approaching.

*Task:* I needed to maintain quality and speed without being physically co-located with the engineers, and without adding process overhead that would slow the team down.

*Action:* I introduced a lightweight 'definition of ready' checklist so no user story entered a sprint without acceptance criteria, edge cases documented, and mockups attached. I set up a brief weekly sync with just the tech lead, not the whole team, to catch blockers early before they became delays. I also created a shared domain glossary after noticing several misaligned assumptions were coming from terminology differences between banking contexts.

*Result:* Rework dropped measurably across the following sprints, and the client signed off on UAT with fewer change requests than the previous engagement. The account team used our delivery track record as a reference point in the next proposal.

---

Q: How do you prioritise between client-specific customisations and platform-level improvements?

*Situation:* At my previous company, the product served five enterprise clients. The backlog was increasingly dominated by one-off requests, and the engineering team felt like they were building five different products.

*Task:* I needed a transparent framework that leadership, the sales team, and the engineering team could all align on, not just a PM judgment call that could be overridden every quarter.

*Action:* I introduced a scoring model with three inputs: how many clients would benefit, the strategic value of the requesting client to the business, and the engineering cost estimate. Anything above a threshold went into the shared roadmap. Anything below could be funded as a paid customisation or deferred. I walked leadership through the model with real examples from the current backlog so they could challenge the logic before it became policy.

*Result:* Within two quarters, the share of platform-level work in the backlog grew, the team felt less fragmented, and every client received a consistent, defensible answer when their request was deprioritised. FPT interviewers typically look for frameworks you can explain clearly, not just outcomes.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the baseline for every behavioural question. Keep Situation and Task brief, two to three sentences combined, and spend most of your time on Action and Result. Thin on results? State what you learned and what you would do differently.

'Why, What, How' structure works well for roadmap and strategy questions. State why the problem matters to the business, what outcome you are targeting, then how you plan to get there. This is especially useful at FPT, where clients often arrive with a vague 'we need digital transformation' brief and expect the PM to add structure.

CIRCLES (Comprehend, Identify, Report, Cut, List, Evaluate, Summarise) helps with product design or product improvement questions. Always clarify who the user is before jumping to features. In a B2B context, the buyer and the daily user are often different people, so name both.

Hypothesis-driven thinking is valued at enterprise IT companies. When asked about metrics or a product that failed, lead with what you expected to happen, what actually happened, and what that told you. This signals analytical honesty rather than polished storytelling.

For prioritisation questions, name your framework explicitly (RICE, MoSCoW, weighted scoring) and explain why you chose it for that specific situation. Candidates report that generic answers like 'I align with stakeholders' land poorly at FPT. A repeatable, nameable process lands much better.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Enterprise and B2B instincts. FPT's core business is delivering technology to large clients, not building consumer apps. Interviewers want evidence that you distinguish between a client's stated request and their underlying business problem, and that you can push back respectfully when those differ.

Cross-cultural and cross-geography awareness. The company operates across multiple Asian markets and works with distributed delivery teams. Questions about localisation, time zone coordination, and managing client relationships across geographies are common. Show concrete examples, not generic claims about being 'a good communicator.'

Structured, clear communication. Candidates report that interviewers pay close attention to how precisely you articulate trade-offs. Use numbers where you have them, be honest when you do not, and avoid vague filler like 'we aligned and moved forward.'

Delivery orientation alongside product thinking. PM roles at IT services companies often require stronger project coordination skills than equivalent roles at pure product companies. Show that you can manage scope, timelines, and stakeholder expectations simultaneously.

Balance between client advocacy and internal protection. The strongest candidates show they can represent the client's needs to engineering while also protecting the team from unrealistic scope or timeline demands. Interviewers look for this tension acknowledged and navigated, not avoided.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Understand the company deeply. Read FPT Corporation's publicly available annual reports and press releases to understand their key verticals and recent wins. Identify two or three digital transformation projects they have publicised and think through what a PM's role would have looked like on each. This gives you material for 'why FPT' questions and shows genuine research.

Week 1-2: Build your story bank. Map your past experience to the question themes above. Prepare at least one strong STAR story for each of: saying no to a client request, working with a distributed team, using data to reverse a decision, and handling a product that underperformed. Practise each story out loud until it runs under two minutes.

Week 2: Practise B2B product cases. Pick an enterprise product you have used, an ERP, a logistics platform, a banking tool, and practise walking through how you would improve it. Focus on the client-user distinction: the company buying the software is not always the person using it daily. FPT interviewers respond well to candidates who name both.

Ongoing: Prepare your own questions. Ask about how PMs are embedded in client engagements, how roadmap decisions are made when client and internal priorities conflict, and what success looks like in the first six months. These questions signal that you understand the B2B services context and are thinking seriously about the role.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating it like a consumer product interview. Many candidates prepare only for 'design an app for X' questions. FPT's context is enterprise and services-led. If all your examples come from B2C products, spend time translating them into a B2B framing before you go in.

Vague answers on stakeholder management. Saying 'I communicated clearly with all stakeholders' without specifying what you said, to whom, and what changed as a result is one of the most common misses. Be specific about who was in the room and what you actually said.

Ignoring the client-user distinction. In B2B products, the buyer and the end user are frequently different people with different incentives. Candidates who conflate them reveal a gap in enterprise product thinking that is hard to recover from mid-interview.

Rattling off metrics without context. Numbers matter, but stating a metric without explaining what you expected and why the result was meaningful shows shallow analysis. Always pair a number with a 'and here is why that was good or bad.'

Coming to the interview with no questions. Candidates report that rounds at FPT typically leave time for your questions. Arriving with none, or asking only about compensation, signals low engagement. Ask about the team structure, the client engagement model, or a product challenge they are currently working through.

Assuming a purely agile delivery model. IT services companies often blend agile delivery with fixed-scope client contracts. If you have navigated this tension before, say so explicitly. If you have not, at least show awareness that it exists.
