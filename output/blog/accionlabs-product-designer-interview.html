02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up frequently in Accion Labs Product Designer interviews, based on candidate reports and the consulting-heavy nature of the role.

  1. Walk us through a product you designed end-to-end. What was your process from discovery to delivery?
  2. How do you handle a situation where your design recommendation conflicts with what an engineer says is too complex to build?
  3. Describe how you approach user research when the client has limited time or budget for it.
  4. How do you maintain design consistency across a large product when multiple feature teams are working in parallel?
  5. Tell us about a time you had to design for a domain you knew nothing about. How did you ramp up quickly?
  6. Designers at Accion Labs often juggle multiple client projects at once. How do you manage that context-switching without losing quality?
  7. How do you present and defend design decisions to a non-design audience, like an engineering lead or a business stakeholder?
  8. What does a good design handoff look like to you? Walk us through your process.
  9. Have you ever had to redesign a feature after it was already built? What caused it, and what did you learn?
  10. How do you measure whether a design is actually working after it ships?
  11. What is your approach to accessibility in your day-to-day design work?
  12. Tell us about your experience with design systems. Have you built one, contributed to one, or had to work without one?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format to keep answers concrete and under two minutes.

Q: Walk us through a product you designed end-to-end.

*Situation:* I joined a fintech startup mid-project. The product had a prototype, but users were dropping off during onboarding at a high rate.

*Task:* My job was to redesign the onboarding flow to improve completion, working with a product manager and two front-end engineers over a few weeks.

*Action:* I started with several user interviews to understand where and why people were stopping. I mapped a revised flow that cut the number of screens significantly, added inline validation, and ran two rounds of usability testing on prototypes before handoff to engineering.

*Result:* After the redesign shipped, the team tracked a meaningful improvement in onboarding completion based on product analytics. Support tickets about signup confusion also dropped noticeably in the first month.

---

Q: How do you handle conflict between your design and engineering constraints?

*Situation:* On a healthcare dashboard project, I proposed an animated data visualisation to make trends easier to read. The lead engineer flagged it would require a library they had not used before, adding scope to an already tight sprint.

*Task:* I needed to either defend the animation or find a solution that met the user need without extending the timeline.

*Action:* I went back to the user research and asked what the animation was actually solving. The core need was 'make the trend readable at a glance.' I redesigned using a static sparkline with clear colour coding, which the engineer confirmed was straightforward to build. I documented the animated version as a future enhancement.

*Result:* The feature shipped on time. The static version tested well with users, and the animated version was picked up in a later sprint when capacity opened up.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you designed for an unfamiliar domain.

*Situation:* I was assigned to a logistics client project with no prior exposure to warehouse management software.

*Task:* I had a short ramp-up window to deliver wireframes for a new inventory tracking module.

*Action:* I spent the first few days doing focused contextual research: reading the client's existing user manuals, sitting in on calls with warehouse supervisors, and mapping their workflow step by step. I identified that the biggest pain point was not the interface but the volume of manual data-entry steps, and I shared that insight with the product manager before touching any screen designs.

*Result:* The wireframes were approved with minor revisions. The product manager noted that the domain insight I surfaced early helped the team reprioritise a feature that had been on the backlog for months.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

The 'Problem First' opening. Before describing what you built, spend one sentence naming the user problem and the business context. Interviewers at consulting-heavy firms like Accion Labs want to see that you understand why a design decision was made, not just what was made.

The constraint acknowledgement. When discussing any design, briefly name the key constraints you worked within: timeline, technology, user literacy, accessibility requirements. This shows you design in the real world, not just in ideal conditions.

The 'so what' close. End every case study with a result that is meaningful to the business or the user. If you do not have hard metrics, name the qualitative signal you received: user interviews post-launch, stakeholder feedback, or changes in support ticket volume. Honest data, even thin data, beats vague claims.

The trade-off narration. If you made a design choice that had a downside, say so. Interviewers at product engineering firms value designers who can articulate what was sacrificed and why the trade-off was worth it. This signals maturity and makes your answers memorable.

Handling 'what would you do differently' questions. Pick one genuine learning, not a humble-brag. The best answers are specific: 'I would have involved the engineer earlier when I was exploring animation options' is stronger than 'I would have done more research.'

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Client-facing communication skills. Accion Labs is a services company. Designers regularly present work to client stakeholders who may have no design background. Interviewers look for candidates who can explain decisions clearly and handle pushback without becoming defensive.

Speed with structure, not just speed. Delivery timelines at consulting firms tend to be tight. Candidates who show they have a fast but principled design process stand out. Bring examples where you made a smart shortcut, like substituting a full research cycle with targeted expert interviews, without compromising the outcome.

Cross-functional fluency. The ability to work comfortably with engineers, product managers, and business analysts is tested directly. Interviewers want to see that you understand technical constraints at a basic level and do not treat engineers as people who simply execute your specs.

Portfolio depth, not breadth. Candidates report that interviewers at Accion Labs go several layers deep on one or two projects rather than skimming across many. Know your portfolio projects well enough to explain every decision, including the ones that did not work.

Adaptability across domains. Because Accion Labs serves clients across industries, interviewers value designers who are comfortable learning a new domain quickly. Be ready to talk about how you approach an unfamiliar vertical from scratch.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know your own work cold. Pick your two or three strongest portfolio projects. For each one, write down the user problem, your process, the key trade-offs you made, and the result. Practice narrating each one in under two minutes. Recording yourself and playing it back helps catch filler language and vague claims.

Week 2: Research Accion Labs specifically. Look at their public case studies and note the types of industries they serve. Read the job description carefully and flag any tools, domains, or skills mentioned. Tailoring at least one portfolio example to client industries Accion Labs works in (fintech, healthcare, enterprise software) typically makes a strong impression.

Week 3: Practice the design exercise out loud. Candidates report one round typically involves either a take-home brief or a live whiteboard or Figma session. The goal is not a pixel-perfect output. It is a clear, structured narration of how you think as you work. Practice with a timer and talk through your process as if explaining it to a product manager.

Before the final round. Prepare a few questions for the interviewer that show you understand what it means to design in a services context. Asking how design and engineering collaborate on client projects, or how client feedback gets incorporated into the design process, signals you have thought beyond the portfolio review.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Presenting too many projects superficially. Candidates sometimes try to show everything in their portfolio to appear well-rounded. Interviewers at Accion Labs typically prefer depth. Lead with your two strongest projects and be ready to go several layers deep on each.

Skipping the 'why' in design decisions. Saying 'I chose this layout because it looks cleaner' without a user rationale is a common gap. Always connect visual or interaction choices back to a user need or a constraint you were working within.

Listing tools you have barely used. If you have listed Figma, Maze, or any prototyping tool on your resume, expect a follow-up question about how you used it in a real project. Do not list tools you have only experimented with briefly.

Treating the design exercise as a solo sprint. Candidates report that interviewers value those who ask clarifying questions before diving into a brief. Jumping straight into solutions without questioning assumptions is a red flag in consulting-context roles where ambiguity is the norm.

Being vague about results. 'The client was happy' is not a result. Even rough qualitative outcomes, like a reduction in support tickets or positive feedback from user testing, are more credible than general satisfaction statements.

Not asking questions at the end. In a services firm interview, your questions signal whether you understand the business model and the day-to-day reality of the role. Candidates who ask nothing tend to come across as disengaged.
