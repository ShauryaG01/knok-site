01 Overview

Overview

BILL is a US-based financial operations platform that helps small and medium businesses manage payments, invoicing, and cash flow. As of July 2026, knok's job radar shows 56 open Product Designer roles at BILL, making it one of the more active companies hiring designers right now.

Product Designers at BILL work on financial workflows where clarity and trust matter as much as visual polish. Candidates report that the process typically includes a recruiter screen, a portfolio review with a design lead, a take-home design challenge, and final rounds with cross-functional stakeholders. Rounds vary by team, so confirm the structure with your recruiter early.

BILL's design culture emphasises simplifying complexity for SMB owners who are often not technical. If you have experience designing for fintech, SaaS dashboards, or multi-step approval workflows, that background travels well here.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly in candidate reports and reflect BILL's core product priorities.

  1. Walk us through a project where you simplified a complex, multi-step workflow for a non-technical user.
  2. How do you approach designing for trust in a product that handles real business money?
  3. Describe a time user research or data changed the direction of your design.
  4. How do you balance shipping speed with design quality when deadlines are tight?
  5. Tell us about a time you pushed back on a product or engineering decision and how you handled it.
  6. How would you redesign a first-time invoice creation experience to reduce user errors?
  7. Describe your process for building or contributing to a design system used by multiple teams.
  8. How do you design for accessibility in a B2B financial product where users have varying tech comfort?
  9. Walk us through a project where you made real-time design tradeoffs during engineering implementation.
  10. How do you measure whether a design succeeded after it ships?
  11. Tell us about a time you designed for multiple user roles with different permissions or mental models.
  12. What does 'good UX for a small business owner' mean to you, and how does that show in your portfolio?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through a project where you simplified a complex workflow for a non-technical user.

*Situation:* At my previous company, our accounts payable module had a lengthy multi-step approval flow that finance teams found confusing. Drop-off analytics showed many users abandoned the process mid-way.

*Task:* I was asked to redesign the flow without removing any compliance steps that the legal team required.

*Action:* I ran several user interviews with finance managers to map their mental model. I found that users thought of approval in three broad stages, not as many individual steps, so I grouped the steps into three labelled phases. I added contextual help at each decision point and replaced technical jargon with plain labels. I worked closely with the engineer to ensure the grouping did not add API complexity.

*Result:* In A/B testing, task completion improved and support tickets about the flow dropped noticeably. The redesign shipped within the sprint cycle.

---

Q: Describe a time data changed the direction of your design.

*Situation:* I had designed a dashboard summary card that I believed would help users spot overdue invoices at a glance.

*Task:* Before full build, we ran a usability test with a group of SMB owners.

*Action:* I observed that users ignored the card entirely and went straight to the invoice list. In post-test interviews, I found they mistrusted summarised numbers without seeing the source data. I revised the card to show a live count that was directly clickable into a filtered list, removing the standalone summary.

*Result:* In the follow-up test, users interacted with the card immediately and said it was 'finally useful.' The insight also fed into our design system's data display guidelines.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you pushed back on a product decision.

*Situation:* A PM wanted to add a prominent upsell banner inside the payment confirmation screen to promote a premium feature.

*Task:* I felt this placement would damage user trust at the most sensitive point in the flow, right after a user submits a payment.

*Action:* Instead of simply saying no, I pulled session recordings showing users paused on confirmation screens longer than any other screen, which I framed as 'high-anxiety moments.' I proposed placing the upsell in the post-payment success state instead, and presented a tradeoff table comparing both options to the PM and stakeholders.

*Result:* The team agreed to test my alternative placement. The upsell conversion was comparable, and the satisfaction score on the payment flow held steady.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For workflow and complexity questions, use a 'chaos to clarity' structure: describe the original user confusion with evidence, explain the insight that unlocked the solution, show your design decision, and end with a measurable or observable outcome. BILL interviewers care that you can name the insight, not just the output.

For data and research questions, lead with what you expected versus what you found. Interviewers want intellectual honesty. A story where research confirmed your hypothesis is less compelling than one where it surprised you.

For conflict and pushback questions, the safest structure is: state your concern clearly, show the evidence you used to make the case, describe the alternative you proposed, and end with how the team landed. Frame the story as 'here is how we reached a better decision together,' not 'I was right.'

For system and process questions, talk in layers: user problem, design decision, team collaboration, and long-term maintainability. BILL's design system spans multiple product areas, so interviewers look for designers who think beyond their own screen.

For hypothetical redesign questions (like the invoice flow), open by asking clarifying questions aloud and stating your assumptions before diving in. Interviewers are evaluating your thinking process, not whether you land on a single correct answer.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

BILL interviewers typically look for four qualities across the process.

Empathy for non-technical users. Most BILL customers are small business owners, not product-savvy power users. Show that you default to plain language, progressive disclosure, and designs that reduce anxiety rather than pile on features.

Systematic thinking. Candidates who reference design systems, component reuse, and how their work fits into a larger product are rated higher than those who present one-off screens. Bring examples of contribution to shared systems.

Cross-functional collaboration. BILL's design team works closely with product managers and engineers. Interviewers pay attention to how you describe stakeholders. Saying 'I convinced the PM' reads very differently from 'I presented the tradeoffs and we decided together.'

Data-informed, not data-dependent. You should be comfortable with metrics and qualitative research, but also confident making decisions when data is thin. Show that you know when to run a test and when to just ship and learn.

Financial domain knowledge is a plus but not a requirement. Clear thinking about trust, transparency, and error prevention matters more than fintech-specific experience.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Understand BILL's product. Sign up for the free trial or watch walkthrough videos on their site. Pay attention to the payment and approval flows. Note any moments that feel confusing or wordy. These are natural starting points for your portfolio framing.

Curate two or three portfolio projects. Pick work that shows workflow simplification, data-informed decisions, and collaboration. For each project, prepare a short verbal walkthrough: problem, insight, decision, outcome. Practise saying it aloud, not reading from slides.

Prepare for the design exercise. Candidates typically report a take-home challenge focused on redesigning a specific flow or feature. Time-box your work to what the brief asks. Show your thinking process clearly: document assumptions, show explorations you ruled out, and explain tradeoffs. Submit something complete rather than ambitious and unfinished.

Practise STAR answers. Write out answers to the questions listed above. The goal is not to memorise scripts but to have the key beats ready so you are not scrambling mid-interview.

Prepare questions for them. Ask about how the design team measures impact, how design system contributions work, and what a typical week looks like. These show genuine interest and give you real signal about the role.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Presenting only final screens. BILL interviewers want to see your process. If your portfolio shows only polished Figma files with no context, you will struggle to answer follow-up questions. Add a slide or two on the problem, your research, and alternatives you considered.

Generic answers about user-centred design. Saying 'I always put the user first' without evidence reads as a filler phrase. Every answer needs a specific project, a specific insight, and a specific decision you made.

Skipping clarifying questions in the design exercise. Jumping straight into solutions without stating assumptions is a common reason candidates are rejected. Treat the exercise brief as a conversation starter, not a complete spec.

Underselling cross-functional work. Many designers focus only on the visual output and forget to mention how they aligned with PMs, collaborated with engineers during implementation, or influenced a roadmap decision. These moments are exactly what BILL is looking for.

Ignoring the SMB user context. If your portfolio work is all consumer or enterprise, make the translation explicit. Say how designing for a small business owner differs from your past context and what you would carry over. Do not assume the interviewer will make that connection for you.

Negotiating without data. Going in without market salary data leaves money on the table. Look up Glassdoor and levels.fyi before the offer conversation, and use the salary bands in this guide as a starting point.
