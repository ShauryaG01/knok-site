01 Overview

Overview

Agoda, the travel-tech platform known for hotel and flight bookings across Asia and beyond, runs a large engineering operation with a strong India presence. As of July 2026, knok jobradar tracked 299 open roles at Agoda, out of 313 Technical Program Manager positions open across India at that time. That volume of hiring signals Agoda is building out its program management function at scale.

Bangalore leads TPM hiring with 41 openings. Delhi follows with 14, Pune with 13, Hyderabad with 12, Chennai with 5, and Mumbai with 1. If you are open to relocating, Bangalore gives you the widest choice of teams and roles.

The TPM role at Agoda is engineering-heavy. You will own programs that span multiple product teams, manage technical dependencies, and communicate status to senior leaders. Candidates report that the interview process typically includes a recruiter screen, a hiring manager conversation, a technical depth round, and a panel of behavioral interviews. Process details can vary by team, so treat every stage seriously.

Salary data for this role is not available in current public filings. Publicly reported ranges on Glassdoor and levels.fyi can give you a starting reference, but verify with recent candidates in forums like Blind or LinkedIn.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Based on patterns candidates report from Agoda TPM interviews, these are the questions that come up most often:

  1. Walk me through a large-scale program you owned end-to-end. How did you manage cross-team dependencies?
  2. Agoda serves travelers across many countries. How have you managed programs with geographically distributed teams or stakeholders?
  3. Describe a time a critical technical risk surfaced mid-program. What did you do to contain it?
  4. How do you decide what to prioritize when engineering, product, and business each want something different?
  5. Tell me about a time you influenced a technical direction without having formal authority over the team.
  6. How do you communicate program health to senior leadership? What signals or metrics do you use?
  7. Walk us through how you would set up a program to launch a new payment method across multiple markets.
  8. Describe a situation where you had to push back on a stakeholder request. How did you handle it?
  9. What do you do when an engineering team consistently slips their delivery commitments?
  10. How do you approach programs that touch backend systems, data pipelines, or platform infrastructure?
  11. How do you define success for a program, and how do you know when you have achieved it?
  12. Tell me about a process improvement you introduced that meaningfully reduced delivery risk.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample STAR answers for the questions candidates find hardest:

Q: Walk me through a large-scale program you owned end-to-end.

*Situation:* My company was migrating its checkout service to a new payments infrastructure. Multiple engineering squads, a product team, and the finance compliance function were all involved, each with competing timelines.

*Task:* My job was to land the migration on schedule with no revenue-impacting incidents at launch.

*Action:* On day one I built a dependency map and identified the compliance team sign-off as the longest lead-time item. I pulled their review forward by several weeks and introduced a shared risk register, visible to all leads, alongside a weekly cross-functional sync.

*Result:* We delivered a week ahead of the revised plan with no P1 incidents at launch. The early compliance engagement prevented what would have been a significant delay to the overall program.

---

Q: Describe a time a critical technical risk surfaced mid-program.

*Situation:* Shortly before a major feature launch, the backend team discovered that a third-party data provider had changed its API response format without notice.

*Task:* I needed to assess the impact quickly and decide whether to delay the launch or find a workaround within the existing plan.

*Action:* I immediately pulled together the backend lead, the data engineer, and the product manager for a focused working session. We scoped the fix, confirmed it was feasible within days, and communicated a revised launch date to leadership with a clear rationale.

*Result:* We launched with a short, planned delay and no customer-facing issues. Leadership appreciated the transparent communication rather than discovering the problem on launch day.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you influenced a technical decision without formal authority.

*Situation:* An engineering team wanted to build a custom internal tool instead of adopting a standard platform solution already used by other teams in the organization.

*Task:* I believed the custom build would create long-term maintenance cost and delay the program, but I had no authority to block their decision.

*Action:* I prepared a short comparison of the two paths, focusing on total ownership cost and delivery risk, and shared it with both the team lead and the engineering director. I also connected the team with a squad that had faced the same choice and gone with the platform solution.

*Result:* The team agreed to adopt the platform solution. The program delivered on schedule and the team avoided a maintenance burden that would have slowed future work significantly.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioral questions. Every 'tell me about a time' question deserves a structured Situation, Task, Action, Result answer. Keep the Situation brief. Spend most of your time on Action, since that is what differentiates you from other candidates. The Result should be concrete, though even directional outcomes ('we reduced escalations' or 'leadership approved the next phase') are better than no result at all.

Program setup framework for hypothetical scenarios. When asked 'how would you approach launching X across multiple markets,' use a structured walkthrough: clarify scope and success metrics first, then map stakeholders and dependencies, then identify risks and mitigation, then define your communication and escalation plan. Agoda operates across Asia-Pacific and Europe, so showing awareness of localization, regulatory, and time-zone complexity will resonate with interviewers.

Data-driven status reporting. When asked how you track program health, describe a layered approach: team-level signals (sprint completion, open blockers), program-level milestones, and a simple red/amber/green summary for leadership. Mention that you tailor the level of detail to the audience. This signals stakeholder maturity, which Agoda interviewers actively look for.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Agoda interviewers for the TPM role are typically looking for several qualities, based on what candidates report.

Technical credibility. You do not need to write code in the interview, but you should be comfortable discussing system design concepts, understanding trade-offs in backend and distributed architecture, and engaging with engineers as a peer. Candidates who cannot hold a technical conversation at this level often do not advance past early rounds.

Structured thinking under ambiguity. Agoda operates in a fast-moving, multi-market environment. Interviewers will deliberately leave scenario questions vague to see whether you ask clarifying questions, structure your thinking out loud, and arrive at a reasoned plan rather than jumping straight to an answer.

Stakeholder management depth. The company has many product lines and teams working in parallel. Interviewers want evidence that you can manage upward to leadership, sideways to peer teams, and downward to engineering without losing anyone. Specific examples from your past work matter far more than generic claims.

Ownership and follow-through. Agoda's engineering culture values a strong ownership mindset. Interviewers notice whether your stories end with 'the team did X' or 'I drove X to completion.' Take clear credit for the parts you personally drove, while being honest about where others contributed.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused preparation approach for the Agoda TPM interview:

Build your story bank first. Write out several programs you have managed, each as a short STAR story. Cover a range of themes: cross-team dependency management, a risk that materialized, a stakeholder conflict, a process improvement, and a program you are most proud of. Practicing these out loud matters more than writing them perfectly on paper.

Sharpen your technical foundation. Review the fundamentals of distributed systems, API design, and data pipeline architecture at a conceptual level. You do not need to code, but you should be able to ask an engineer good questions about technical trade-offs. Reading recent engineering blog posts from Agoda can help you understand their technology choices and the scale they operate at.

Research Agoda specifically. Read Agoda's engineering blog, recent product announcements, and any publicly available interviews with their engineering leaders. Understand their core products: accommodation, flights, and activities. Think about where a TPM adds value in each area. Prepare a few questions for your interviewers that show genuine curiosity about the team's challenges, not just generic career questions.

Practice with peers. Ask a colleague or mentor to run a mock behavioral round with you. Record yourself if no one is available. Hearing your own answers helps you cut filler, tighten your STAR structure, and notice when you are being vague about your personal contribution.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Being vague about your personal contribution. Saying 'we delivered the program' without explaining what you specifically did is the most common TPM interview mistake. Interviewers want to know what you drove, not what the team collectively achieved.

Skipping the technical detail. When describing a past program, candidates sometimes focus only on process and people management without mentioning any technical context. At Agoda, showing that you understood the underlying technical decisions is important and expected.

Treating hypotheticals as rhetorical questions. When an interviewer asks 'how would you handle X,' some candidates say 'it depends' and stop there. That is not an answer. Use a framework, make reasonable assumptions out loud, and show your reasoning step by step.

Not asking clarifying questions. In scenario questions, jumping straight to an answer without checking scope and constraints signals poor judgment. Pausing to ask 'what is the scale of this program?' or 'which teams are involved?' shows program management maturity.

Underselling outcomes. Many candidates describe what they did but forget to state the result. Even a directional result ('we reduced the frequency of escalations' or 'the launch went live without major incidents') is far better than ending a story on the action and trailing off.

Ignoring the multi-market context. Agoda serves customers across many countries. If your stories have any international or cross-regional dimension, highlight it. If they do not, at least show awareness of what multi-market complexity means for program planning and stakeholder management.

How many rounds does the Agoda TPM interview typically have?

Candidates report that the process typically includes a recruiter screen, a hiring manager conversation, a technical or system-design discussion, and one or more behavioral panel interviews. The exact number of rounds can vary by team and level. Budget for several conversations and treat each one as a full evaluation, not a warm-up.

Do I need to code in the Agoda TPM interview?

Candidates generally report no coding exercises in the TPM process. However, you should be comfortable discussing technical concepts, asking engineers good questions about architecture trade-offs, and demonstrating that you can engage meaningfully with an engineering team. Being technically fluent, without necessarily writing code, is what interviewers look for.

What should I research about Agoda before the interview?
