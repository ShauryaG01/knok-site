02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These are the questions candidates commonly report from Executive Talent Ltd Data Analyst interviews. Prepare a concrete answer for each before your first round.

  1. Walk me through a project where you used data to solve a real business problem.
  2. Write a SQL query to find the top 5 customers by revenue in the last 30 days.
  3. How would you handle missing or inconsistent data in a dataset you have been given?
  4. Explain the difference between a left join and an inner join, and when you would use each.
  5. You have been given a dashboard that stakeholders stopped using. How would you diagnose why and fix it?
  6. How do you decide which metrics matter most for a new business question?
  7. Describe a time you disagreed with a business stakeholder about what the data was showing. What did you do?
  8. What is the difference between a mean and a median, and when does it matter which one you use?
  9. You spot an unexpected spike in a key metric overnight. Walk me through your investigation process.
  10. How do you communicate a complex finding to someone who is not technical?
  11. Have you built or maintained any automated reports or pipelines? Describe the process.
  12. What tools have you used for data visualisation, and how do you choose between them?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for all behavioural questions. Here are three worked examples.

Q: Walk me through a project where you used data to solve a real business problem.

*Situation:* Our e-commerce team noticed that cart abandonment had crept up over two quarters but nobody knew why.

*Task:* I was asked to dig into the purchase funnel data and find where users were dropping off.

*Action:* I pulled session-level data from our analytics tool, cleaned it in Python, and built a funnel visualisation in Tableau. I segmented by device type and found that mobile users were abandoning at the payment step at a much higher rate than desktop users. I ran SQL queries to confirm the pattern held across regions and time periods, then wrote a one-page brief for the product team.

*Result:* The product team prioritised a mobile payment UX fix. Candidates in similar situations commonly report seeing conversion improvements after such fixes, though specific numbers vary by context.

---

Q: Describe a time you disagreed with a stakeholder about what the data was showing.

*Situation:* A marketing manager was convinced a recent campaign had driven a large sales uplift and wanted to double the budget for the next quarter.

*Task:* I had to review the analysis behind the claim before budget decisions were finalised.

*Action:* I found the original analysis had not controlled for a seasonal spike that happens every year in that period. I rebuilt the comparison using a year-over-year baseline and showed the incremental lift was much smaller than reported. I brought the manager into the process early, shared my methodology step by step, and framed it as 'let us make sure the investment is protected' rather than a challenge to their work.

*Result:* The manager appreciated the rigour. The campaign budget was adjusted to a smaller test, and we agreed on clearer measurement criteria going forward.

---

Q: You spot an unexpected spike in a key metric overnight. Walk me through your investigation.

*Situation:* I was on rotation monitoring our daily revenue dashboard and saw a spike well outside the normal range.

*Task:* I needed to quickly determine whether it was a real business event or a data quality issue before the morning leadership meeting.

*Action:* I first checked the data pipeline logs for any ingestion errors or duplicates. Finding none, I segmented the spike by region, product category, and channel. The spike was isolated to one product category and one region. I cross-referenced with the marketing calendar and found a flash sale had been run by a regional team without central notification.

*Result:* I flagged it as a legitimate event with context before the meeting, which saved leadership from making a reactive decision based on an unexplained number. I also proposed a shared marketing calendar integration to prevent the same confusion in future.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions. Every 'tell me about a time' question should follow Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep the Situation brief (one or two sentences), spend most of your answer on Action, and always close with a concrete Result.

Explain, Interpret, Recommend for analytical questions. When asked to look at data or solve a problem, do not just describe what you see. Explain what the numbers mean, interpret why it might be happening, and recommend what to do next. This shows business thinking alongside technical skill.

Think aloud for technical questions. For SQL or Python problems, narrate your thought process as you write. Interviewers want to see how you approach a problem, not just whether you get the right answer. If you hit a snag, say so and explain how you would debug it.

Clarify before you answer. For open-ended questions like 'how would you analyse this?', ask one clarifying question first. It shows you think before you act, which is a valued trait in analysts working with ambiguous business problems.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates typically report, Executive Talent Ltd interviewers are looking for a few consistent things across all experience levels.

Technical fluency without over-engineering. You should be comfortable with SQL (joins, aggregations, window functions), at least one of Excel or Python, and a visualisation tool. You do not need to be a data engineer, but you should be able to get your own data independently.

Business context. Analysts who can connect a metric to a business outcome stand out. When you answer a technical question, add one sentence about what that analysis would mean for a decision.

Clear communication. Data Analyst roles at companies like Executive Talent Ltd typically involve working with non-technical stakeholders. Interviewers will probe whether you can explain findings without jargon.

Ownership and curiosity. Candidates who say 'I noticed a pattern and went to investigate' perform better than those who wait to be asked. Show examples of self-directed work.

Accuracy and honesty about your skills. Do not claim tools you have not used. Interviewers will test specifics. It is better to say 'I have used this tool at a basic level and I am building on it' than to overstate and get caught out in the interview.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Start your preparation at least one week before your first round. Here is a practical structure.

Days 1-2: Technical refresh. Revise SQL joins, GROUP BY, window functions (ROW_NUMBER, RANK, LAG), and subqueries. If you use Python, revisit pandas basics. Do at least five practice problems on a free SQL platform.

Days 3-4: Story bank. Write down four to six work stories that cover: solving a business problem with data, handling a data quality issue, disagreeing with a stakeholder, and building or improving a report. Map each to the STAR format before you practise saying them out loud.

Day 5: Company and domain context. Research what industry Executive Talent Ltd operates in and what a Data Analyst in that domain typically measures. Think about which KPIs would matter to their business. This lets you tailor your examples to sound relevant.

Day 6: Mock answers. Say your answers out loud, not just in your head. Time yourself. Behavioural answers should land in two to three minutes. Technical walkthroughs should be clear and methodical, not rushed.

Day 7 (day before): Light review and rest. Re-read your story bank once. Check the job description for any tools mentioned that you have not revised. Sleep well.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Answering without structure. Candidates who ramble through a behavioural question without a clear arc lose the interviewer quickly. Practise STAR until it feels natural before your interview day.

Only describing the analysis, not the impact. Saying 'I built a dashboard' is weak. Saying 'I built a dashboard that the ops team now uses every morning to catch delivery delays early' is strong. Always close with what changed because of your work.

Overclaiming tool experience. If you list a tool on your resume, expect a specific question about it. Be precise about your depth: 'I have used Tableau to build bar charts and basic calculated fields' is more credible than a vague claim of broad experience.

Not asking clarifying questions. Jumping straight into an answer for an open-ended problem looks impulsive. A brief clarifying question shows analytical discipline.

Ignoring the business context. Giving a technically correct answer to a business question without connecting it to a decision or outcome signals that you think like a data technician, not a data analyst.

Forgetting to prepare questions for the interviewer. Candidates who ask nothing at the end of a round leave a weaker impression. Prepare two or three genuine questions about the team's data stack, how analysts collaborate with stakeholders, or what a successful first three months looks like.

Frequently asked

How many rounds does Executive Talent Ltd typically have for a Data Analyst role?

Candidates typically report two to three rounds, starting with an HR screening call, followed by one or two technical or case-based rounds. Some teams add a final discussion with a senior stakeholder or hiring manager. The exact number can vary by team and seniority level, so confirm the process with your recruiter after the first call.

What salary can I expect for a Data Analyst role at Executive Talent Ltd?

Salary depends heavily on your experience level. Market salary bands for Data Analysts in India run from 5-10 LPA at entry level (0-2 years), 10-18 LPA at mid-level (3-5 years), 18-30 LPA at senior level (6-9 years), and 28-45+ LPA at lead level. These are broad market bands and your actual offer will depend on your specific skills, the team, and your negotiation.

Based on knok jobradar data from July 2026, Bangalore leads with 41 openings across the market, followed by Delhi with 22, Mumbai with 19, Hyderabad with 14, Pune with 10, and Chennai with 5. Executive Talent Ltd alone has 176 open Data Analyst roles nationally, suggesting a broad hiring push across multiple locations.

Do I need Python or is SQL enough for a Data Analyst interview?

For most Data Analyst roles, strong SQL is the core requirement and what interviewers test most heavily. Python is a significant plus, especially for data cleaning, automation, and more complex analysis. Candidates typically report that technical rounds focus on SQL, with Python questions more common at mid to senior levels. Know your SQL well, and be honest about your Python depth rather than overclaiming.

How should I prepare for case-based or analytical questions?

Practise the Explain, Interpret, Recommend structure: describe what you see in the data, explain what it might mean for the business, and suggest what to do next. Do not just narrate numbers. Interviewers at companies like Executive Talent Ltd want to see business thinking alongside technical skill. Using real examples from your own work is more convincing than hypothetical answers.

Is it worth applying to all 176 open roles or should I target specific ones?

Focus on roles where the job description closely matches your current skills and experience level. Applying to every listing without tailoring your resume reduces your match quality and can work against you. Prioritise roles where you meet most of the core requirements, and customise your resume for each application to highlight the most relevant experience.
