03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for all behavioural questions. Three worked examples are below, tailored to zluri's B2B SaaS context.

---

Q: How would you make a dense data dashboard easier to scan for a non-technical finance manager?

*Situation:* At my previous role, our product had a single dashboard showing application health, cost breakdowns, and upcoming renewals all at once. Enterprise clients were missing renewal alerts because the screen felt overwhelming.

*Task:* I was asked to redesign the dashboard so that time-sensitive items did not get buried under general information.

*Action:* I ran a round of user interviews with finance leads to understand what they looked for first each morning. Based on that, I introduced a 'Today's Focus' strip at the top surfacing only time-sensitive items. I moved detailed tables below the fold and switched to urgency-based colour coding rather than category-based coding. I also looped in engineering early to flag which changes were costly, so we could phase the rollout.

*Result:* Internal usability test scores improved after the redesign and customer success reported fewer missed-renewal escalations. The 'Today's Focus' strip was later adopted in two other parts of the product.

---

Q: Describe a time you pushed back on a product manager's feature request.

*Situation:* A product manager wanted to add a bulk-action feature for revoking app access across all employees in one click. The request was driven by a vocal enterprise client.

*Task:* My role was to translate that request into a safe, usable design, but I had concerns that a single-click bulk action could cause irreversible damage if triggered by mistake.

*Action:* I put together a short risk analysis showing scenarios where IT admins had accidentally revoked access in competing tools, sourced from publicly available community forums. I proposed a two-step confirmation pattern with a mandatory preview screen listing affected users. I presented both the PM's original spec and my alternative in the same review, framing it as 'here is how we give the client what they asked for while protecting them from support escalations.'

*Result:* The PM agreed to the two-step flow. The feature shipped with the preview screen. Adding confirmation steps for destructive bulk actions is now a commonly cited best practice in enterprise SaaS design, and this became a reference pattern for the team.

---

Q: Tell us about a design that did not perform as expected after shipping.

*Situation:* I redesigned a filter panel for a reporting tool. I was confident the new layout was cleaner and more logical than the old one.

*Task:* After the redesign shipped, I was responsible for monitoring adoption through session recordings and support tickets.

*Action:* Within the first two weeks, support tickets about 'missing filters' spiked. Session recordings showed users looking for filters in the old location out of muscle memory. The new layout was objectively better but we had not communicated the change or provided transitional guidance. I designed a one-time tooltip overlay pointing to the new filter location and coordinated a brief in-app announcement with the marketing team.

*Result:* Support tickets dropped back to baseline within a week of the tooltip going live. I added a 'change communication' step to my personal design checklist, especially for redesigns of existing flows.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Three frameworks cover most questions you will face in a zluri Product Designer interview.

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the standard for all behavioural questions. Keep Situation and Task brief (two to three sentences each). Spend most of your time on Action, because interviewers at product-focused companies want to understand your thinking process, not just the outcome. Always close with a concrete Result, even if it is qualitative.

Problem, Who, Solution, Trade-off works well for design critique and case study questions. Start with: what problem does this product solve and for whom? Then describe your proposed solution. Always name at least one trade-off you consciously made, because senior interviewers at B2B companies look for designers who acknowledge constraints rather than present solutions as perfect.

Jobs to Be Done (JTBD) helps when you are asked about designing for multiple personas, which comes up often at zluri given its multi-stakeholder product. Frame each user type around the job they are hiring the product to do. For example: 'The IT admin is hiring zluri to stop being surprised by shadow IT. The CFO is hiring it to cut SaaS spend without disrupting teams.' This framing signals enterprise product maturity quickly.

For portfolio walkthroughs, use a four-beat structure: the problem, your process (including dead ends), the shipped solution, and what you would do differently now. zluri interviewers reportedly appreciate candour about what did not work.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

zluri interviewers are typically looking for four things in a Product Designer.

B2B design instincts. Consumer product portfolios are not disqualifying, but you need to show you understand enterprise constraints: limited user research access, multiple personas with conflicting needs, admin workflows, and data-dense interfaces. If your portfolio is mostly consumer apps, prepare to explicitly map your skills to B2B contexts during the interview.

Systems thinking. zluri's product is deeply interconnected. An action in one part of the product (like revoking an app licence) has downstream effects in another (spend reports, compliance logs). Interviewers want to see that you think about these ripple effects in your designs, not just the happy path on a single screen.

Collaboration with PM and engineering. Because zluri ships a complex B2B product, design cannot operate in isolation. Expect questions about how you have navigated disagreements, managed handoffs, and influenced roadmap decisions. Candidates who frame design as a team sport tend to do better than those who present it as a solo craft.

Data literacy. You do not need to be an analyst, but you should be comfortable talking about metrics, interpreting session data, and designing for data-heavy interfaces. Showing that you understand the difference between a table that informs and a table that overwhelms is a strong signal in this interview.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Understand the product and the user.

Sign up for a zluri trial or watch their product demo videos. Map out the main user flows: app discovery, licence management, renewal alerts, and spend optimisation. Write down three things that feel unclear or confusing as a first-time user. These observations become the basis of strong, genuine case study answers.

Research the IT admin and finance manager personas. Reading communities like Reddit's r/sysadmin and finance-focused LinkedIn groups gives you real language that enterprise buyers use when talking about SaaS management pain points.

Week 2: Prepare your portfolio and stories.

Select two to three projects from your portfolio that are closest to B2B, data-heavy, or multi-persona design. For each, prepare a five-minute verbal walkthrough using the four-beat structure: problem, process, shipped solution, and what you would change now. If your portfolio is consumer-focused, add a section explicitly mapping your process skills to enterprise contexts.

Write out STAR stories for at least five themes: pushing back on stakeholders, design failure and recovery, research with limited access, cross-functional collaboration, and designing for conflicting user needs.

Week 3: Practise and sharpen.

Do at least two mock portfolio reviews with a peer or on a design community platform. Time yourself: walkthroughs that run over ten minutes are a commonly cited problem in design interview feedback.

Prepare one unsolicited case study, a short redesign of one specific zluri screen you think could be improved. Bring it up only if the interviewer invites open-ended discussion, but having it ready signals genuine product interest.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Bringing only consumer product examples. zluri is a B2B enterprise tool. Interviewers have seen many portfolios with e-commerce and social app redesigns. If that is all you have, bridge the gap explicitly in your words: explain how your process translates to enterprise constraints like admin personas, data tables, and multi-stakeholder sign-off.

Skipping the 'why' behind design decisions. Saying 'I chose a card layout because it looks clean' is a weak answer at a B2B company. Always explain the user behaviour or research insight that drove the decision.

Presenting only final screens. zluri interviewers typically want to see your thinking, not just your Figma output. Include rough wireframes, dead-end explorations, and the version you rejected and why. Showing that you considered alternatives is a stronger signal than a polished final design alone.

Underestimating the take-home assignment. Candidates report that design tasks at B2B SaaS companies are evaluated on problem framing as much as visual execution. Spend as much time writing your rationale as you do designing screens. A well-reasoned average design typically beats a beautiful design with no explanation.

Ignoring the business context. Enterprise SaaS design always has a business angle. If you can tie your design decisions to outcomes like reduced support tickets, faster onboarding, or higher feature adoption, you demonstrate the commercial awareness that B2B companies value in senior designers.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the zluri Product Designer interview typically have?

Candidates typically report a portfolio screening call followed by a take-home design assignment, and then one or two rounds of panel discussions with the product and engineering teams. The process can vary depending on the seniority of the role. Some candidates report a final culture or leadership round for senior positions. Always confirm the exact structure with your recruiter at the start of the process.

What kind of design assignment does zluri give candidates?

Candidates report being given a product problem related to SaaS management, such as designing a specific feature or rethinking an existing user flow. The brief typically asks you to show your process, not just final screens. Spend as much time on your written rationale and the trade-offs you considered as on the visual designs. Time estimates shared by candidates suggest a few hours to a full day of work.

Does zluri expect Figma expertise?

Figma is the industry standard for product design in India and is commonly cited as a baseline requirement at most SaaS companies. zluri product teams are reported to use Figma for design and handoff. Strong proficiency in components, auto-layout, and prototyping is recommended. Tooling alone will not decide the outcome, but being slow in Figma during a live design exercise can hurt your performance.

What salary can I expect as a Product Designer at zluri?

Market data for Product Designers in India shows ranges of 6-12 LPA at entry level, 14-24 LPA at mid level, and 26-40 LPA at senior level. Actual zluri compensation may sit anywhere within or outside these ranges depending on your experience and the specific role. Check Glassdoor and levels.fyi for company-specific reports and recent offer data before negotiating.

Is prior B2B or SaaS experience required for a Product Designer role at zluri?

Prior B2B experience is strongly preferred but candidates report it is not always a hard requirement, especially for mid-level roles where strong design process and thinking can compensate. What matters most is demonstrating that you understand enterprise user needs, multi-persona design, and data-heavy interfaces. If your portfolio is consumer-focused, prepare explicit bridges to B2B contexts in your verbal answers.

How do I stand out in a zluri Product Designer interview?

Arrive with a working knowledge of zluri's product and at least one specific observation about a user flow you found interesting or confusing. Interviewers notice when candidates have actually used or researched the product before the call. Show your thinking process clearly, name the trade-offs you made, and frame your outcomes in terms of user behaviour or business impact rather than aesthetics alone.
