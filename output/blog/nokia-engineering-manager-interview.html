01 Overview

Overview

Nokia has 222 Engineering Manager openings as of July 2026, making it one of the more active hirers in this role right now (source: knok jobradar). Nokia builds network infrastructure, 5G equipment, and cloud-native software for telecom operators worldwide, so Engineering Managers here work at the intersection of deep technology and mission-critical delivery.

Candidates report that the interview process typically runs across multiple rounds, including an initial HR screen, a hiring manager discussion, technical depth conversations with senior engineers or architects, and a leadership round with a director or VP. Some candidates report a case study or presentation round as well, though this varies by team.

Bangalore has the highest concentration of openings (182 roles), followed by Delhi (53), Pune (20), Chennai (20), Hyderabad (16), and Mumbai (12). If you are open to multiple cities, your options increase substantially.

Salary bands for this level, based on knok jobradar data, run 35-60 LPA for Manager roles, 55-90 LPA for Senior Manager roles, and 90-150+ LPA for Director-level positions.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up consistently in Nokia Engineering Manager interviews, based on candidate reports and Nokia's known focus areas.

  1. Nokia is shifting from hardware-centric products to software-defined networking. How have you led a team through a major technology transition?
  2. How do you build and maintain a high-performing engineering team? Walk us through your hiring and onboarding process.
  3. Nokia teams often work across India, Europe, and North America. How do you manage distributed teams and keep everyone aligned?
  4. How do you handle an engineer who is consistently missing deadlines or delivering below expectations?
  5. Nokia products run in live telecom networks where downtime can affect millions of users. How do you build a culture of quality and reliability?
  6. How do you balance technical debt against new feature requests when product managers are pushing for speed?
  7. Nokia has gone through significant organizational changes over the years. Tell us about a time you led a team through restructuring or uncertainty.
  8. How do you measure engineering team health and productivity? What signals do you track?
  9. Nokia works directly with large telecom operators as customers. How do you incorporate customer feedback into your engineering roadmap?
  10. Describe a time you made a significant architectural or build-vs-buy decision. How did you approach it?
  11. How do you keep your team current with fast-moving areas like 5G, open RAN, or cloud-native networking?
  12. Tell us about a time a project you owned went off track. What happened and what did you do?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Nokia is shifting from hardware to software-defined networking. How have you led a team through a major technology transition?

*Situation:* At my previous company, the infrastructure team I managed ran a monolithic C++ codebase that needed to migrate to cloud-native microservices. Most engineers had strong embedded systems backgrounds but limited cloud experience.

*Task:* I had to upskill the team, maintain delivery commitments on existing products, and complete the migration without losing key engineers who felt uncertain about the new direction.

*Action:* I started with one-on-one conversations to understand each person's comfort level and career goals. I then built a parallel track model where a portion of each sprint was ring-fenced for learning and proof-of-concept work. I paired experienced cloud engineers with existing team members and ran weekly 'tech clinic' sessions where anyone could ask questions without judgment. I also set incremental milestones so the team could see progress early rather than waiting for a single big launch.

*Result:* The team successfully migrated three core services and overall deployment frequency improved noticeably. Two engineers who were initially resistant became strong internal advocates and took on mentoring roles. We had no attrition during the transition.

---

Q: How do you handle an engineer who is consistently missing deadlines or delivering below expectations?

*Situation:* I managed a senior engineer who had been at the company for several years. After a team restructure, his output dropped noticeably and two consecutive sprint reviews had incomplete work from his tasks.

*Task:* I needed to address the performance issue without demoralizing someone who had historically been a strong contributor, and I needed to understand whether the root cause was personal, technical, or motivational.

*Action:* I set up a candid one-on-one and asked open questions rather than leading with criticism. It turned out he felt disengaged because his skills were not being used well after the restructure. I worked with him to redesign his role around a technical leadership track he cared about. I set clear short-term goals with weekly check-ins and documented expectations in writing so there was no ambiguity for either of us.

*Result:* Within two months, his delivery was back on track. He later became the go-to person for architectural reviews on the team. The situation reinforced for me that underperformance is often a signal worth investigating, not just a problem to manage away.

---

Q: Tell us about a time a project you owned went off track. What happened and what did you do?

*Situation:* We were building a network monitoring feature for a telecom client with a fixed contract deadline. Three weeks before delivery, a key dependency from an external vendor slipped by two months.

*Task:* I had to protect the client relationship, manage stakeholder expectations honestly, and find a path to deliver something meaningful by the original date.

*Action:* I convened the team immediately to map out which features could be delivered without the vendor component. I personally called the client's technical lead to explain the situation before it escalated through formal channels. I proposed a phased delivery: core monitoring functionality by the original date, and advanced alerting once the vendor dependency arrived. I also negotiated a small scope reduction with the product owner to protect the team from burnout.

*Result:* The client accepted the phased plan. We delivered on time for the first phase and the client later expanded the contract. The client specifically cited the honest, early communication as the reason they trusted us through the delay.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the standard for behavioral questions at Nokia. Every answer needs a clear setup, an explanation of what you personally were responsible for, specific actions you took, and a measurable or observable result. Nokia interviewers typically follow up with 'what would you do differently', so prepare a short reflection for each story before you go in.

The People-Process-Product lens works well for team management questions. When asked how you build teams or improve delivery, structure your answer around: how you develop people, what processes you put in place, and how those connect to product outcomes. This signals that you think at multiple levels, not just execution.

The Stakeholder Map is useful for cross-functional questions. Before answering questions about collaboration or conflict, mentally identify who the stakeholders were (engineering, product, customer, leadership), what each party needed, and how you navigated competing priorities. Nokia values cross-functional coordination highly because their products involve hardware teams, software teams, and customer-facing teams working in parallel.

The Reliability Frame is specific to Nokia's domain. When discussing technical decisions or incidents, tie your answer back to availability, reliability, or customer impact. Phrases like 'we could not afford any downtime because the client was a live network operator' immediately signal that you understand Nokia's operating context and the stakes involved.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Nokia Engineering Manager interviews assess four things above almost everything else.

Technical credibility without being a bottleneck. Interviewers want to see that you can engage meaningfully with architects and senior engineers, ask the right questions, and spot risks. But they also want to know you trust your team and do not create decision-making bottlenecks. They are not hiring a senior individual contributor with a people management side task.

People leadership with evidence. Nokia has a reputation for valuing engineering culture. Expect probing questions about how you hire, develop people, and handle difficult situations. Vague answers like 'I empower my team' will not land. Bring specific examples with real contexts and observable outcomes.

Comfort with ambiguity and change. Nokia has reorganized multiple times over the past decade. Candidates who can show they have led teams through uncertainty, restructuring, or shifting priorities are consistently rated higher. Interviewers at Nokia are often themselves navigating change and want to know you will not freeze when direction is unclear.

Customer and business awareness. Nokia sells to telecom operators, governments, and large enterprises. Engineering Managers here are expected to understand why reliability and SLAs matter commercially. Connecting an engineering decision to a customer outcome or a commercial risk will make you stand out from candidates who only speak in technical terms.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Know the company and domain. Read Nokia's recent product announcements and understand their main business units: Network Infrastructure, Mobile Networks, Cloud and Network Services, and Nokia Technologies. You do not need to be a telecom expert, but you should be able to explain why 5G and cloud-native networking matter to Nokia's business. This also signals genuine interest to the interviewer.

Step 2: Build your story bank. Write out STAR stories covering: a team you built or rebuilt, a project that went wrong, a technology transition, a difficult stakeholder situation, a hiring decision, and a time you said no to a request. Practice each story out loud. Nokia interviews typically include several behavioral questions per round, and candidates report that answers work best when they are concise, roughly two to three minutes each.

Step 3: Practice out loud with a partner. Do mock interviews with a peer or a coach, or record yourself. Pay close attention to whether you say 'I' or 'we': Nokia interviewers note this specifically, and they want to hear what you personally did, not what the team did collectively.

Step 4: Prepare your questions. Before each round, re-read the job description and map your stories to the responsibilities listed. Prepare thoughtful questions for the interviewer, such as asking about the team's biggest current challenge, how success is measured in the first six months, or how the team collaborates with product and design. Strong questions signal operational maturity.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Talking about the team instead of yourself. Nokia interviewers are evaluating you as a leader. Saying 'we decided' or 'the team delivered' without explaining your specific role is one of the most common reasons candidates do not progress. Use 'I' when describing your decisions and actions, and save 'we' for context.

Vague answers on people management. Saying you 'give regular feedback' or 'have an open-door policy' is not enough. Interviewers want to hear how you structured a specific performance conversation, what you actually said to a struggling engineer, or how you ran your one-on-ones. Specificity is what builds credibility in these rounds.

Ignoring Nokia's domain. Candidates who treat this like a generic tech company interview often struggle. Nokia operates in a space where reliability, regulatory compliance, and long-term customer relationships matter deeply. Not connecting your answers to that context makes you sound like a generic manager who could be interviewing anywhere.

Underestimating the technical bar. Nokia Engineering Managers are expected to engage with architecture discussions, understand trade-offs in distributed systems or network protocols, and earn the respect of senior engineers. Going in without brushing up on relevant technical concepts is a mistake candidates often regret.

Asking no questions or weak questions. Asking 'what does success look like?' is fine but thin. Candidates who ask about the team's current technical debt, how incidents are handled, or what the relationship with the product organization looks like signal genuine interest and operational maturity.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many interview rounds does Nokia typically have for Engineering Manager roles?

Candidates report going through multiple rounds for Engineering Manager roles at Nokia, typically including an HR screen, a hiring manager discussion, one or two technical or domain-depth rounds, and a leadership round with a senior leader. Some teams add a case study or presentation round, but this is not universal. The overall process can span several weeks depending on the team and location.

Does Nokia ask coding questions in Engineering Manager interviews?

Candidates report that live coding is not common for Engineering Manager roles at Nokia. However, you may be asked to review a system design, discuss architectural trade-offs, or explain how you would approach a technical problem conceptually. Being rusty on coding syntax is fine, but you should be comfortable discussing distributed systems and technical decision-making at a high level.

What salary can I expect for an Engineering Manager role at Nokia in India?

Based on knok jobradar data, Manager-level roles at Nokia typically range from 35-60 LPA, Senior Manager roles from 55-90 LPA, and Director-level positions from 90-150+ LPA. These figures reflect base salary and do not include bonus or stock components. For additional data points, Glassdoor and levels.fyi have Nokia-specific entries, though keep in mind that sample sizes for India-specific roles may be limited.

Which Nokia office has the most Engineering Manager openings?

Bangalore is the largest hub by far, with 182 of the 975 total Engineering Manager openings tracked by knok jobradar as of July 2026. Delhi has 53 openings, while Pune, Chennai, Hyderabad, and Mumbai each have fewer. If you are flexible on city, Bangalore gives you the most options by a wide margin.

How should I research Nokia before the interview?

Focus on Nokia's main business segments and their publicly stated shift toward software and cloud-native networking. You do not need to memorize financial figures, but understanding why Nokia matters to telecom operators (5G infrastructure, open RAN, private wireless networks) will help you frame your answers in context. Reading the specific job description closely and mapping your past experience to the listed responsibilities is equally important and often overlooked by candidates.

Is Nokia a good company for Engineering Managers in India in 2026?

Nokia has a large and active engineering presence in India, particularly in Bangalore and Delhi, and the 222 open roles on knok jobradar suggest continued investment in the region. Candidates and employees commonly cite Nokia's domain depth and global project exposure as positives. As with any large company, team culture can vary significantly by business unit, so it is worth asking your interviewers specifically about the team you would be joining before making a decision.
