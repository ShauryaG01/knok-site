02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These are the questions candidates commonly report from Strategic Employment Platform Engineer interviews:

  1. Walk us through how you have designed or maintained a CI/CD pipeline end-to-end.
  2. How do you approach infrastructure as code? Which tools have you used and why did you choose them?
  3. Describe a time when a deployment went wrong in production and how you handled it.
  4. How do you ensure observability across distributed services on a platform you manage?
  5. What is your experience with Kubernetes? How have you managed cluster upgrades or scaling events?
  6. How would you design a self-service developer portal for internal engineering teams?
  7. Tell us about a time you improved developer experience or meaningfully reduced time to deploy.
  8. How do you handle secrets management across multiple environments such as dev, staging, and production?
  9. Describe your approach to capacity planning and cost optimisation on cloud infrastructure.
  10. How do you prioritise platform reliability work when product teams are asking for new features at the same time?
  11. What metrics do you track to measure platform health, and how do you decide when to act on them?
  12. How would you handle a situation where a critical platform dependency is deprecated by the vendor?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time when a deployment went wrong in production and how you handled it.

*Situation:* At my previous company, a Kubernetes rollout caused a memory spike that took down a significant portion of our API pods during peak traffic hours.

*Task:* I was the on-call platform engineer and needed to restore service quickly while also identifying the root cause.

*Action:* I immediately triggered a rollback using 'kubectl rollout undo', confirmed pods were returning to a healthy state, then opened a dedicated war room channel with the team. Pulling resource metrics from Prometheus, I found the new service version had accidentally removed memory limits in the Helm chart. I then added a pre-deploy validation step to our CI pipeline that checks resource quotas before any rollout proceeds to production.

*Result:* We restored full service within minutes. The pre-deploy check has since blocked similar misconfigurations before they could reach production.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you improved developer experience or meaningfully reduced time to deploy.

*Situation:* Our engineering team had a manual release process that required developers to raise tickets and wait for the platform team to trigger each deployment.

*Task:* I was asked to build a self-service deployment system so developers could ship to staging independently, without depending on the platform team for every release.

*Action:* I built a GitHub Actions workflow integrated with our internal Slack bot. Developers could type a single slash command in Slack, which triggered the workflow, ran integration tests, and deployed to staging with a full audit trail. I then ran two lunch-and-learn sessions so teams could adopt it confidently and gave them a short runbook for troubleshooting common errors.

*Result:* Time to staging dropped dramatically compared to the previous process. Developer satisfaction in our quarterly survey improved noticeably, and the platform team's ticket queue shrank, freeing us to focus on reliability improvements.

---

Q: How do you prioritise platform reliability work when product teams want new features at the same time?

*Situation:* At one point we had several product teams each requesting new platform integrations, while we also had unresolved reliability incidents carried over from the previous quarter.

*Task:* I needed a framework the whole team agreed on so that prioritisation felt transparent and fair rather than arbitrary.

*Action:* I introduced a lightweight scoring approach based on three factors: user impact (how many engineers or end-users are affected), incident risk (does delaying this increase the chance of an outage), and strategic alignment (does this unlock a key business goal this quarter). I shared the scoring sheet openly with product leads and held a monthly sync to revisit priorities as conditions changed.

*Result:* The team cleared the backlog of reliability work within a few weeks while still delivering most of the requested product integrations on schedule. Product leads reported feeling more included in the prioritisation process rather than simply blocked by the platform team.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For technical 'how do you' questions: Open with the tool or approach you use, then walk through a specific example from your own experience. Interviewers are checking that you can move from theory to practice. Name the actual technologies you have used, such as Terraform, Helm, ArgoCD, Prometheus, or similar, rather than speaking generically about 'infrastructure tooling'.

For behavioural questions: Use the STAR structure: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task brief. Spend most of your time on Action, describing what you specifically did rather than what the team did collectively. End with a concrete Result, something measurable or at least observable, and hedge if you cannot recall exact figures.

For system design questions: Start by clarifying scope and constraints before diving into architecture. Interviewers want to see that you ask the right questions first. Talk through trade-offs out loud rather than jumping straight to a solution. If asked to design a developer portal, for example, address auth, service discovery, secrets, and rollback before proposing a specific stack.

For prioritisation questions: Show that you use a framework rather than gut feel. Reference how you communicate with stakeholders when trade-offs are made. Platform roles at Strategic Employment often sit at the centre of multiple teams, so interviewers pay close attention to how you manage competing demands without becoming a bottleneck.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Hands-on tool depth, not just familiarity. Interviewers typically probe beyond 'I have used Kubernetes' to ask about specific upgrade strategies, RBAC configurations, or how you have handled node pool scaling. Be ready to go deeper on your strongest tools.

Ownership mindset. Platform Engineers here are expected to treat the platform as a product. Interviewers look for candidates who proactively identify reliability gaps, write runbooks, and close the loop with developer teams rather than waiting to be told what to fix.

Clear communication across teams. Because platform work affects every engineering team, interviewers want to see that you can translate infrastructure concerns into business language and build alignment with product managers or non-technical stakeholders.

Reliability thinking. Expect questions about SLOs, error budgets, alerting thresholds, and incident retrospectives. Even if your current company does not use formal SRE practices, be ready to explain how you think about uptime, toil reduction, and what you would change with more resources.

Cost awareness. Cloud cost optimisation comes up frequently. Know from your own experience how you identified waste, what actions you took, and what improved as a result.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Audit your own experience. List every platform or infrastructure project you have worked on. For each one, note the stack, your specific contribution, a challenge you faced, and the outcome. This becomes your answer bank for both technical and behavioural questions.

Week 1 into Week 2: Sharpen your weakest tool area. If you are strong on AWS but light on Kubernetes, spend focused time on cluster management, Helm chart authoring, and ArgoCD or Flux basics. If CI/CD is your gap, build a sample pipeline on GitHub Actions or GitLab CI using a small personal project and push it to a public repo.

Week 2: Practice system design out loud. Pick two design prompts, such as 'design a multi-tenant Kubernetes cluster for a large engineering organisation' or 'build a secrets rotation system', and talk through your architecture with a timer running. Recording yourself and reviewing for clarity and structure helps a great deal.

Week 2 into Week 3: Research Strategic Employment specifically. Check their LinkedIn page, Glassdoor reviews, and any public engineering content for clues about their stack and culture. With 107 open roles currently active, the company is scaling fast, so expect questions about building platform capabilities from scratch or managing rapid growth.

Before the interview: Prepare at least three strong STAR stories covering: a production incident, a developer experience improvement, a cross-team prioritisation challenge, and a technical decision you made that you would approach differently today. Having these ready means you can adapt them to almost any behavioural question that comes up.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Staying too generic on tools. Saying 'I have worked with Terraform' without describing what you built, how you structured modules, or how you handled state management tells interviewers very little. Be specific about your actual work.

Skipping the Result in STAR answers. Many candidates walk through a detailed action and then trail off. Always close the loop: what improved, what you measured, or what feedback you received.

Confusing Platform Engineering with pure DevOps. Strategic Employment Platform Engineer roles typically expect a product mindset toward internal infrastructure, not just tooling and deployment scripts. If your answers never mention developer experience, reliability goals, or stakeholder communication, you may come across as too narrow for the role.

Underestimating the cost and reliability angle. Candidates who cannot speak to cloud cost awareness or SLO thinking often struggle in later rounds. Even rough familiarity with error budgets and cost tagging strategies makes a noticeable difference.

Not asking good questions at the end. Interviewers often judge your engagement by the questions you ask. Asking about their on-call culture, how the platform team measures success, or what the biggest reliability challenge is right now signals genuine interest and product-oriented thinking.

What technical skills matter most for this role?

Based on what candidates report and the nature of Platform Engineering roles, key areas include Kubernetes and container orchestration, infrastructure as code (Terraform or Pulumi), CI/CD pipeline design (GitHub Actions, GitLab CI, or Jenkins), observability tooling (Prometheus, Grafana, or similar), and cloud platforms such as AWS, GCP, or Azure.
