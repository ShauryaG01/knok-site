03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How would you prioritise features for a vulnerability management platform when security engineers and business heads have conflicting needs?

*Situation:* At my previous company, we built an internal risk-tracking tool. Security engineers wanted deep technical filters; the CISO wanted a one-page executive summary.

*Task:* I had to ship a version that satisfied both groups without splitting the team's bandwidth.

*Action:* I ran separate discovery calls with each group to document their core jobs-to-be-done. I then mapped every request to one of three buckets: must-have for compliance sign-off, must-have for analyst workflow, and nice-to-have. I proposed a tiered view, a detailed technical dashboard for analysts and a plain-language summary view for leadership, and validated wireframes with both groups before writing a single ticket.

*Result:* Both groups approved the roadmap. Analysts reduced their manual reporting time and leadership adopted the summary view for weekly reviews, based on feedback collected three months after launch.

---

Q: A critical flaw is found in a product you own, mid-sprint. How do you manage the response?

*Situation:* During a live client demo, a tester found that one of our modules was logging sensitive data in plain text.

*Task:* I had to coordinate a fix without derailing the sprint or alarming the client.

*Action:* I immediately pulled the affected feature from the demo, looped in the engineering lead and security team within the hour, and created a severity-one ticket. I communicated transparently with the client, shared a resolution timeline, and negotiated a two-day scope reduction with the sprint team to make room for the hotfix. I also scheduled a post-incident review to prevent recurrence.

*Result:* The fix shipped within the agreed window. The client appreciated the transparency and extended their contract. The post-incident review produced a pre-release security checklist the team adopted going forward.

---

Q: If Kratikal wanted to launch a lighter product for small businesses, how would you approach discovery?

*Situation:* My previous employer explored an SMB tier after noticing that a large share of inbound leads were small companies who dropped off at the pricing page.

*Task:* I led discovery to understand whether this was a product opportunity or purely a pricing problem.

*Action:* I interviewed a sample of those dropped-off leads, ran a competitor analysis on self-serve security tools, and mapped the jobs a small business owner actually needed versus what our enterprise product provided. I found the core pain was not the price but the complexity of setup and jargon-heavy reports. I recommended a guided scan-and-fix product with plain-language summaries and built a lightweight prototype for user testing.

*Result:* Prototype testing showed strong interest from the SMB segment. The findings were presented to leadership and shaped the product strategy for the following year.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the baseline for behavioural questions. Keep the Situation and Task brief, spend most of your time on the Action, and always close with a measurable or observable Result. Open-ended closings like 'it went well' are weak; close with something concrete.

Why, What, How works well for product strategy questions. Start with why the problem matters (user pain, business opportunity), move to what you would build or change, then explain how you would execute and measure it. This structure prevents candidates from jumping straight to solutions without establishing context.

Prioritisation with ICE or RICE is useful when asked to rank features. ICE stands for Impact, Confidence, and Ease. RICE adds Reach. For Kratikal's context, always factor in security severity as a weighting criterion alongside business value. A low-revenue feature that closes a critical risk gap may outrank a high-revenue feature with no safety implications.

The CXO translation test is a mental check worth mentioning in interviews. Before proposing any feature or metric, ask yourself: can I explain this to a non-technical buyer in two sentences? Kratikal sells to enterprises where the final decision-maker is often not a security practitioner, so this translation skill carries real weight.

Stakeholder mapping is especially relevant here. Kratikal's products typically serve at least three distinct personas: security analysts who run tests, IT managers who act on findings, and business leaders who approve budgets. Naming which user you are designing for, and which one you are selling to, signals B2B product maturity.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Candidates report that Kratikal interviewers look for a consistent set of qualities across rounds.

Security curiosity, not security expertise. You are not expected to be a penetration tester, but interviewers want to see that you have taken time to understand how vulnerability assessments work, what a security audit report contains, and why enterprise buyers care about compliance. Light domain awareness stands out.

Clear communication across technical and non-technical audiences. Kratikal's clients range from security teams to C-suite executives. Interviewers will probe whether you can translate complex findings into plain language without losing accuracy or depth.

Structured thinking under pressure. Product case questions at security companies often involve ambiguous, high-stakes scenarios, such as a breach mid-cycle or a client escalation. Interviewers want to see a calm, logical process rather than a perfect answer.

End-to-end ownership. Kratikal is a growing company and interviewers are looking for PMs who take full ownership rather than handing off problems to adjacent teams. Examples of staying with a problem through delivery, not just defining it, resonate here.

Genuine interest in the mission. Cybersecurity products protect businesses and individuals from real harm. Candidates who connect their work to that purpose tend to leave a stronger impression than those treating this as just another product role on their career ladder.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

First: Understand Kratikal's products and services. Read through their service pages covering VAPT, red teaming, and compliance offerings. Take notes on the terminology they use and the client problems they highlight. This gives you a vocabulary foundation before your first round.

Second: Build your security vocabulary. Learn the basics of how a vulnerability assessment works end to end, what penetration testing reports contain, what common attack types look like, and what enterprise buyers care about in compliance. You do not need deep technical knowledge, but knowing the difference between a scan and a full penetration test signals preparedness.

Third: Line up your STAR stories. Prepare at least four to five examples from past work: one on prioritisation under constraints, one on a difficult stakeholder situation, one on a product that underperformed and what you learned, and one on cross-functional collaboration. Adapt each story to a B2B or security context where possible.

Fourth: Practice product cases with a security lens. Take a standard PM case prompt such as 'design a dashboard for X' and reframe it for a security audience. Practice defining success metrics for products where outcomes blend technical indicators with business results.

Fifth: Prepare smart questions for the panel. Ask about product team structure, how roadmap decisions are made, and what the biggest customer pain point is right now. Thoughtful questions signal engagement and help you evaluate whether the role is a real fit.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating cybersecurity as a black box. Candidates who say 'I will rely on the security team for domain knowledge' without showing any curiosity come across as passive. Even a basic understanding of how a VAPT engagement works signals genuine interest in the role.

Using generic PM frameworks without adapting them. Saying 'I use RICE prioritisation' is fine, but failing to explain how security severity and compliance deadlines factor into your scoring in this specific context shows a lack of situational thinking.

Overcomplicating answers. Security products deal with high-stakes, jargon-heavy problems. Interviewers want to see that you can simplify, not add more complexity. Answers that are hard to follow suggest you will struggle to communicate with non-technical clients.

Ignoring the enterprise sales dynamic. Kratikal sells to businesses, not individual consumers. Candidates who frame all answers around consumer product thinking, such as virality, growth loops, or app ratings, miss the B2B context entirely.

Arriving with no questions. In a specialised domain like cybersecurity, having no questions at the end signals low engagement. Prepare at least two thoughtful questions about the product, the customer segment, or the team's current challenges.

Underselling past impact. Candidates often describe what they did without stating the outcome. Always close your STAR answers with a result, even if qualitative, such as 'the client renewed' or 'the team adopted the process going forward.' Interviewers are looking for evidence of actual impact, not just activity.
