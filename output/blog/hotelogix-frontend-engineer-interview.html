02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions appear frequently in Hotelogix Frontend Engineer interviews based on candidate reports and the nature of the PMS product:

  1. How do you build a room availability grid that updates in real time without a full page reload?
  2. Walk us through how you structure a large React application for maintainability across a team.
  3. How do you handle performance on a dashboard showing hundreds of booking rows?
  4. Describe your experience integrating a frontend with REST APIs, including how you handle errors and loading states.
  5. How do you manage complex multi-step form state, for example in a hotel check-in or reservation flow?
  6. What is your approach to responsive design for screens used by hotel staff on tablets and desktops?
  7. Have you implemented role-based access control on the frontend? Walk us through your approach.
  8. How do you handle timezone and date formatting in a product used across multiple countries?
  9. Describe your approach to writing unit and integration tests for UI components.
  10. What is your experience with TypeScript in a production codebase? How do you type API responses?
  11. How do you approach accessibility in admin-facing tools where end users may not be technically savvy?
  12. How do you conduct a code review, and what signals do you look for in a pull request from a junior developer?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time you improved the load time of a data-heavy page.

*Situation:* At my previous company, a booking history page that loaded many months of reservation data was very slow, causing hotel staff to wait before they could act on their daily reports.

*Task:* I was asked to make the page feel fast without requiring changes to the backend API.

*Action:* I introduced virtual scrolling so only visible rows were rendered in the DOM at any time. I also applied React.memo on row components to prevent unnecessary re-renders, split the chart bundle using lazy loading, and added a skeleton screen so staff could see progress immediately on load.

*Result:* The page became interactive much faster and staff reported noticeably less frustration during testing. The team adopted virtual scrolling as a standard pattern for all list-heavy screens going forward.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you built a feature specifically for non-technical end users.

*Situation:* Hotel property managers at a client needed a daily occupancy and revenue summary, but earlier versions of the dashboard were too data-dense and most managers simply avoided opening it.

*Task:* I was responsible for redesigning and rebuilding the frontend of this reporting view from scratch.

*Action:* I worked with the product and customer-success teams to reduce the screen to the few metrics managers cared about most. I used a charting library for clear visual trends, added plain-language tooltips explaining what each number meant, and made sure the key figures appeared above the fold on a tablet without any scrolling.

*Result:* Feature adoption climbed steeply within two months according to product analytics. It became one of the most-opened screens in the entire dashboard.

---

Q: Describe a time you fixed a tricky bug under production pressure.

*Situation:* A booking widget on a client hotel's website stopped accepting reservations on a Friday evening. The bug appeared only in Safari on iOS, not in Chrome or Firefox.

*Task:* I was the on-call frontend engineer and needed to diagnose and fix it quickly to avoid lost bookings over the weekend.

*Action:* I reproduced the issue using browser dev tools with a Safari user-agent, traced the root cause to a date constructor that parses ISO strings differently in WebKit, replaced it with a reliable date-parsing utility, and deployed a hotfix after a quick review with my team lead.

*Result:* The widget was restored within the hour. I wrote a post-mortem and added a cross-browser date test to the CI suite so the same class of bug could not reach production again.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions. Situation (one or two sentences of context), Task (what you specifically needed to do), Action (the steps YOU took, not the team), Result (a concrete outcome with a metric or visible change). Keep the whole answer under three minutes when spoken aloud.

Explain your thinking for technical questions. State your first instinct, walk through the trade-offs, then land on your choice. Interviewers at product companies like Hotelogix often care more about how you reason than which library you ultimately pick.

Requirements first for architecture questions. Start with the constraints (scale, real-time needs, user type), describe your component or data-flow design, then note what you would change if the constraints shifted. This signals senior-level thinking.

Acknowledge before improving for 'how would you change X' questions. Note what works first, then propose one or two specific changes with clear reasoning. Avoid harsh criticism of existing product decisions since you do not have the full context behind why they were made.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Domain empathy. Hotelogix ships software that hotel staff use under real pressure: check-in queues, overbooking alerts, revenue reports at shift start. Candidates who understand or can quickly absorb the hospitality workflow stand out. Show that you think about the end user (a front-desk agent, not another developer).

React fluency with practical depth. The team works with React and TypeScript. They want to see that you understand hooks, component architecture, and state management at a level where you can make confident trade-off calls, not just follow tutorials.

Comfort with data-heavy UI. PMS products are full of booking grids, calendar views, and reporting charts. Experience building performant tables, pagination, filters, or data visualisations is directly relevant to the work.

Clear communication. Because Hotelogix works with hotel clients across many markets, candidates who can explain technical decisions in plain language do well. Practise describing your choices as if you are talking to a product manager, not another engineer.

Ownership mindset. Stories where you identified a problem independently, drove it to resolution, and followed up afterward are valued over stories where you only completed tasks handed to you.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Core skills audit.

Revise React hooks (useState, useEffect, useCallback, useMemo, useRef) and be ready to explain when and why to use each. Revisit JavaScript fundamentals: closures, event loop, promises, and async/await. Pick one state-management approach (Redux Toolkit or Zustand) and prepare a clear justification for when you would choose it over the other.

Week 2: Domain and product prep.

Sign up for a Hotelogix free trial or watch their product demo to understand what the software actually does. Think about two or three UI challenges you notice (slow tables, complex forms, multi-user data conflicts) and form short opinions on how you would approach them. Read about real-time patterns: polling versus WebSockets versus Server-Sent Events.

Week 3: Practice and mock interviews.

Solve frontend coding exercises daily, focusing on component design, DOM behaviour, and API integration patterns. Do at least two mock interviews out loud, timing your STAR answers. Revisit your past projects and prepare specific, outcome-backed stories for each one.

Before the interview:

Test your setup (camera, mic, code editor) if the round is remote. Prepare two or three informed questions for the interviewer about the team's tech stack, deployment process, or how they handle product feedback from hotel clients. Candidates report that asking specific, relevant questions signals genuine interest in the role.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Giving vague STAR answers. Saying 'we improved performance' is much weaker than describing a measurable before-and-after that you personally drove. Prepare specific, concrete outcomes for every project story before you walk in.

Saying 'we' when you mean 'I'. Interviewers want to know your individual contribution. It is fine to acknowledge the team, but make your specific role explicit. Use 'I' deliberately.

Skipping trade-off discussion on technical questions. Jumping straight to 'I would use Redux' without acknowledging alternatives suggests shallow thinking. Briefly note what you considered and why you chose your answer.

Not asking anything about the product domain. Candidates who ask nothing about how the software is actually used by hotel staff miss a clear opportunity to show domain curiosity, which Hotelogix values highly.

Underselling past projects. Many candidates downplay their own work. If you built something, own it. Describe the business context, the constraints you worked within, and the impact it had.

Ignoring cross-browser and device issues. For a product used by hotel staff on various devices, browsers, and screen sizes, this is not an afterthought. If you have not debugged browser-specific bugs or built for touch interfaces, brush up before the interview.

Frequently asked

What does the Hotelogix Frontend Engineer interview process typically look like?

Candidates typically report two to four rounds. The first is usually a screening call with HR to confirm basics and discuss the role. This is followed by one or two technical rounds covering React, JavaScript, and sometimes a live coding exercise or short take-home assignment. A final round with a hiring manager or team lead typically covers past experience, system design, and cultural fit. Round names and order can vary, so ask your recruiter for the current process.

What tech stack does Hotelogix use on the frontend?

Based on publicly available job descriptions and candidate reports, Hotelogix primarily uses React and TypeScript. The product involves data-heavy interfaces like booking calendars, grid views, and reporting dashboards backed by REST APIs. Specific libraries for state management and testing may vary by team, so ask your interviewer what the current stack looks like in practice.

Do I need hospitality industry experience to get hired?

You do not need prior hotel industry experience to get the role. However, showing genuine curiosity about how the product is used matters a lot. Hotel staff operate under real time pressure during check-ins, booking conflicts, and shift handovers, so understanding those workflows helps you ask better questions and think through UI problems more concretely. Spend a couple of hours with their product demo or free trial before your interview.

What salary can I expect as a Frontend Engineer at Hotelogix?

Hotelogix-specific salary data is thin, so treat these as market benchmarks rather than company guarantees. Across Frontend Engineer roles in India, commonly cited ranges are 5-11 LPA for entry level (0-2 years), 12-22 LPA for mid level (3-5 years), and 24-40 LPA for senior engineers (6-9 years). For Lead and Staff roles, publicly reported figures go up to 38-58+ LPA. Your actual offer will depend on your experience level, the specific role, and negotiation.

Is there a coding assignment or take-home test in the process?

Many candidates report a coding round, either live on a shared editor or as a short take-home task. Common formats involve building a React component, debugging a broken piece of code, or designing a small feature like a booking form or data grid. Practise building small, well-structured components and explaining your design choices aloud as you code. Clarity of reasoning matters as much as getting to a working solution.
