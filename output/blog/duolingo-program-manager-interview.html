01 Overview

Overview

Duolingo, the language-learning app known for its gamified approach and beloved green owl, runs a structured interview process for Technical Program Managers (TPMs). As of July 2026, Duolingo has 77 open roles globally, reflecting its continued expansion. Targeting a TPM role there means preparing for a process that tests both technical depth and your ability to drive cross-functional programs in a fast-moving, consumer-product environment.

TPM roles at Duolingo sit at the intersection of engineering, product, and data. The company is deeply metrics-driven, so expect questions around experiment design, A/B testing, and what 'success' means for a learning product. Candidates report a process that typically includes a recruiter screen, one or two technical or program-management phone screens, and a virtual onsite with multiple panels covering program management, technical judgment, and behavioral competencies.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These are the questions candidates most commonly report from Duolingo TPM interviews:

  1. Tell us about a large, cross-functional program you drove end to end. How did you manage dependencies?
  2. Duolingo relies heavily on A/B experiments. How have you used experimentation to inform a technical decision?
  3. How do you handle a situation where engineering estimates keep slipping and the launch date is fixed?
  4. Describe a time you had to influence a senior engineering leader without direct authority.
  5. Walk us through how you would scope and plan a mobile-platform migration affecting multiple product teams.
  6. How do you prioritize technical debt work against new feature delivery when both have strong advocates?
  7. Duolingo's product changes rapidly. Describe how you keep program plans flexible without losing accountability.
  8. Tell us about a time you caught a risk early that others had missed. What was your process?
  9. How do you communicate technical tradeoffs to a non-technical audience, such as a marketing or content team?
  10. Describe a program where the initial scope was wrong. How did you course-correct?
  11. How would you approach building a new program management process in a team that has little prior structure?
  12. What metrics would you use to measure the health of a large engineering program at a consumer app company?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell us about a large, cross-functional program you drove end to end.

*Situation:* My company was migrating its Android app from a legacy architecture to a modern one. Six teams were involved, including platform, product, QA, and localization.

*Task:* I owned the program plan, tracked interdependencies, and was accountable for the launch date.

*Action:* I set up a shared RAID log (Risks, Assumptions, Issues, Dependencies) reviewed weekly by all tech leads. I created a 'canary criteria' document so teams agreed upfront on what conditions must hold before each phase rolled forward. When the localization team flagged a string-freeze conflict, I negotiated a two-week buffer by pulling in QA automation earlier.

*Result:* The migration launched on schedule across all markets. Post-launch crash rate on Android dropped noticeably per internal dashboards, and the platform team freed up capacity for new features the following quarter.

---

Q: How have you used experimentation to inform a technical decision?

*Situation:* Our notification delivery pipeline had two candidate architectures. One was cheaper but had higher tail latency; the other was costlier but more predictable.

*Task:* I needed to recommend one to the VP of Engineering within six weeks, with data to back it.

*Action:* I worked with the data engineering team to design a shadow-traffic test. We ran both architectures in parallel on a subset of traffic and instrumented latency percentiles, error rates, and cost per notification. I defined success metrics before the test started so we avoided 'moving the goalposts' after seeing results.

*Result:* The data clearly favoured the costlier architecture during peak hours. Leadership approved it based on the experiment, not gut feel. The decision held up through the next two product launches without rework.

---

Q: Describe a time you had to influence a senior engineering leader without direct authority.

*Situation:* A principal engineer on a partner team disagreed with the sequencing I had proposed for a shared infrastructure project. He believed his team's workstream should come first; I believed the reverse order reduced overall risk.

*Task:* I needed to align on sequencing without escalating and without damaging the relationship.

*Action:* Instead of debating opinions, I built a simple dependency map and risk matrix and shared it before our next sync. I asked him to review it and mark where he disagreed. He corrected two assumptions I had wrong, and once I updated those, the revised map actually supported a third sequence neither of us had initially proposed. We both owned the new plan.

*Result:* The project ran with no sequencing rework. The engineer later cited our process as a model in a broader retrospective.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioral questions. Every story needs a concrete Situation, a clear Task (your specific accountability), deliberate Actions (not 'we did'), and a Result with something measurable or observable. Duolingo interviewers are reported to probe for your individual contribution, so avoid saying 'we' without following up with what you specifically owned.

Program clarity framework for TPM design questions. When asked how you would approach a new program, walk through: (1) scope and success criteria, (2) stakeholder map and decision rights, (3) dependency identification, (4) risk log, (5) cadence and communication plan. This shows structured thinking without needing to know the company's internal tools.

Data-before-opinion for technical judgment questions. Duolingo is a heavily data-driven company. When discussing any tradeoff, state what data you would look for first, then what you would recommend if the data were unavailable. This signals both rigor and pragmatism.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Duolingo TPM interviewers, based on candidate reports, look for three core traits.

Technical credibility. You do not need to write production code, but you should understand distributed systems, mobile release cycles, API contracts, and experiment design well enough to challenge engineers and spot hidden risks.

Ownership without authority. TPMs at Duolingo drive programs across teams they do not manage. Interviewers want evidence that you create alignment through clarity and data, not by escalating to a manager every time there is friction.

Comfort with ambiguity in a consumer product context. Duolingo ships fast, runs many concurrent experiments, and pivots based on learning data. Interviewers want to see that you can keep a program on track even when product direction is not fully settled.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know the company and product. Use Duolingo daily in the weeks before your interview. Note the onboarding flow, the streak mechanic, push notifications, and any recent feature changes. Read publicly available engineering blog posts and product announcements. This context will make your examples feel grounded.

Week 2: Map your experience to their themes. List three to five programs you have led. For each, prepare a STAR story covering scope, cross-functional coordination, a risk you caught, and a metric that showed success. Practice saying each story in under three minutes.

Week 3: Technical prep. Review A/B testing concepts: statistical significance, novelty effects, guardrail metrics, and experiment segmentation. Review mobile release pipeline basics if your background is primarily backend. Practice explaining a technical tradeoff to a non-engineer in plain language.

Week 4: Behavioral and situational practice. Do at least four mock interviews with a peer or mentor who will push back with follow-up questions. Prepare a clear answer to 'why Duolingo specifically' that goes beyond 'I love language learning.'

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Vague ownership. Saying 'our team delivered X' without explaining your specific role is the most common rejection reason for TPM candidates. Be explicit: 'I owned the program plan,' 'I facilitated the weekly dependency review,' 'I wrote the risk criteria.'

Ignoring metrics. Duolingo is metrics-obsessed. If your STAR story does not include at least one number or observable outcome, it will feel thin. Use data from your own work; if your company does not share metrics externally, describe the outcome in concrete, observable terms.

Over-relying on process jargon. Saying 'I ran an agile scrum with a Kanban overlay' is less useful than explaining concretely what problem you were solving and how the process choice helped. Show thinking, not vocabulary.

Not preparing questions. Duolingo interviewers typically expect candidates to ask sharp, informed questions. Generic questions like 'what does success look like in this role' are weak. Better: ask about a specific technical program challenge the team faces, based on your research into their product and engineering blog.

Underselling the 'why Duolingo.' The company has a strong culture and product mission. Candidates who cannot articulate a genuine connection to the product or the company's educational goals often lose to equally qualified candidates who can.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Duolingo TPM interview typically have?

Candidates report a process that typically includes a recruiter screen, one or two technical or program-management phone screens, and a virtual onsite with multiple interviewers. The onsite panels typically cover behavioral competencies, technical judgment, and program design. Round structure can vary by team and level, so confirm the format with your recruiter early in the process.

What salary can I expect for a TPM role at Duolingo in India?

Duolingo does not publicly list salary bands for India-based TPM roles. For current benchmarks, check Glassdoor, levels.fyi, or AmbitionBox, where candidates have publicly reported their offers. TPM compensation is commonly cited on these platforms as varying by city, years of experience, and scope of the role.

Is the Duolingo TPM interview more technical or more behavioral?

Based on candidate reports, the process balances both. You should expect at least one conversation focused on how you have managed technical complexity, covering topics like experiment design, dependency management, and architectural tradeoffs. Behavioral questions around influence, risk management, and stakeholder communication are equally important. Neither side alone is sufficient to clear the process.

Where are most TPM jobs in India right now?

Across all companies tracked by knok jobradar as of July 2026, there are 313 TPM openings in India. Bangalore leads with 41 openings, followed by Delhi with 14, Pune with 13, and Hyderabad with 12. Duolingo's specific India office footprint may differ from this overall market picture, so confirm location with your recruiter.

How should I prepare if I come from a non-mobile background?

Review the basics of mobile release pipelines: app store submission timelines, feature flags for gradual rollouts, crash rate monitoring, and A/B testing on mobile surfaces. You do not need deep iOS or Android engineering knowledge, but you should be able to discuss tradeoffs fluently. Frame your existing program management experience around how it transfers to a fast-release, consumer-product environment like Duolingo's.

Does Duolingo use a take-home assignment as part of the TPM interview?

Some candidates report a take-home or structured written exercise as part of the process, while others report it is not included. Formats can change over time and vary by team. Ask your recruiter early whether there is a written component so you can allocate preparation time accordingly.
