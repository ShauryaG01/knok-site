03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a production incident you personally led. What was your debugging process and what did you change?

*Situation:* At my previous company, we ran a payment processing microservice on Kubernetes. One evening, our monitoring dashboards showed severe latency spikes well above our agreed service thresholds, affecting several downstream teams.

*Task:* I was the on-call platform engineer and took full ownership of the incident. My goal was to identify the root cause, restore normal service, and prevent recurrence.

*Action:* I reviewed our alerting dashboards and traced the spike to a specific pod group that had exhausted its CPU limits after a config change pushed earlier that day. I rolled back the config change, which immediately brought latency back to normal. I then added a pre-deployment validation step in our CI pipeline to check resource limit changes against historical usage before any change could reach production.

*Result:* The service stabilized quickly after I took ownership. The new validation step has since blocked multiple similar misconfigurations from reaching production, and I wrote up the incident as a learning resource for the wider team.

---

Q: How have you incorporated compliance requirements into infrastructure work?

*Situation:* My team was migrating staging and production environments to a new cloud setup. A compliance audit was scheduled shortly after the migration target date.

*Task:* I needed to ensure the new infrastructure met our SOC 2 controls around access management, encryption at rest and in transit, and audit logging, without delaying the migration timeline.

*Action:* I created an infrastructure-as-code checklist mapping each Terraform module to the specific control it satisfied. I also added automated policy checks using Open Policy Agent so that any pull request violating a control would fail the pipeline before merge. I worked closely with our security team to produce audit evidence as we built, rather than scrambling at the end.

*Result:* The audit passed with no major findings on the infrastructure side. The OPA policies became a standard part of our platform team's workflow and were later adopted by other engineering teams.

---

Q: How do you decide when to build versus buy a platform component?

*Situation:* Our platform team was evaluating whether to build a custom internal developer portal or adopt an existing open-source solution like Backstage.

*Task:* I was asked to lead the evaluation and present a recommendation to engineering leadership.

*Action:* I mapped out the specific needs: a service catalog, runbook links, CI/CD status, and on-call rotation visibility. I ran a proof-of-concept with Backstage over a couple of weeks, tracked integration effort, and estimated ongoing maintenance cost. I also spoke with engineers at other companies who had built custom portals to understand the long-term overhead.

*Result:* I recommended adopting Backstage with a focused plugin set rather than building from scratch. The decision avoided significant custom development work, and we shipped a working portal within six weeks of the decision.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioral questions: Lead with the Situation (one or two sentences of context), then the Task (what you specifically owned), then Action (what you did, step by step, in first person), then Result (what changed, with a concrete outcome). Keep Situation and Task brief. Most of your answer should cover the Action.

For system design questions: Start by clarifying requirements and constraints before proposing any architecture. Ask about scale, compliance requirements, and team size. In a fintech interview, raise reliability, auditability, and security concerns early rather than waiting to be prompted.

For build-vs-buy questions: Frame your answer around total cost of ownership, team expertise, vendor lock-in risk, and compliance implications. Sardine will likely probe the compliance angle, so address it even if the interviewer does not raise it directly.

For incident and debugging questions: Show a structured approach. State what signal you noticed first, how you isolated the problem, what you changed, how you verified the fix, and what you did to prevent recurrence. Interviewers want to see calm, methodical thinking under pressure.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Sardine is a fintech company where platform failures can have direct compliance or financial consequences. Based on what candidates typically report, interviewers look for a few specific qualities.

Security and compliance awareness: You do not need to be a security engineer, but you should naturally consider access controls, audit trails, and data handling as part of every platform decision. Candidates who treat compliance as someone else's problem tend not to advance.

Operational maturity: Sardine wants engineers who have been on-call, handled real incidents, and improved systems after failure. Specific, honest stories of what went wrong and what you learned carry more weight than polished success narratives.

Developer empathy: Platform teams serve other engineers. Interviewers will probe whether you understand the day-to-day experience of the teams using your platform, not just the infrastructure metrics.

Ownership mindset: With 35 open roles, sardine runs a focused org. They want engineers who pick up problems without waiting to be told, and who follow through from design to production to post-mortem.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Company and domain context

Read sardine's public blog, their approach to fraud and compliance, and any engineering posts they have published. Understand what device intelligence and risk signals mean in their product context. This will help you connect your platform work to business outcomes during the interview rather than speaking in pure infrastructure terms.

Week 2: Technical depth

Revise Kubernetes internals: scheduling, resource limits, RBAC, and cluster upgrade strategies. Be ready to draw a CI/CD pipeline end-to-end and explain each stage. Review your infrastructure-as-code experience, including how you handle drift detection and state management.

Week 3: Compliance and security for fintech

Familiarize yourself with SOC 2 controls at a high level: access management, encryption, audit logging, and change management. You do not need to be an auditor. Being able to name specific controls and describe how you have addressed them in past work will set you apart from candidates who treat security as an afterthought.

Week 4: Mock interviews and story prep

Prepare STAR stories covering: an incident you led, a system you improved significantly, a time you pushed back on another team, a compliance or security decision you owned, a build-vs-buy decision, and a time you improved developer experience. Practice each story out loud until it fits comfortably within a few minutes.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping the 'why' on architecture decisions: Saying 'we used Kubernetes' is not enough. Sardine interviewers typically want to know why you chose it over alternatives and what tradeoffs you accepted.

Treating compliance as someone else's job: In a fintech interview, saying 'the security team handled that' without showing your own involvement signals low ownership. Describe how you contributed to or drove compliance-related decisions directly.

Vague incident stories: Saying 'we had an outage and I helped fix it' will not land. Prepare specific details: what the symptom was, what you ruled out, what the root cause was, and what you changed afterward.

Over-engineering in system design: Sardine is a focused company. Proposing a complex multi-cloud active-active setup for every design scenario can read as impractical. Show that you calibrate complexity to the actual requirement.

Not asking clarifying questions in design rounds: Jumping straight to a solution without asking about scale, team structure, or compliance requirements is a common signal that an engineer does not think in product context.

Underselling reliability work: Platform engineers often do invisible work. Be explicit about impact: reduced deploy times, fewer incidents, faster onboarding. Interviewers cannot infer impact you do not mention.
