01 Overview

Overview

Adani Group is one of India's largest conglomerates, with operations spanning renewable energy, ports, airports, logistics, and data centers. For a Technical Program Manager, this means working at true enterprise scale, coordinating across multiple business units, external vendors, and often government stakeholders. With 264 open roles listed as of July 2026, the group is actively scaling its program management function, particularly in its digital and green energy verticals.

Candidates typically report a multi-round process. Early rounds focus on program management fundamentals and past experience. Later rounds tend to go deeper on strategic thinking, stakeholder management, and handling ambiguity. Some candidates also report a technical discussion where interviewers probe your understanding of engineering concepts, even if you are not writing code yourself.

Adani's scale means they value people who can navigate complexity, manage up and down simultaneously, and keep large programs on track even when the ground shifts beneath them. Coming prepared with stories that show both technical credibility and executive presence will give you a strong edge.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions are drawn from patterns candidates commonly report for senior technical program management interviews at large Indian conglomerates like Adani Group.

  1. Walk us through a large program you managed end-to-end. How did you track milestones, manage dependencies, and communicate status to leadership?
  2. Adani operates across energy, ports, airports, and data centers. How would you prioritize competing programs from different business units when resources are limited?
  3. Tell us about a time a critical milestone slipped. How did you identify the risk, what did you do, and what was the outcome?
  4. Describe a situation where you had to push back on a senior stakeholder. How did you frame the conversation and what happened?
  5. How do you manage a program where technical requirements are unclear or changing frequently?
  6. Adani is investing heavily in renewable energy and digital infrastructure. How would you approach a greenfield program in a domain where you have limited prior experience?
  7. Tell us about a time you identified a risk that others had missed. How did you surface it and what was the result?
  8. How do you measure program health? What signals or metrics do you track on a week-to-week basis?
  9. Describe a situation where you coordinated between engineering teams, third-party vendors, and business leaders who had conflicting priorities.
  10. How do you handle scope creep in a long-running program without damaging relationships with the teams requesting changes?
  11. Tell us about a time you had to build a program from scratch with no existing process or playbook.
  12. What does success look like for a Technical Program Manager at the end of a major program? How do you define and measure it?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell us about a time a critical milestone slipped. What did you do?

*Situation:* I was running a data platform migration for a fintech company. Two weeks before a major go-live, we discovered that the data reconciliation layer had an architectural gap that had not been caught in earlier reviews.

*Task:* I needed to contain the damage, reset stakeholder expectations quickly, and build a credible recovery plan without losing the confidence of the business team or the engineering leads.

*Action:* I convened a recovery session with the engineering and QA leads within two days to scope the actual gap, not the feared worst case. I separated deliverables into two tiers: features that could still go live on schedule and those that needed more time. I then presented this tiered plan to the business sponsor with clear trade-offs, rather than a blanket delay. I escalated blockers promptly rather than waiting for the weekly standup, and I set up a daily check-in for the final stretch.

*Result:* We launched tier-one features on the original date, which preserved business confidence. The remaining features went live two weeks later with no further issues. The business sponsor later cited the transparency and structured communication as a positive, despite the initial slip.

---

Q: Describe a situation where you had to push back on a senior stakeholder.

*Situation:* A VP asked my team to absorb a large new feature into an infrastructure program already in its final testing phase. The feature was legitimate but its scope would have required re-architecting a core module.

*Task:* I needed to protect the program timeline while keeping the relationship intact and showing I understood the business need.

*Action:* I requested a short meeting to walk through the impact analysis rather than simply declining over email. I showed the VP a dependency map and explained that adding the feature at that stage would push the program out by nearly a month and introduce regression risk. I proposed logging the feature as the first item on the next program's backlog, and offered to help draft the requirements so momentum would not be lost.

*Result:* The VP agreed to defer the feature. It became the anchor feature of the next release, and the original program launched on schedule. The VP later mentioned in a retrospective that the impact analysis was exactly what the conversation needed.

---

Q: How do you approach a greenfield technical program in a domain you are new to?

*Situation:* I was asked to lead delivery of an IoT-based asset tracking program for a logistics client. I had a strong program management background but limited IoT experience.

*Task:* I needed to build credibility quickly, identify the right technical advisors, and set up a program structure that would hold even as I was still learning the domain.

*Action:* I spent the first two weeks doing structured discovery: reading internal docs, interviewing the three most senior engineers on the team, and mapping out what I did not yet understand. I made a deliberate choice to separate my role as program driver from my role as technical learner, so engineers did not feel I was slowing them down. I set up a lightweight technical review process where leads flagged risks to me in plain language, and I translated those into program-level actions.

*Result:* The program delivered its pilot phase on schedule. More importantly, the engineering team reported in the retrospective that they felt more supported than in previous programs because risks were acted on quickly rather than sitting in a log.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the baseline format Adani interviewers expect for behavioral questions. Keep each component tight: one or two sentences for Situation and Task, the bulk of your time on Action, and a concrete Result with a real outcome.

For program health and metrics questions, use a simple three-layer structure: (1) delivery signals (milestones, blockers, velocity), (2) quality signals (defect rates, rework, test coverage), and (3) stakeholder signals (alignment, escalation frequency, confidence). This shows you think about health beyond just 'are we on schedule.'

For prioritization questions, candidates report that a clear trade-off framework lands well. Name the criteria you use (business impact, technical risk, dependency order, resource availability), show how you weight them for the specific context, and explain what you would deprioritize and why. Adani spans many business units, so showing you can reason across competing priorities without needing a single authority to decide for you is valuable.

For stakeholder conflict questions, frame your answer around interests rather than positions. Show that you diagnosed why each party wanted what they wanted, found the shared interest underneath, and built a solution around that. This signals maturity that goes beyond 'I facilitated a meeting.'

For 'what does success look like' questions, go beyond 'on time and on budget.' Mention program outcomes (what the business can now do that it could not before), team outcomes (did the team build capability or burn out), and process outcomes (what would you do differently next time). This shows strategic thinking, not just execution focus.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Adani Group operates at a scale that demands program managers who are equally comfortable in a board-level conversation and a detailed engineering war room. Interviewers are typically looking for a few things.

Enterprise-scale thinking. Can you manage programs that span multiple teams, cities, vendors, and business units simultaneously? Stories from small startups can work, but you need to show you understand how complexity grows non-linearly at scale.

Technical credibility without over-reach. You do not need to write code, but you must show you can read a system architecture diagram, understand why an engineering estimate is high or low, and spot when a technical assumption is risky. Candidates who cannot engage at this level typically struggle in later rounds.

Structured communication. Adani's leadership layers are deep. Interviewers want evidence that you can take complex program status and translate it clearly for audiences at every level, from engineers to group-level executives.

Proactive risk management. Rather than reacting when things go wrong, the best TPMs at this scale are expected to find risks early and resolve them before they become escalations. Surface examples where you caught something others missed.

Ownership and composure under pressure. Programs at Adani's scale will hit turbulence. Show that you stay rational, keep the team focused, and do not play the blame game when things go sideways.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week one: ground yourself in Adani's context.
Read the Adani Group annual report and any recent news about their infrastructure, green energy, and digital initiatives. Identify two or three programs they have publicly announced. Be ready to discuss how a TPM would approach those specific challenges. This preparation signals genuine interest, not generic job seeking.

Week one (parallel): map your story bank.
List every program you have led or contributed to significantly. For each one, note the scale (teams, timeline, budget range if publicly known), the biggest risk you managed, and the outcome. Aim for at least six distinct stories so you are not recycling the same example across multiple questions.

Week two: practice out loud.
Recording yourself answering questions is uncomfortable but effective. Play back the recording and check: Did you get to the action within the first minute? Did you give a concrete result? Did you use any filler or jargon that an executive would find vague? Practice the twelve questions listed above at minimum.

Before each round: prepare three questions for the interviewer.
Ask about the current state of programs in the team, what a successful first quarter looks like for someone in this role, and what the biggest coordination challenge is across business units. These questions show you are already thinking like a TPM, not just a candidate.

On the day: carry a one-page summary of your top three programs with timelines and outcomes. Interviewers at this level appreciate candidates who can reference specifics without fumbling.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Staying too high-level on actions. Many candidates describe what their team did rather than what they personally drove. Interviewers are assessing your judgment and decisions. Use 'I' when describing your choices, even within a team context.

Treating Adani like a software product company. Their programs often involve physical infrastructure, government coordination, and timelines that span years. Framing all your answers around sprint cycles or agile rituals without acknowledging this context can signal a mismatch.

Skipping the 'so what.' Candidates frequently end answers at the action step and forget the result. Always close with a concrete outcome: what changed, what was delivered, what the stakeholder said, or what the team learned.

Answering prioritization questions without trade-offs. Saying 'I would prioritize based on business impact' is incomplete. Show what you would deprioritize and why, and how you communicated that decision to the team or stakeholder who lost out.

Vague risk management stories. Saying 'I proactively managed risks' without explaining how you identified a specific risk, what you did, and what would have happened if you had not caught it is a missed opportunity. Interviewers at this level want specifics.

Not asking questions. Candidates who end the interview with no questions signal low curiosity or low preparation. Asking about the team's current challenges and what the role's first quarter looks like shows you are serious and already thinking about how to succeed.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Adani Group TPM interview typically have?

Candidates typically report three to five rounds, though the exact structure varies by team and seniority level. Early rounds tend to cover past experience and program management fundamentals. Later rounds often involve senior stakeholders from the business or group leadership. Some candidates also report a technical design discussion, particularly for roles tied to digital or infrastructure programs.

Is there a coding round for a Technical Program Manager role at Adani?

Most candidates report no live coding requirement for TPM roles. However, interviewers do assess technical depth through system design discussions and questions about how you evaluate engineering estimates or architectural trade-offs. You should be able to explain concepts like API design, data pipelines, or infrastructure scaling in plain terms, even if you are not expected to write code.

What salary range can I expect for a TPM at Adani Group?

Adani Group does not publicly publish salary bands for most roles, and the range varies significantly by the specific vertical (energy, airports, data centers) and level of seniority. For a sense of market benchmarks, Glassdoor and levels.fyi list commonly cited ranges for senior TPM roles at large Indian conglomerates. It is worth researching these before your offer discussion so you can negotiate from an informed position.

How important is domain knowledge in energy or infrastructure for a TPM role at Adani?

Strong program management fundamentals matter more than domain expertise, particularly at the entry-to-mid TPM level. Adani values people who can get up to speed quickly in a new domain. That said, showing genuine curiosity about the energy transition or infrastructure challenges, and doing some research before your interview, will differentiate you from candidates who treat it as a generic TPM role.

How should I handle questions about programs I ran that did not go well?

Be honest and structured. Adani interviewers, like most at this level, are less interested in perfect track records and more interested in how you think and respond under pressure. Use STAR format, focus the bulk of your answer on what you did to recover or learn, and close with what you would do differently. Candidates who try to spin failures into disguised successes come across as less credible than those who own the situation directly.
