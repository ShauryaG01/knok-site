01 Overview

Overview

GreyOrange builds AI-powered robots and warehouse automation software used in fulfillment centers across Asia, Europe, and North America. Their product lineup includes autonomous mobile robots (AMRs), fleet management systems, and the GreyMatter software platform that coordinates hundreds of bots in real time.

An Engineering Manager at GreyOrange typically leads teams working on embedded systems, robotics middleware, cloud-based orchestration, or computer vision, depending on the business unit. The role sits at the intersection of deep technology and high-stakes production environments, because their robots run live in warehouses where downtime directly affects client SLAs.

As of July 2026, GreyOrange has 63 open roles on knok jobradar. Across India, the broader Engineering Manager market shows 975 openings, with Bangalore leading at 182 positions, followed by Delhi (53), Pune (20), Chennai (20), Hyderabad (16), and Mumbai (12).

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

GreyOrange interviews for Engineering Manager roles focus on three themes: technical credibility in complex systems, people leadership in a fast-moving environment, and the ability to handle real-world production pressure. Here are the questions candidates report most often.

  1. Walk us through your most complex engineering project. How did you lead the team through it?
  2. GreyOrange's robots run in live warehouses. How do you ensure your team ships reliable, production-grade software under tight timelines?
  3. Describe a time your team had to deal with a critical production failure. What was your role in the response?
  4. How do you manage engineers who are more technically skilled than you in certain areas?
  5. Tell us about a time you had to make a build-versus-buy decision on a key component. How did you frame the trade-off?
  6. How do you set technical direction when the domain, such as robotics or AI, is evolving rapidly?
  7. Describe how you balance technical debt against new feature work when stakeholders are pushing for speed.
  8. How do you handle a situation where your engineers disagree with a product or business decision?
  9. Tell us about your experience hiring or growing engineers. What do you look for, and how have you built strong teams?
  10. GreyOrange works with global clients. How have you managed engineering in cross-geography or cross-functional setups?
  11. How do you measure engineering team performance beyond delivery velocity?
  12. How would you onboard a senior engineer onto a large, unfamiliar codebase in a domain like robotics or embedded systems?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for all behavioral questions. Each answer should run two to three minutes when spoken aloud. Always close with a concrete result, not a vague 'it went well.'

Q: Describe a time your team faced a critical production failure. What did you do?

*Situation:* Our fleet management service crashed in a live warehouse during a peak dispatch window, taking down robot coordination for a major e-commerce client.

*Task:* I needed to restore service quickly, keep the client informed, and make sure we understood the root cause so this would not happen again.

*Action:* I immediately pulled in our two strongest backend engineers, set up a live war room, and personally handled all client and leadership communication so the engineers could focus. We rolled back to the previous stable build within the hour. I then led a post-mortem the next morning, identified a race condition in our state synchronization logic, and worked with the team to add circuit breakers and stronger integration test coverage.

*Result:* The client acknowledged our response time and the quality of the post-mortem write-up. We shipped the fix in the next sprint, and that class of failure has not recurred since.

---

Q: How do you set technical direction when the domain is evolving fast?

*Situation:* My team was asked to add a computer vision layer to our picking robot. None of us had shipped CV in production before.

*Task:* I had to help the team build confidence in an unfamiliar area without stalling our existing roadmap.

*Action:* I ran a structured two-week spike: one engineer owned a proof of concept, I brought in an external ML consultant for two focused sessions to pressure-test our architecture, and I set clear criteria for what 'good enough to build on' looked like. We documented all assumptions as we went so decisions had an audit trail.

*Result:* We shipped a working CV prototype on schedule. The spike surfaced a model architecture choice that would have created significant retraining costs later. Catching it early saved us from a painful rework mid-project.

---

Q: Tell us about managing engineers who disagreed with a business decision.

*Situation:* Leadership asked us to cut a planned refactor from the roadmap to ship a client-requested feature faster. Two senior engineers felt this was the wrong call.

*Task:* I had to balance respecting their technical judgment with executing on a business commitment I had already made.

*Action:* I held a team discussion where I explained the business context honestly, including the client contract terms driving the decision. I also asked both engineers to co-author a one-page technical risk brief that we shared with the product director, so their concerns were formally on record. We agreed to schedule the refactor in the following quarter, and I personally made sure it stayed on the roadmap.

*Result:* The feature shipped on time. The refactor happened as committed. Both engineers told me later that being heard and having the process documented made a real difference to how they felt about the final decision.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the primary format for behavioral questions. Every answer needs a concrete result, even if qualitative, such as improved team retention, a renewed client contract, or a process that prevented a repeat incident. Avoid vague endings.

For technical questions, structure your answer as: context (what the system was and why it mattered), trade-offs you considered, the decision you made and why, and what you would do differently today. This shows engineering judgment, not just recall.

For 'how do you handle X' questions covering conflict, pressure, or ambiguity, use a three-part structure: your default principle, a real example, and what it taught you. Interviewers at product-driven companies like GreyOrange check whether your instincts match their culture before they evaluate whether your stories are polished.

For system design at the EM level, you will typically discuss architecture at a high level rather than whiteboard code. Focus on trade-offs: consistency vs. availability, latency vs. throughput, build vs. buy. In the robotics domain, highlight any experience with real-time constraints, edge compute, or hardware-in-the-loop testing.

A useful self-check before any answer: can you name the specific people involved, the system or product, and a real outcome? If not, the answer is probably too abstract to be convincing.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

GreyOrange is a product company solving hard real-time problems in physical environments. Interviewers are generally looking for four things.

Technical credibility without needing to write production code. You do not need to code as an EM, but you do need to understand your engineers' choices well enough to push back, unblock, and make trade-off calls. Show that you can engage meaningfully in a design review, ask the right questions, and spot an over-engineered solution.

Comfort with production pressure. Robots failing in a warehouse is not an abstract incident. Interviewers want to see that you stay calm, communicate clearly to clients and stakeholders, and drive to root cause rather than just patching symptoms.

People leadership with specifics. Saying 'I care about my team' is not enough. Have concrete stories about how you hired, developed, or course-corrected engineers. GreyOrange teams are often small and high-impact, so each person's growth matters.

Cross-functional fluency. Their EMs typically work closely with product managers, hardware engineers, and client-facing teams. They want to see you can operate across boundaries without waiting for direction from above.

Candidates also report that GreyOrange values ownership and intellectual honesty. Saying 'I got that wrong and here is what I changed' lands better than a perfectly polished story with no self-reflection.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know the company and role deeply.
Read GreyOrange's publicly available content about their robotics platform and GreyMatter software. Search for interviews or talks by their engineering leaders to understand how they think about reliability, scalability, and team culture. Map each requirement in the job description to a specific story from your own experience.

Week 2: Build your story bank.
List the six to eight most significant projects you have led. For each one, write out the STAR structure, your specific contribution as a manager rather than as an individual contributor, and a concrete result. Prioritize stories covering production incidents, technical decisions under uncertainty, team growth, and cross-functional work, since these are the most common themes at GreyOrange.

Week 3: Practice out loud.
Behavioral answers that sound good in your head often run long or lose structure when spoken. Practice with a peer or record yourself. Aim for two to three minutes per answer. Do at least two mock system-design conversations focused on distributed systems, real-time constraints, or high-availability platforms.

Before each round:
Prepare two or three thoughtful questions for your interviewer. Questions about how engineering decisions get made, what the team's biggest current technical challenge is, or how success is measured in the first six months show genuine interest and seniority. Avoid questions easily answered by the company website.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Telling IC stories instead of EM stories. The most common mistake is describing what 'we built' or what 'I coded' rather than how you led the team, made decisions, and removed blockers. Interviewers at the EM level want to hear about judgment and influence, not personal technical execution.

Vague results. Ending a STAR answer with 'the project was successful' or 'the team was happy' wastes the strongest part of the format. Anchor your results to something specific: a client outcome, a timeline, a process change, or a system improvement. Even 'the client renewed the contract' is more convincing than 'it went well.'

Underselling the domain complexity. GreyOrange operates at the edge of hardware and software. If you have experience in embedded systems, real-time software, or high-availability distributed systems, make that explicit. Do not assume interviewers will connect the dots on their own.

Skipping clarifying questions. In system design or case-style questions, jumping straight to an answer without scoping the problem is a red flag at the EM level. Asking good clarifying questions is itself a signal of seniority.

Over-preparing for technical depth and under-preparing for culture fit. Candidates report that the culture and values conversation is taken seriously at GreyOrange. Be ready to talk honestly about failure, about what you have changed your mind on, and about what kind of environment brings out your best work.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the GreyOrange Engineering Manager interview typically have?

Candidates report a process that typically runs four to five rounds, though this varies by team and level. This commonly includes an HR screen, one or two technical or system-design discussions, a behavioral and leadership round, and a final conversation with a senior engineering or business leader. Some candidates also mention a take-home assignment or presentation round, so it is worth clarifying the exact format with your recruiter at the start.

Does GreyOrange expect Engineering Managers to write code during interviews?

Candidates generally report that EM interviews at GreyOrange focus on system design, architecture trade-offs, and leadership discussions rather than live coding. However, you should be comfortable discussing code at a conceptual level and engaging with technical trade-offs in robotics or distributed systems contexts. If you are uncertain, ask your recruiter whether any coding component is part of the process for your specific role.

What salary can an Engineering Manager expect at GreyOrange?

Based on knok jobradar data, Engineering Manager roles in India broadly range from 35-60 LPA, Senior Manager roles from 55-90 LPA, and Director-level roles at 90 LPA and above. GreyOrange is a well-funded deep-tech product company, so their compensation tends to be competitive within the robotics and automation segment. Actual offers depend on your level, years of experience, and the specific team you are joining.

Is prior robotics experience required to interview for an EM role at GreyOrange?

Not necessarily. GreyOrange hires Engineering Managers across multiple domains including cloud infrastructure, data platforms, and enterprise software, not only robotics. However, prior experience in embedded systems, real-time software, or hardware-software integration is a strong differentiator if you are targeting teams that build the robot control stack or fleet management systems. Be honest about your background and focus on transferable skills like managing complex, reliability-critical systems.

How should I prepare for the system design part of the interview?

Focus on distributed systems concepts that apply to GreyOrange's core challenges: real-time state synchronization, event-driven architectures, fault tolerance, and edge compute versus cloud compute trade-offs. You do not need to know their proprietary internals, but you should be able to reason through how you would design a system that coordinates autonomous robots in a warehouse, handles partial failures, and scales without downtime. Interviewers at the EM level evaluate your trade-off reasoning as much as your technical knowledge, so practice explaining your thinking clearly, not just arriving at an answer.

What does GreyOrange value most in an Engineering Manager?

Based on what candidates report, GreyOrange values ownership, intellectual honesty, and the ability to operate across technical and business boundaries. They tend to respond well to candidates who speak plainly about past failures and what they learned, who show genuine care for the engineers on their team, and who understand the stakes of building software that runs in live, physical environments. Being overly corporate or relying on process-heavy frameworks without real technical depth tends not to land well in their culture.
