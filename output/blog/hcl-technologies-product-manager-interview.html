01 Overview

Overview

HCL Technologies is one of India's largest IT services and product companies, with PMs working across enterprise software (HCL Domino, HCL Commerce, HCL BigFix), cloud platforms, and digital transformation projects for global clients. The company actively hires PMs at every level, from Associate PM to Group PM.

As of July 2026, knok jobradar tracked 349 open PM roles at HCL across India, within a broader market of 2009 Product Manager openings nationally. Bangalore leads the market with 271 PM openings, followed by Delhi (177) and Mumbai (56). HCL hires PMs across its product engineering, SaaS, and IT services verticals, so your preparation should reflect the context of the specific role you are applying for.

HCL PM interviews typically run 3-5 rounds covering product thinking, behavioural scenarios, technical depth, and a business case or stakeholder discussion. Candidates report the full process takes 3-6 weeks end to end. Salary bands range from 12-20 LPA for Associate PMs up to 55-90+ LPA for Group and Principal PM positions.

  11. How would you improve one of HCL's existing products, such as HCL Domino or HCL Commerce? Walk me through your thinking.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How would you prioritise a roadmap feature request from a major enterprise client that conflicts with your product vision?

*Situation:* At my previous company, a large BFSI client asked us to build a custom compliance reporting module. It was not on our roadmap and would require several weeks of engineering work.

*Task:* I had to decide whether to build it, delay it, or find a workaround, while keeping the client relationship intact and not derailing the core roadmap.

*Action:* I asked the client team to share the underlying compliance regulation, not just the feature request, to understand the real need. I then checked with three other clients and found two had the same requirement. I ran a RICE score against the current roadmap: the reach was now broader across multiple clients, which justified bumping a lower-impact feature. I negotiated a compressed timeline with engineering by scoping it as a configurable report template rather than a fully custom build.

*Result:* We shipped it on schedule. The original client renewed with an expanded contract, and two other clients adopted the same module, making it a net positive for the roadmap rather than a detour.

---

Q: Describe a time you used data to change a product decision.

*Situation:* We had just launched a new onboarding flow for our B2B product. The team was confident in it because it had tested well in usability sessions.

*Task:* I was monitoring post-launch metrics when I noticed the drop-off rate on step three of onboarding was unusually high compared to publicly reported industry benchmarks. My goal was to identify why and fix it quickly.

*Action:* I pulled session recordings for users who dropped off at that step. The pattern was clear: users were being asked to upload a file before they had seen any value from the product. I ran an A/B test moving the file upload to a later step, after users had completed one core task. I also added a 'skip for now' option with a follow-up reminder email.

*Result:* Onboarding completion improved significantly. Activation (users completing their first meaningful task in the product) also improved, which fed into monthly retention numbers. The insight changed how we designed all future onboarding flows.

---

Q: Tell me about a product launch that did not go as planned.

*Situation:* We were launching a mobile app update that added a real-time collaboration feature. The launch was tied to a client demo at a large industry event.

*Task:* I was the PM responsible for the launch timeline and go-to-market readiness.

*Action:* Shortly before launch, QA flagged a performance issue on Android devices with older OS versions. I had to make a call: delay the launch and the demo, or ship with a known bug affecting a small share of users. I pulled usage data and confirmed that segment was a small minority of active users based on OS version distribution. I escalated to the VP of Product and the client success lead with a clear risk summary. We agreed to ship with an in-app message for affected users that acknowledged the issue and gave a fix timeline. We briefed the client contact before the demo.

*Result:* The demo went ahead successfully. The client appreciated the transparency. We shipped the fix within days. Post-incident, I introduced a launch readiness checklist that included device and OS coverage thresholds as a mandatory sign-off step.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

CIRCLES (for product design questions)
Use this when asked 'design a product for X' or 'how would you improve Y'. Comprehend the situation, Identify the customer, Report the customer needs, Cut through prioritisation, List solutions, Evaluate trade-offs, Summarise your recommendation. Stating the framework name out loud signals structured thinking.

RICE (for prioritisation questions)
Reach (how many users affected), Impact (how much it moves the metric), Confidence (how sure you are), Effort (engineering time required). Divide Reach x Impact x Confidence by Effort. HCL interviewers respond well to candidates who name the framework and apply it, rather than just listing pros and cons.

STAR (for behavioural questions)
Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task brief. Spend most of your time on Action (what you specifically did, not what the team did) and Result (quantify where you can, or reference publicly reported benchmarks if you lack internal numbers).

Jobs-to-be-done (for user needs questions)
Frame user needs as: 'When I am in situation X, I want to do Y, so that I can achieve Z.' This reframes feature requests into outcomes and is particularly useful in HCL's enterprise context, where clients often arrive with a proposed solution rather than a problem statement.

North-star metric framing
When asked to define success for a product, lead with one north-star metric (what the product ultimately optimises for), then support it with a few secondary metrics. Avoid listing every possible metric. HCL interviewers typically look for focus over comprehensiveness.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

HCL PM interviews tend to reward four things above everything else.

Structured thinking. Interviewers want to see how you break down a problem, not just what answer you reach. State your framework before you dive in. Saying 'I am going to use RICE to prioritise this' signals confidence and makes your reasoning easy to follow.

Enterprise and B2B instincts. HCL's core business is enterprise. Questions about stakeholder management, large client relationships, compliance requirements, and long sales cycles come up frequently. If your background is mostly consumer products, prepare at least one strong story about managing multiple decision-makers or complex, competing requirements.

Technical credibility (not deep coding). You do not need to write code, but you should be comfortable discussing APIs, system architecture at a high level, and how engineering estimates work. Candidates report that HCL's technical rounds are more about communication with engineering teams than algorithms.

Data fluency. You do not need to be a data analyst, but you should be able to say which metric you would track, why you chose it, and what a meaningful change would look like. Vague answers like 'I would look at engagement' are regularly flagged as weak by HCL interviewers.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know the company
Read HCL's product pages for HCL Domino, HCL Commerce, and HCL BigFix. Understand where each product sits: enterprise messaging, e-commerce, and IT security respectively. Pick one product and prepare a short 'how I would improve this' answer using the CIRCLES framework.

Week 2: Build your story bank
Write out several STAR stories covering: a product decision you made with data, a stakeholder conflict you resolved, a launch that went wrong, a feature you chose not to build, and a time you worked closely with an engineering team. Practice telling each story concisely and out loud.

Week 3: Practice frameworks out loud
Do one product design question each day using CIRCLES. Do one prioritisation question each day using RICE. Record yourself and listen back. Candidates report the biggest gap is not knowledge but articulation under pressure.

Week 4: Mock interviews and logistics
Do at least two full mock interviews with someone who can give critical feedback. Prepare a few thoughtful questions to ask HCL interviewers: for example, ask about the PM team structure, how roadmap decisions are made, and what a successful first 90 days looks like.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping the 'why' behind a decision. HCL interviewers regularly ask follow-up questions like 'why that metric?' or 'why not build it differently?'. If your answer stops at what you did, expect to be pushed. Always have a clear 'because' ready.

Being too technical or too vague. Both extremes hurt. Going too deep into engineering details makes interviewers wonder if you can communicate with non-technical stakeholders. Being too vague signals a lack of technical credibility. Aim to explain things the way you would to a smart colleague from a different team.

Claiming team wins as personal wins. In STAR answers, be specific about what you personally did. 'We shipped the feature' is weak. 'I negotiated the scope down by proposing a phased rollout' is strong.

Not researching HCL's actual products. HCL is not just an outsourcing company. Candidates who walk in without knowing Domino, Commerce, or BigFix miss easy opportunities to show genuine interest and domain fit.

Giving answers that assume a consumer product context. Most HCL PM roles involve enterprise clients, long procurement cycles, and multi-stakeholder decision-making. Consumer product examples are fine, but always bridge them to an enterprise equivalent. Say: 'In an enterprise context, I would adapt this by...'

Asking no questions at the end. Candidates report that HCL interviewers notice when someone has no questions prepared. Thoughtful questions about the team, the roadmap, and what success looks like in the role signal genuine interest.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the HCL PM interview typically have?

Candidates report the HCL PM interview process typically involves 3-5 rounds. These commonly include an HR or recruiter screen, one or two product or case rounds, a technical or cross-functional round, and a final round with a senior leader. The exact structure can vary by team and level, so it is worth asking your recruiter about the process at the very start.

Does HCL ask product design or case questions, or is it mostly behavioural?

Most candidates report a mix of both. Early rounds tend to be behavioural (STAR-format questions about past experience), while middle rounds often include a product case such as designing a feature, improving an existing HCL product, or prioritising a backlog. Technical understanding of HCL's product areas can come up in any round, so prepare for both types from day one.

What salary should I expect for a PM role at HCL?

Salary depends on level and experience. Based on market data, Associate PM roles range from 12-20 LPA, mid-level PM roles for candidates with 3-6 years of experience are in the 24-40 LPA range, and Senior PM roles range from 40-60 LPA. Group or Principal PM positions can go to 55-90+ LPA. Actual offers vary by negotiation, team, and location, so treat these as reference ranges rather than guarantees.

Is coding or a technical test part of the HCL PM interview?

Typically, no. HCL PM interviews do not include a coding test. However, candidates report that interviewers probe for technical understanding, particularly around APIs, system design at a high level, and how you communicate requirements to engineering teams. Being able to discuss technical trade-offs clearly without writing code is the standard expectation.

How long does the full HCL PM hiring process take?

Candidates report the process typically takes 3-6 weeks from the first recruiter call to an offer, though this can vary significantly. Enterprise hiring at large IT companies can take longer if multiple internal approvals are needed. Follow up with your recruiter after each round if you have not heard back within a week, as delays are common and rarely a signal of rejection.

What is the best way to stand out in an HCL PM interview?

Show genuine knowledge of HCL's products (Domino, Commerce, BigFix) and demonstrate that you understand the enterprise context of the role. Use structured frameworks like RICE and CIRCLES out loud during your answers. Bring specific examples from your past work and connect your experience to HCL's core business: IT services, enterprise clients, and large-scale delivery. Candidates who make this connection consistently report stronger outcomes than those who give generic product answers.
