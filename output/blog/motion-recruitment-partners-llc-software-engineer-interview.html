01 Overview

Overview

Motion Recruitment Partners, LLC is a technology-focused staffing firm that places software engineers with client companies. When you apply to one of their Software Engineer openings, you are not joining Motion directly as a permanent employee in most cases. You are entering a process where Motion first screens you and then presents your profile to a client company that has the actual opening.

Candidates typically report two main stages: a recruiter screen with a Motion consultant covering your tech stack, availability, notice period, and compensation expectations, followed by technical interviews conducted by the end client. The depth and number of technical rounds depends entirely on that client.

Motion Recruitment Partners currently has 3 Software Engineer openings on knok's radar, out of 5,395 Software Engineer roles open across India as of early July 2026. Understanding the two-layer nature of this process is the single most important thing to know before you prepare.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly in Motion Recruitment Partners recruiter screens and in the technical rounds they arrange with clients. Prepare a clear answer for each one before your call.

  1. Walk me through your resume and your most recent project.
  2. What is your primary tech stack, and which part are you most confident in right now?
  3. Are you currently employed? What is your notice period, and can it be shortened or bought out?
  4. What are your salary expectations? Are you open to contract or fixed-term roles as well as full-time?
  5. Describe a time you had to pick up a new technology quickly to deliver a project.
  6. Tell me about a production bug that was hard to diagnose. How did you find and fix it?
  7. How do you handle changing requirements mid-project? Give a specific example.
  8. Have you worked in an Agile or Scrum environment? What did your sprint ceremonies look like?
  9. Describe a situation where you disagreed with a technical decision made by a senior colleague. What did you do?
  10. What kind of team and company culture helps you do your best work?
  11. Have you collaborated with globally distributed or US-based teams? What adjustments did you make for time zones or communication styles?
  12. Where do you see your career in the next two to three years, and how does this role fit that path?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use these as templates and swap in your own project details while keeping the STAR structure.

Q: Describe a time you had to learn a new technology quickly.

*Situation:* My team won a client project that required building a microservices backend in Go. Our entire team had only worked in Java up to that point.

*Task:* I was assigned as the lead developer and had a short runway before the first sprint began.

*Action:* I worked through Go's official documentation and built a small REST API as a practice project. I also arranged pair-programming sessions with a colleague who had done Go side-projects on his own time, and we documented the patterns we agreed on so the rest of the team could onboard faster.

*Result:* We delivered the first sprint on time. Within a few sprints the team was meeting its velocity targets, and the client extended the project scope. The documentation we created became a standard onboarding reference for Go projects at the company.

---

Q: Tell me about a production bug that was hard to find.

*Situation:* We had an intermittent issue where a small fraction of API calls returned a stale cache response even after cache invalidation had been triggered.

*Task:* The bug was affecting real users and I was responsible for triaging and resolving it.

*Action:* I added structured logging around the cache invalidation path, reproduced the failure in a staging environment under load, and traced it to a race condition where two threads were checking cache existence and writing simultaneously.

*Result:* I fixed it by introducing a distributed lock with a short TTL on the write path. Post-deployment monitoring showed the intermittent errors drop to zero. I also wrote a post-mortem that flagged two other places in the codebase with the same pattern, which the team fixed proactively.

---

Q: How do you handle changing requirements mid-project?

*Situation:* Midway through a multi-month engagement, the product team changed the core data model of the application we were building.

*Task:* I needed to assess the impact, communicate the risk to stakeholders, and keep the team on track without a large scope explosion.

*Action:* I put together a quick impact analysis, flagged the timeline risk in the next sprint review, and worked with the product manager to separate changes needed immediately from those that could be phased in.

*Result:* We avoided a large rework by shipping the core model update first and handling edge cases in a follow-on sprint. The delivery slipped by far less than initially feared, and the client appreciated the transparent communication throughout.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For behavioural questions (any 'tell me about a time' prompt): use the STAR method.

  • Situation: Set the context briefly in one or two sentences.
  • Task: What was your specific responsibility?
  • Action: What did YOU do? Use 'I' not 'we.'
  • Result: What was the measurable or observable outcome? If the outcome was mixed, include what you learned.

For tech stack questions ('how strong are you in X?'): give a three-part answer. State your honest level (beginner, working knowledge, or strong), name one concrete project where you used the technology, and mention one gap you are actively closing. Recruiters at staffing firms flag candidates who oversell stack depth, so honesty builds more trust than a perfect answer.

For 'why this role' or career-goal questions: use a Past-Present-Future structure. Where have you been (briefly), where are you now, and where does this specific opportunity take you next? Keep it under two minutes.

For salary questions: name a range, not a single number. Research market data on Glassdoor or levels.fyi for your stack and experience band, then mention what matters to you beyond the number (growth, project type, team quality). This gives the recruiter room to work while showing you have done your homework.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Motion recruiters are evaluated on how well the candidates they present match their client's requirements. They look for a few things beyond technical skill.

Clarity on logistics. Notice period, work authorisation, willingness to relocate or work remotely, and salary floor. Vague answers here create real problems when the recruiter tries to present your profile. Be specific and honest from the start.

Evidence of delivery. Client companies want engineers who ship. Every story you tell should end with a concrete result: something deployed, a metric improved, or a deadline met. 'We explored this approach' is weaker than 'we shipped it and it cut latency significantly.'

Communication that works for a client-facing context. Since you will work with the recruiter's client, interviewers watch for how clearly you explain technical concepts to a non-technical person. If the Motion recruiter can follow your story, the client hiring manager probably can too.

Stability signals. Staffing firms take a reputational risk every time they present a candidate. Frequent short stints without a clear explanation, or visible frustration about past managers, are flags. Be matter-of-fact about any short roles and frame them around what you learned or what the role achieved.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

In the week before your recruiter screen:

Write down three to five project stories in STAR format. Cover at least one story each for: a technical challenge you solved, a situation where you handled changing requirements, and a conflict or disagreement with a colleague. Practice saying them out loud until they feel natural, not rehearsed.

Decide on your salary range. Check Glassdoor or levels.fyi for your stack and experience band, and know your floor before the call.

Review your notice period in your current contract and know whether a buyout is possible and at what cost.

The day before the interview:

Read the job description carefully. Identify the two or three skills mentioned most prominently and make sure you have a project story for each. Update your LinkedIn and have your resume PDF ready to share if asked.

On the day:

For a video call, test your audio and lighting beforehand. For a phone screen, find a quiet space. Have your GitHub or portfolio link ready. Keep a notepad nearby so you can refer back to the interviewer's questions without asking them to repeat.

After each round, send a short follow-up note to your Motion contact confirming your interest and clarifying any points you want to add. Candidates report this small step makes a noticeable difference in how quickly feedback comes back.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating the recruiter screen as informal. It is the gateway to the client interview. The recruiter decides whether to present your profile at all. Come prepared with stories, not just a resume.

Being vague about salary expectations. Saying 'I am flexible' or 'whatever is standard' signals either that you have not done your research or that you are not confident in your own value. Name a range based on your research.

Overselling your tech stack. Motion recruiters present you to technical hiring managers. If you claim strong proficiency in a tool and cannot answer basic questions about it in the client round, both you and the recruiter look bad. Be honest about your current level and mention what you are actively learning.

Not knowing your notice period options. Clients often have a firm start-date requirement. Not knowing whether you can buy out your notice period can disqualify an otherwise strong profile before the technical round even happens.

Focusing only on what the role offers you. Interviewers at client companies want to hear what you bring. Lead with your contributions and let the role's benefits come out through your questions at the end.

Skipping the follow-up. Candidates report that a short, professional note after each round keeps you top of mind and signals genuine interest. It takes only a few minutes and most candidates skip it entirely.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does a Motion Recruitment Partners Software Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates typically report two to three rounds in total. The first is a recruiter screen with a Motion consultant covering logistics, your tech stack, and salary expectations. After that, the number of client rounds varies by company, but one to two technical interviews is the most common pattern candidates describe. Some clients also add a take-home coding assignment.

Is the job a direct hire or a contract placement?

Motion Recruitment Partners places candidates in both full-time and contract roles depending on what the client needs. The recruiter will clarify the engagement type in the first screen. If this distinction matters to you, ask directly at the start of the call so you do not invest time in a placement that does not fit your situation.

What salary can I expect for a Software Engineer role placed through Motion?

It depends on the client and your experience level. Based on knok's job data, salary bands for Software Engineers in India run from 6-12 LPA at entry level (0-2 years) up to 28-45 LPA for senior engineers (6-9 years). For roles placed with US-based clients, the compensation structure may differ from these bands. Always confirm the full package details with your Motion recruiter before accepting any offer.

How long does the process take from application to offer?

Candidates report that the timeline varies significantly because it depends on the client's urgency. A fast-moving client can go from recruiter screen to offer in one to two weeks, while others take a month or more. Ask your Motion recruiter about the client's expected timeline during the first call so you can plan your other interviews accordingly.

What tech stacks does Motion Recruitment Partners typically hire for?

Motion works across a wide range of client companies, so the tech stacks in their openings vary considerably. Java, Python, JavaScript frameworks, cloud platforms, and data engineering tools all appear in their listings. Review each specific job description carefully and focus your preparation on the skills it calls out most prominently.

Can I negotiate the offer that comes through a staffing firm?

Yes, and candidates report that negotiating is both expected and worthwhile. Your Motion recruiter acts as the go-between and has an incentive to close at a number that works for both sides. Have your floor ready and be clear about what would make you say yes immediately versus what would need more thought. Polite, specific asks tend to get better responses than open-ended requests.
