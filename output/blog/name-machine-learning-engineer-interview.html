02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

The questions below are drawn from commonly reported interview experiences for ML Engineer roles at Indian product companies and global tech firms hiring in India in 2026.

  1. Walk me through a machine learning project you shipped to production. What were the main challenges?
  1. Explain the bias-variance tradeoff. Give an example from a real project where you had to manage it.
  1. How would you detect and handle data drift in a model that is serving live traffic?
  1. Implement gradient descent from scratch in Python. What happens when the learning rate is too high or too low?
  1. Design a recommendation system for an e-commerce platform at scale. Walk through the data source, model choice, and serving layer.
  1. Your dataset has a severe class imbalance, with the positive class making up a very small fraction of total samples. How do you handle it?
  1. How do you decide between a simple model like logistic regression and a complex one like a neural network for a new problem?
  1. Explain how the attention mechanism in transformers works. How does it differ from how RNNs process sequences?
  1. How would you set up an A/B test to evaluate whether a new ranking model outperforms the one currently in production?
  1. Your model has strong offline metrics but performs poorly after deployment. Walk me through how you would diagnose this.
  1. What metrics do you track to monitor a deployed ML model? How do you decide when to retrain?
  1. Write a function to compute precision, recall, and F1 score from scratch without using any library.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Three STAR-format sample answers. Use these as structure, not as scripts. Replace the details with your own project experience.

---

Q: Tell me about an ML project you shipped to production.

*Situation:* My team's fraud detection model was flagging too many legitimate transactions as suspicious. Customer support tickets were climbing and some users were getting locked out of their accounts.

*Task:* I was responsible for improving the model's precision without hurting recall on actual fraud cases, since missing real fraud was not acceptable.

*Action:* I audited the training data and found a significant class imbalance. I applied SMOTE to oversample the minority class, switched to a gradient boosted model, and tuned the decision threshold using the precision-recall curve rather than defaulting to a standard cutoff. I also engineered new features around transaction velocity and device fingerprinting.

*Result:* False positives dropped substantially after we validated the model in a shadow deployment period. Support tickets related to false blocks fell noticeably in the weeks after launch. I also set up drift monitoring so the team would be alerted if feature distributions shifted.

---

Q: Describe a time your model worked well in evaluation but failed in production.

*Situation:* I built a content ranking model for an internal search tool. Offline evaluation metrics looked strong, but after deployment, user engagement on search results dropped compared to the previous rule-based system.

*Task:* My job was to diagnose the gap and fix it quickly, since reverting to the old system was also not ideal.

*Action:* I added server-side logging to capture real user click patterns. After analysis, I found the training set had a selection bias: it only included sessions where users clicked something, so the model had never learned from abandoned or zero-result queries. I retrained with a dataset that included those negative signals and ran a controlled rollout.

*Result:* Engagement recovered within the first week after the corrected model went live. I documented this as a case study internally, and the team now audits training data for selection bias before any model goes to production.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you disagreed with a stakeholder about a technical decision.

*Situation:* A product manager wanted to use a rule-based classifier for a content moderation task because it was easier to explain to the legal and compliance team. I believed a gradient boosted model would be meaningfully more accurate.

*Task:* I had to either provide evidence strong enough to change the PM's mind or find a solution that satisfied both performance and explainability requirements.

*Action:* Instead of pushing back in the meeting, I ran both approaches on a held-out test set and prepared a side-by-side comparison focused on the metrics the PM cared about. I also researched explainability tooling and showed that SHAP values could make the complex model auditable enough for the compliance team.

*Result:* The PM agreed to a pilot of the gradient boosted model with SHAP explanations attached. It went to production a few months later and is still running.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Three frameworks that help structure answers in ML Engineer interviews.

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) works best for behavioral questions and project walkthroughs. Lead with the business context, not just the technical setup. Spend most of your time on the Action section and make sure it describes what you personally did. Always end with a result that is observable, even if you cannot put a precise number on it.

Problem-Data-Model-Deploy (PDMD) is the most useful structure for system design questions. Start by clarifying the problem and the success metric. Describe the data you would need and where it comes from. Move to model selection and justify why this approach fits this problem. End with serving, monitoring, and retraining. Interviewers at this stage want to see that you think beyond training accuracy.

Hypothesis-Experiment-Learn works well for debugging scenarios and 'what would you do if' questions. State what you think is wrong and why. Describe the simplest experiment you would run to test it. Explain what each outcome would tell you and what your next step would be. This structure demonstrates the kind of clear reasoning under uncertainty that real ML debugging requires.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Production mindset. Candidates who can train a model in a notebook are common. Candidates who can deploy, monitor, and maintain that model in production are rarer. Expect questions about monitoring strategy, retraining cadence, and handling distribution shift. If you have shipped something that served real traffic, make that clear early in your answers.

Coding fluency. Most Indian product companies and global firms hiring in India test Python coding. You will typically be asked to implement ML algorithms from scratch or manipulate data with NumPy and Pandas. Clean, readable code matters as much as correctness.

System design thinking. For mid-level and senior roles, interviewers want to see that you can reason about an end-to-end ML pipeline: data ingestion, feature stores, training pipelines, model serving, and feedback loops. Practice describing these systems out loud before your interview.

Communication. ML Engineers collaborate with product managers, data engineers, and business stakeholders. Interviewers probe whether you can explain a model choice or a failure in plain terms. If you reach for jargon when a simpler explanation would work, that registers as a weak signal.

Intellectual honesty. If you do not know an answer, say so and explain how you would find out. Interviewers consistently report preferring candidates who know the limits of their own knowledge over those who give confident-sounding answers that fall apart under follow-up.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused multi-week plan for ML Engineer interview prep.

Weeks 1-2: Solidify fundamentals. Review core ML concepts: supervised and unsupervised learning, regularization, optimization algorithms, evaluation metrics for classification and ranking, and the mathematics behind models you use most often. Fill gaps in probability and statistics (Bayes theorem, common distributions, hypothesis testing). For coding, practice Python problems involving arrays, strings, and data structures that appear in ML contexts (heaps for top-k problems, graphs for recommendation systems).

Weeks 3-4: Strengthen practical ML skills. If you do not already have them, build a few end-to-end projects: one classification problem, one regression or ranking task, and one involving unstructured data such as text or images. For each project, prepare to explain the problem, the data source, the model choice reasoning, the evaluation strategy, how you would deploy it, and how you would monitor it in production.

Week 5: System design practice. Practice designing ML systems out loud. Common prompts include a recommendation engine, a fraud detection pipeline, a search ranking system, and a content moderation tool. Use the Problem-Data-Model-Deploy framework and practice covering all four layers in one sitting, leaving room for discussion and follow-up questions.

Week 6: Behavioral prep and mock interviews. Collect a handful of strong project stories and map each to a STAR answer. Practice saying them out loud, not just writing them down. Do a few mock interviews with a peer or on a platform that gives live feedback. 07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping the business context. Many candidates dive straight into the algorithm when asked about a project. Interviewers want to know why the problem mattered, what success looked like, and what the constraints were. Open every project answer with one sentence on the business goal before explaining the model.

Treating offline metrics as the full story. If your answer ends with 'the model achieved strong test set performance,' you are leaving out everything that matters in production. Always mention how you validated in a real or simulated deployment setting and what monitoring you put in place.

Memorizing answers without understanding them. Interviewers follow up. If you give a textbook definition of dropout but cannot say when you would or would not use it on a real project, the gap becomes visible fast. Study concepts through examples from your own work, not just definitions.

Underselling engineering skills. ML Engineers are expected to write production-quality code and understand infrastructure. Candidates who discuss only modeling and skip pipelines, APIs, and deployment often come across as junior even with strong research backgrounds. Be specific about the engineering work you have personally done.

Not asking clarifying questions in design rounds. Jumping into a system design answer without clarifying scale, latency requirements, or data availability is a common mistake. Spend a few minutes asking questions at the start of a design round. It signals senior-level thinking before you have drawn a single diagram.

Ignoring the specific job description. With 803 ML Engineer openings in India tracked in mid-2026, the roles vary widely, from NLP research to MLOps to computer vision. Tailor your project stories and preparation to the stack and domain in the specific job description. Generic answers work against you when the interviewer can tell you have not read the JD.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does a typical ML Engineer interview have in India?

Candidates typically report 3 to 5 rounds based on publicly shared accounts on Glassdoor and LinkedIn. A common sequence includes an initial screening call, one or two coding rounds, an ML concept discussion, and a system design or hiring-manager round. Some companies add a take-home assignment before the live rounds. The exact structure varies significantly by company size and the seniority of the role.

Do I need a PhD to get hired as an ML Engineer in India?

No. Most ML Engineer roles at Indian product companies and global tech firms hiring in India are open to candidates with a bachelor's or master's degree plus relevant project or work experience. A PhD is typically more relevant for pure research roles at labs or dedicated research teams. What matters most for engineering roles is demonstrable experience shipping ML systems, strong Python coding skills, and a clear understanding of how to take a model from notebook to production.

Which Python libraries are tested most often in ML Engineer interviews?

You should be comfortable with NumPy, Pandas, Scikit-learn, and at least one deep learning framework. PyTorch appears more commonly than TensorFlow in interview questions in 2026, though both come up. For MLOps-flavored roles, familiarity with tools like MLflow, Airflow, or cloud ML platforms is commonly expected by hiring teams. More importantly, be able to explain what a library is doing under the hood, not just how to call its APIs.

How important is system design for ML Engineer interviews in India?

For mid-level and senior roles it is often the deciding round. Companies want to see that you can design a full ML pipeline, not just train a model in a notebook. Interviewers typically probe your thinking on data collection, feature engineering at scale, model serving latency, and monitoring for drift or degradation. If you are targeting a senior role, treat ML system design as the highest-priority area in your preparation.

Where are most ML Engineer jobs in India right now?

Bangalore leads with 165 openings out of 803 tracked by knok as of mid-2026, followed by Delhi at 50, Hyderabad at 27, Mumbai at 15, and Pune and Chennai at 14 each. A meaningful share of open roles are also remote or hybrid, so your options are not limited to these cities. Check the job description for location flexibility before applying.

How should I prepare if I am coming from a data analyst background?

Focus on closing two specific gaps: production Python coding and model deployment. Analysts often have strong data intuition and SQL skills but may not have experience writing code that runs in a production environment or deploying models as APIs. Build a few end-to-end projects where you train a model, expose it as a REST API, and host it on a cloud platform. Be ready to walk interviewers through these projects as evidence of engineering capability, not just analysis work.
