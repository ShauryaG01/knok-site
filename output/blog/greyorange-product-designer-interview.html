01 Overview

Overview

GreyOrange is a robotics and AI company building autonomous fulfillment systems for warehouses and distribution centres. Their Ranger AMR (autonomous mobile robot) fleet and the Fulfillment Operating System software run live operations for enterprise clients across retail, e-commerce, and third-party logistics in India, the US, and Southeast Asia.

A Product Designer here works at a genuinely unusual intersection: shaping interfaces that warehouse operators, operations managers, and fulfilment supervisors use in high-pressure, real-time environments. The work spans fleet management dashboards, robot scheduling UIs, exception-handling flows, and mobile apps for floor staff. This is not consumer design. The stakes are high, the users are trained professionals, and the feedback loop is seconds, not weeks.

GreyOrange currently has 63 open roles across functions. Candidates report the interview process typically runs 3-5 rounds: an initial recruiter screen, a portfolio review with the design team, one or two deep-dive design rounds (which may include a take-home assignment), and a final conversation with a senior leader or cross-functional stakeholder. The process can take several weeks end to end.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly in GreyOrange Product Designer interviews, based on what candidates typically report.

  1. Walk us through a project where you designed for operational or enterprise users. How did your process differ from consumer design?
  1. GreyOrange products run in real-time warehouse environments where a bad UX decision can cost significant time and money per minute. How do you design for high-stakes, time-pressured operators?
  1. Describe a time you had to dramatically simplify a very complex workflow. What did you cut, and how did you decide what stayed?
  1. Our products sit at the hardware-software boundary. Have you designed an interface that reflects the state of physical hardware or IoT devices? How did you handle latency, uncertainty, or failure states in the UI?
  1. How do you approach designing a dashboard for users who have only a few seconds to glance at a screen?
  1. Tell us about a design decision that engineering or product pushed back on. How did you defend your position, and when did you compromise?
  1. GreyOrange sells to large enterprise clients. How do you do user research when the end users (warehouse floor workers) are not the economic buyers?
  1. Describe your experience with design systems. Have you built or contributed to a component library for a complex enterprise product?
  1. How do you handle multiple user types in the same product, for example a floor associate versus a regional operations manager who needs aggregate views?
  1. Walk us through how you would approach a design brief like: 'redesign the exception management flow for a large warehouse robot fleet.'
  1. How do you measure whether a design is working in an operational context, where standard metrics like session duration or click-through can be misleading?
  1. GreyOrange operates across India, the US, and Southeast Asia. How have you approached internationalisation or designing for users with different language and literacy levels?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for every behavioural question. Here are three full examples tailored to GreyOrange's context.

Q: Walk us through a project where you designed for operational, non-consumer users.

*Situation:* I was working on a B2B logistics platform used by dispatch supervisors at a mid-size courier company. The existing order status screen showed dozens of data fields in a flat table, and supervisors told us they had to 'hunt' for exceptions every shift.

*Task:* I was asked to redesign the screen so supervisors could spot and act on exceptions within seconds of opening the view.

*Action:* I ran four contextual observation sessions at the dispatch desk (not user interviews, actual observation at the workstation). I found that most of the time supervisors needed three things: how many orders were delayed, who was responsible for each delay, and what action was already taken. I stripped the UI to a priority-sorted exception list with a colour-coded status column, collapsed everything else into a detail drawer, and ran two rounds of hallway testing with a small group of supervisors each time.

*Result:* Supervisors in testing found critical exceptions dramatically faster than with the old design. The engineering build took two sprints. After rollout, the operations team reported fewer missed SLA alerts in the first month.

---

Q: Tell us about a design decision that engineering pushed back on. How did you handle it?

*Situation:* I had designed a live robot position view for a warehouse management product. The design showed each robot's position updating in near-real-time with smooth animation.

*Task:* Engineering said the animation approach would require a persistent connection per session and would not scale to many simultaneous operator sessions without significant infrastructure cost.

*Action:* Instead of accepting a flat 'no', I arranged a dedicated working session with the lead engineer. We whiteboarded three options together: full real-time (my original), a short-interval poll with a 'last updated' timestamp, and a hybrid where only the active robot zone streamed live. I ran a quick preference test with two operators. Both said the poll approach felt 'fresh enough' for their actual workflow. I updated the design to use the poll and added a clear 'data age' label so operators always knew how current the view was.

*Result:* Engineering shipped the feature in the planned sprint. The 'data age' label later became a requested addition for the client's compliance team.

---

Q: Describe a time you simplified a very complex workflow.

*Situation:* A warehouse client wanted a 'task assignment' flow where a supervisor could manually assign a specific pick task to a specific robot. The existing flow had many steps spread across multiple screens.

*Task:* Reduce the flow to something a supervisor could complete quickly without needing any training.

*Action:* I mapped every step against the question: 'is the user making a real decision here, or is the system asking for confirmation it does not need?' Most of the steps were confirmations that added no safety value. I collapsed the flow to three core steps using smart defaults (the nearest available robot was pre-selected, the task type was inferred from context). I presented two versions to the product manager: one with all the old confirmations still available as optional expanded settings, and one with them removed entirely. We agreed on a 'simple mode' as the default with an 'advanced override' toggle for edge cases.

*Result:* Usability testing showed new supervisors completing the task with no prior training at all. The client's operations lead called it 'the change they noticed most' in the first-quarter review.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For portfolio and case study questions, structure your answer in three beats: the problem and the users (who were they, what was the context), your process (what you did and why, not just which tools you used), and the outcome (what shipped and what you measured or observed). GreyOrange interviewers specifically look for evidence that you understand operational users, so spend more time on user context than on visual polish.

For 'how would you approach X' hypotheticals, use the Problem, Users, Constraints, Options, Tradeoffs structure. State what you know, what you would need to learn first, and what you would not decide until you had spoken to users. This shows structured thinking without pretending you have answers you do not have.

For conflict and collaboration questions, resist the urge to make yourself the hero who was always right. The strongest answers show that you changed your mind when you got new information, and that you know when to hold a position versus when to compromise. GreyOrange is a cross-functional company and interviewers are checking whether you can work across robotics, software, and operations teams without ego getting in the way.

For metrics and measurement questions, acknowledge honestly when metrics are hard to define. In operational design, 'fewer errors per shift' or 'time to resolve an exception' often matter more than traditional product metrics. Name the specific thing you would measure and explain why it reflects the actual user goal, not just what is easy to instrument.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Deep empathy for non-consumer users. GreyOrange products are used by warehouse operators, logistics managers, and field engineers, not casual app users. Interviewers want to see that you have thought seriously about users who are time-poor, task-focused, and often working in noisy, physically demanding environments. If your portfolio is entirely consumer apps, prepare to explicitly bridge the gap.

Systems thinking. The product is a physical-digital system: robots, sensors, software, and humans all interact. Candidates who think only about the screen in isolation tend to struggle here. Show that you consider how your UI decisions affect what operators do physically on the floor.

Comfort with ambiguity and complexity. Enterprise B2B products have many stakeholders and messy requirements. Interviewers are watching for whether you can make clear decisions in the face of unclear briefs, and whether you can communicate your reasoning to non-designers.

Collaboration and communication. GreyOrange is a deeply cross-functional company. Product designers work closely with robotics engineers, product managers, and client-facing teams. Interviewers typically probe for examples where you navigated disagreement or brought a sceptical stakeholder along.

Craft, but not just aesthetics. Visual quality matters, but interviewers weight interaction design, information hierarchy, and the ability to handle complex data displays more heavily than visual polish. A beautifully animated consumer app will impress less than a thoughtful dashboard design case study that explains every decision.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Understand the domain. Spend time reading about warehouse automation, AMRs, and fulfilment operations. GreyOrange publishes case studies and product videos publicly. Understand what their clients care about (throughput, error rates, SLA compliance) and how software fits into a physical warehouse workflow. Look at how other players in the robotics fulfillment space present their operational UIs.

Week 2: Audit and tailor your portfolio. Pick two or three case studies that show operational or enterprise design, data-heavy interfaces, or hardware-adjacent work. If you do not have any, prepare to speak honestly about what you would do differently given what you have learned about GreyOrange's domain. Thin domain experience paired with genuine self-awareness beats confident but irrelevant case studies every time.

Week 3: Practise out loud. Run through your case studies speaking, not reading. Practise answering 'why did you make that decision' for every screen in your portfolio. Work through the hypothetical prompts in the questions section above. Record yourself once and watch it back. Most designers underestimate how much they skim over the reasoning in verbal walk-throughs.

Before the interview. Read GreyOrange's most recent press releases or LinkedIn posts to catch any recent product announcements. Prepare three or four specific questions for your interviewers, focused on the design team's research process, how access to warehouse floor users works given client constraints, and what a successful first few months looks like in the role.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Showing only consumer app portfolios without bridging to enterprise. GreyOrange interviewers will not automatically make the translation for you. Explicitly draw the connection: 'I designed for consumer users, but here is what I would do differently for operational users and here is why.'

Talking about process without talking about decisions. 'I did user research, then wireframes, then prototyping' is a process description, not a case study. What interviewers want is: 'I learned X from research, which changed my original assumption, which led me to choose option B over option A for these reasons.'

Ignoring engineering constraints in your answers. GreyOrange products run on real hardware with real infrastructure limits. Candidates who never mention feasibility, latency, or technical tradeoffs can come across as designers who will create beautiful but unshippable work.

Under-preparing for the design assignment. Many candidates report receiving a take-home brief. Treat it seriously. Allocate a full day if possible. Show your thinking process, not just your final screens. Comment on what you assumed, what you would validate next, and what you deliberately left out of scope.

Asking no questions. Interviewers at a company like GreyOrange expect curiosity about the domain. Asking nothing signals low interest. Asking generic questions ('what is the culture like?') signals surface-level preparation. Ask about the specific design challenges the team is actively working on right now.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

Is there a design assignment in the GreyOrange Product Designer interview?

Candidates typically report a take-home design brief somewhere in the process, usually after the first portfolio review call. The brief commonly involves an enterprise or operational design challenge rather than a consumer scenario. Treat it as a full case study: show your problem framing, your assumptions, your design decisions, and what you would test or validate next. Budget a full day for it if you can.

Do I need a background in robotics or warehousing to get this role?

No prior robotics or warehousing experience is required, but you need to show genuine curiosity about the domain and a willingness to learn quickly. Interviewers typically want to see that you understand what operational users need, that you have designed for complexity before, and that you can ask smart questions about an unfamiliar domain. Doing your research before the interview matters more than prior industry experience.

How important is visual design skill versus UX thinking at GreyOrange?

Both matter, but interviewers typically weight UX thinking, information architecture, and the ability to handle complex data more heavily than pure visual craft. GreyOrange products are operational tools, not marketing surfaces. A thoughtful case study with clear design rationale will land better than a visually polished portfolio that cannot explain why decisions were made.

How many rounds does the GreyOrange Product Designer interview typically have?

Candidates report 3-5 rounds in total, typically covering a recruiter screen, a portfolio review with the design team, one or two design deep-dives (one of which may be the take-home assignment debrief), and a final round with a senior stakeholder. Round names and sequencing can vary depending on the specific team and the level of the role being hired for.

What salary range should I expect for a Product Designer at GreyOrange in 2026?

Across the Indian market, commonly cited ranges for Product Designers are 6-12 LPA at entry level (0-2 years), 14-24 LPA at mid level (3-5 years), 26-40 LPA at senior level (6-9 years), and 36-55+ LPA at lead or principal level. GreyOrange roles tend to attract mid-to-senior profiles given the enterprise complexity involved, so most offers are likely to sit in the mid-to-senior bands. Always negotiate based on your specific experience and any competing offers you hold.

How do I prepare if my portfolio has no enterprise or operational design work?

Be transparent about the gap rather than trying to hide it. Pick the closest work you have (data-heavy consumer features, internal tools, complex multi-step flows) and explicitly draw the bridge: explain how you would think differently now for operational users. Prepare a short spoken take on a GreyOrange product challenge you find interesting and how you would approach it. Self-awareness and domain curiosity can offset thin portfolio experience, especially at mid level.
