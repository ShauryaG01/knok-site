01 Overview

Overview

GSK (GlaxoSmithKline) is a global healthcare company with a growing digital and technology function in India. Product Managers here work across patient-facing digital health tools, internal data platforms, commercial sales technology, and R&D pipeline systems. As of July 2026, knok jobradar tracked 64 open GSK roles, placing it among the more active pharma employers for PM talent in India.

Typical process (candidates report): Most candidates go through 3-5 rounds: a recruiter screening, one or two conversations with the hiring manager, a product or case exercise, and a panel with cross-functional stakeholders. The full process typically runs several weeks from first contact to offer.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These are the questions GSK PM candidates most commonly report encountering. Prepare for all of them, especially those around regulatory context and stakeholder management.

  1. Tell us about a product you owned end-to-end. What metrics did you choose and why?
  2. GSK operates in a heavily regulated industry. Describe a time when compliance or regulatory constraints shaped your product decisions.
  3. How would you prioritise features for a digital health tool used by both patients and healthcare professionals?
  4. Describe a time you had to align stakeholders from medical, legal, and commercial teams on a single product decision.
  5. How do you approach product discovery when end users are hard to access, for example patients with chronic conditions or busy clinicians?
  6. GSK operates across many markets including India. How would you build a product that works globally but stays relevant locally?
  7. How do you define and track OKRs for a commercial technology product? Walk us through a real example.
  8. Tell us about a product that did not meet its goals. What went wrong and what did you change?
  9. How do you work with data science or analytics teams to drive product decisions? Describe a specific collaboration.
  10. Describe a time you had to say no to a senior stakeholder. How did you handle it and what was the outcome?
  11. How do you manage a roadmap when business priorities shift frequently?
  12. What does genuine patient-centricity look like in a digital product?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time when regulatory constraints shaped your product decisions.

*Situation:* I was PM for a patient data platform at a healthcare technology company. Several weeks before our planned launch, our compliance team flagged that a core feature, allowing patients to share diagnostic data with third-party apps, needed explicit consent flows to meet local data protection rules.

*Task:* My task was to redesign the consent experience without delaying the launch by more than a couple of weeks, while keeping the feature genuinely useful for patients.

*Action:* I ran a quick round of user interviews with a small group of patients and clinicians to understand what information they needed to feel confident consenting. I worked with legal to simplify the consent language from a multi-page form into a clear step-by-step flow. I also negotiated with engineering to ship a 'lite' version of the feature at launch and move the full data-sharing capability to a follow-on sprint.

*Result:* We launched just two weeks after the original date. Consent completion came in well above what the team had projected, and legal later adopted our flow as a model for future feature releases.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you had to say no to a senior stakeholder.

*Situation:* A VP of Sales asked me to add a bulk-messaging feature to our field sales app two weeks before a major trade event, saying it would help reps on the ground.

*Task:* My task was to evaluate the request honestly and communicate my decision in a way that kept the working relationship intact.

*Action:* I showed the VP our current sprint commitments and explained that adding the feature would delay a compliance-critical update already committed to our regulatory team. I offered two alternatives: a lightweight workaround using an existing export feature for the event, or scheduling bulk-messaging as the first item in the next sprint with a firm delivery date.

*Result:* The VP accepted the workaround for the event and appreciated the transparency. The feature shipped in the next sprint on schedule, and the compliance update landed on time. The VP later referenced this conversation in a team retrospective as a strong example of PM decision-making.

---

Q: Walk us through a product that did not meet its goals.

*Situation:* I owned a digital onboarding product for new healthcare provider accounts. We set a goal of reducing onboarding time and launched a redesigned portal after several months of development.

*Task:* Several months after launch, onboarding time had not improved meaningfully despite strong internal confidence in the design. My task was to understand why and fix it.

*Action:* I ran a root-cause analysis combining session recordings, support ticket data, and several in-depth interviews with onboarding coordinators. I found that our portal worked well for simple accounts but broke down for accounts with multiple sites and custom contract terms, a segment we had under-researched during discovery. I then built a targeted fix for the multi-site flow and re-ran the analysis.

*Result:* Onboarding time for multi-site accounts improved substantially in the following quarter. More importantly, I updated our discovery checklist to always segment by account complexity before starting design, a change that has since prevented similar blind spots on other products.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the default format for all behavioural questions. Keep each section concise: one or two sentences on Situation and Task, more detail on Action, and a clear result whether quantified or described qualitatively. GSK interviewers will ask follow-up questions if the result is too vague.

RICE Prioritisation (Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort) works well when asked how you rank competing features or roadmap items. At GSK, factor in regulatory effort alongside engineering effort: a feature that requires clinical review or legal sign-off carries higher effective effort than it first appears.

Jobs-to-be-Done is useful for product design and discovery questions. Frame user needs as: 'When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome].' This approach resonates in healthcare where patient and clinician contexts are highly specific and differ sharply from consumer scenarios.

OKR framing (Objective plus Key Results) is expected for any question about goal-setting or success metrics. Define the qualitative objective first, then name two or three measurable key results. Avoid vanity metrics; GSK interviewers tend to prefer health outcomes or business results over engagement proxies like page views.

Stakeholder mapping is a practical tool for any cross-functional conflict question. Name who is impacted, what they care about, and how you communicated with each group. This matters at GSK because PM roles sit at the intersection of medical, regulatory, commercial, and engineering teams, each with different incentives and timelines.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

GSK PM interviewers typically look for five things across all rounds.

Healthcare and regulatory context. You do not need a pharma background, but you should understand that healthcare products operate under constraints that consumer tech does not. Candidates who treat compliance as an afterthought rather than a product input tend to struggle in later rounds.

Cross-functional credibility. PM roles at GSK span medical, legal, commercial, and engineering stakeholders. Interviewers want evidence that you can earn trust from people with very different incentives and professional vocabularies.

Patient-centricity, not just user-centricity. GSK's mission is about improving patient lives. Answers that connect product decisions to real patient outcomes carry more weight than answers focused only on engagement metrics or revenue uplift.

Structured, evidence-based thinking. Use frameworks, cite data, and show your reasoning clearly. Vague answers about 'balancing trade-offs' without concrete structure are a common reason candidates do not advance past early rounds.

Self-awareness and learning agility. Expect at least one question about failure or a serious mistake. What interviewers actually want to hear is how you diagnosed the problem and what you changed, not a polished story where everything happened to work out in the end.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused plan for the four weeks before your GSK PM interview.

Week 1: Know GSK. Read GSK's publicly available annual report summary and any digital health or technology strategy materials they have published. Identify two or three product areas that genuinely interest you. Understand how GSK generates revenue and where digital products sit in that picture.

Week 2: Build your story bank. Write STAR answers for at least eight behavioural scenarios: a regulatory constraint, a stakeholder conflict, a failed product, a successful launch, a prioritisation decision, a data-driven insight, a time you influenced without authority, and a cross-functional collaboration. Practise speaking each answer aloud rather than just reading it.

Week 3: Product and case practice. Work through one product design question and one metrics question every day. For each answer, apply a framework (Jobs-to-be-Done or RICE) and connect your conclusion to a healthcare or patient outcome. Time yourself and focus on being clear and structured rather than covering every possible angle.

Week 4: Mock interviews and logistics. Complete at least two mock interviews with a peer or mentor. Ask for feedback specifically on structure, clarity, and whether your healthcare framing felt genuine rather than rehearsed. Prepare two or three thoughtful questions for the panel, focused on product strategy, team structure, or how success is measured in the role.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating it like a consumer tech interview. The most common reason strong PM candidates do not clear a GSK interview is ignoring the regulated environment. Every product and case question has a regulatory or patient safety dimension at GSK. Address it proactively, not as a footnote at the end of your answer.

Vague metrics. Saying a product 'improved user experience' or 'increased engagement' without specifying what you measured will not satisfy GSK interviewers. Name your metric, explain why you chose it over alternatives, and describe what you did when the number moved in the wrong direction.

No questions to ask. GSK interviewers note when candidates have nothing to ask. Thoughtful questions about product strategy, team structure, or how success is measured signal genuine interest and a PM mindset.

Over-explaining the framework. Some candidates spend most of their answer describing the methodology and run out of time for the actual example. Lead with a sentence that orients the interviewer, then move into the story. The framework is a tool, not the answer itself.

Underestimating the panel round. Candidates often prepare for the hiring manager but not for medical, commercial, or regulatory stakeholders who may also be present. Prepare answers that work for people who are not traditional PM interviewers and who will probe from their own domain perspective.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the GSK PM interview typically have?

Candidates typically report 3-5 rounds in total. These commonly include a recruiter screening call, one or two conversations with the hiring manager, a product or case exercise, and a panel round with cross-functional stakeholders. The exact structure varies by team and seniority, so it is worth asking your recruiter at the start of the process.

Do I need healthcare or pharma experience to get a PM role at GSK?

A pharma background is not typically required, but candidates who demonstrate an understanding of regulated environments and patient-centred thinking have a clear advantage. GSK has hired PMs from fintech, enterprise SaaS, and e-commerce, provided those candidates showed they had done their homework on healthcare constraints. Prepare to explain how your past experience translates to a healthcare product context.

Is there a case study or product exercise in the GSK PM interview?

Candidates commonly report a product design or analytical exercise as part of the process, though the format varies by team. Some teams give a take-home exercise while others run a live case discussion in a round. Ask your recruiter what to expect so you can prepare in the right format rather than guessing.

How long does the GSK PM hiring process take from first contact to offer?

Candidates typically report a process that runs from a few weeks to a couple of months, depending on how quickly rounds are scheduled and whether multiple candidates are in the pipeline at the same time.
