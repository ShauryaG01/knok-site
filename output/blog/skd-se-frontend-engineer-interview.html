02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates report these questions coming up frequently in SKD SE Frontend Engineer interviews. Prepare depth, not just definitions.

  1. Walk me through what happens in the browser from the moment a user types a URL to when the page is fully loaded.
  2. Explain how React's virtual DOM works and why it matters for performance.
  3. How do you decide between local component state, Context API, and a dedicated state manager like Redux or Zustand?
  4. What is the difference between a 'controlled' and an 'uncontrolled' component? When would you use each?
  5. Our app has a page that loads very slowly on mid-range Android devices. Walk me through how you would debug and fix it.
  6. Explain event delegation in JavaScript with a practical example.
  7. What are closures, and can you describe a real bug you encountered because of unexpected closure behaviour?
  8. How do you approach cross-browser compatibility and responsive design in a production app?
  9. Tell me about a time you refactored a large component or feature. What drove the decision and what was the result?
  10. How do you test your React components? What is the difference between unit tests and integration tests in a frontend context?
  11. How do you ensure your UI is accessible to users who rely on screen readers or keyboard navigation?
  12. Describe how you would set up code-splitting and lazy loading in a React app.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for all behavioural and experience-based questions.

Q: Tell me about a time you significantly improved a page's load performance.

*Situation:* At my previous company, the product listing page was loading too slowly on mid-range connections, and users were dropping off before seeing any results.

*Task:* I was asked to reduce load time without a full rewrite, targeting a measurable improvement within one sprint.

*Action:* I ran Lighthouse and Chrome DevTools profiles to identify the bottlenecks. I found three main issues: large unoptimised images, a JavaScript bundle that loaded everything upfront, and a third-party analytics script blocking render. I converted images to WebP, added lazy loading for below-the-fold images, introduced React.lazy with Suspense to split the bundle by route, and moved the analytics script to load asynchronously.

*Result:* The Largest Contentful Paint improved significantly (publicly reported in our internal performance dashboard), and the team adopted lazy loading as the default pattern for all new routes.

---

Q: Describe a time you had to refactor a complex component.

*Situation:* We had a 'GodComponent' handling user profile display, edit mode, avatar upload, and notification preferences, all crammed into one very long file.

*Task:* A new feature request touched this component and the team agreed it was too risky to extend further without splitting it first.

*Action:* I mapped out the distinct responsibilities, then extracted each into its own component with a clear interface. I kept the parent as a thin orchestrator that held shared state and passed props down. I wrote snapshot and interaction tests for each child before deleting the original code.

*Result:* The file became far shorter and more focused. New feature delivery on that screen went from feeling risky to routine, and the tests caught a regression in the very next release cycle.

---

Q: Tell me about a disagreement with a teammate over a technical decision.

*Situation:* A senior colleague wanted to add Redux to a new internal tool project. I felt the added complexity was not justified for a tool with only a few screens.

*Task:* We needed to align before starting, since the choice would affect everyone on the small team.

*Action:* I prepared a quick comparison: what Redux would add in capability versus what it would add in boilerplate and onboarding time. I proposed starting with React Context and useState, with an agreed review point after a couple of sprints to reassess if complexity grew.

*Result:* The team agreed to try it. After those sprints, Context was sufficient and we shipped ahead of the original plan. My colleague later said the lighter approach made it easier to onboard a junior developer who joined mid-project.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For technical concept questions, use a three-part structure: define the concept clearly, give a concrete example from your own work, then discuss tradeoffs or edge cases. Avoid reading a textbook definition and stopping there.

For 'how would you' design or architecture questions, think out loud. Start with clarifying questions about scale, team size, and constraints. Lay out your options, then recommend one with reasoning. Interviewers want to see your thought process, not just a final answer.

For debugging and performance questions, walk through your diagnostic approach step by step: what tool you would reach for first, what signals you would look for, and how you would confirm the fix worked. Specific tool names (Lighthouse, Chrome DevTools, React DevTools Profiler) signal real hands-on experience.

For behavioural questions, STAR is your structure. Keep Situation and Task brief (two or three sentences), spend most of your time on Action (what you specifically did, not what the team did), and always end with a concrete, ideally measurable Result.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Depth over breadth. Candidates who can explain one concept thoroughly, including the 'why' and the tradeoffs, stand out more than those who name-drop many tools without depth.

Ownership and initiative. SKD SE interviewers, like most product teams, look for signals that you do not wait to be told what to fix. Examples where you spotted a problem proactively score well.

Communication under pressure. Live coding rounds are partly a test of how you think and communicate when you are stuck. Candidates report that verbalising your approach matters as much as getting the final answer right.

Genuine product thinking. For a frontend role, being able to connect your technical choices to user experience shows maturity. Mentioning metrics like page load time or Core Web Vitals signals that you care about outcomes, not just code.

Clean, testable code. Interviewers look for readable variable names, sensible component boundaries, and evidence that you think about how the next person will maintain your code.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: JavaScript fundamentals. Review closures, prototypes, the event loop, async/await, and how the browser renders pages. Do not just read. Write small code snippets to test your understanding and practice explaining each concept out loud in a couple of minutes.

Week 2: React depth. Rebuild a feature you know well from scratch without copy-pasting. Focus on hooks (useState, useEffect, useCallback, useMemo), component composition, and avoiding unnecessary re-renders. Read the React documentation sections on performance.

Week 3: System and architecture questions. Practice answering 'how would you build X' questions for common frontend patterns: infinite scroll, a form with complex validation, a dashboard with real-time updates. Focus on tradeoffs rather than hunting for a single correct answer.

Week 4: Behavioural prep and mock interviews. Write down several specific stories from your career using STAR, covering a performance win, a refactor, a disagreement, a missed deadline, and a time you learned from a mistake. Do at least a couple of timed mock interviews with a peer.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping to code before clarifying. Candidates who start typing immediately in a live coding round often solve the wrong problem. Take a minute or so to confirm inputs, outputs, and constraints with the interviewer before writing a single line.

Vague answers to experience questions. Saying 'we improved performance' without explaining what you personally did, or what actually changed, is a missed opportunity. Use specific actions and concrete results.

Over-engineering answers to simple questions. If asked how you would handle a simple form, suggesting a complex state machine framework signals poor judgment about complexity tradeoffs.

Not asking clarifying questions. Interviewers often leave architecture questions deliberately open-ended. Asking about scale, user base, and constraints shows product maturity, not weakness.

Memorised answers that do not fit the question. Candidates often prepare a 'performance story' and force it into every question. Tailor each answer to what is actually being asked.

Ignoring the 'why'. Defining what a closure is scores partial credit. Explaining why JavaScript closures matter in React hooks (stale state bugs, dependency arrays) is what gets you to the next round.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does SKD SE typically have for Frontend Engineer interviews?

Candidates report a process that typically runs 3-4 rounds, including an initial screening call, one or two technical rounds, a coding assignment or live coding session, and a final round with a senior engineer or hiring manager. The exact structure can vary by team and urgency of the role, so it is worth asking your recruiter at the start of the process. Some candidates report fewer rounds for contract or time-sensitive positions.

What salary can I expect as a Frontend Engineer at SKD SE?

Specific SKD SE compensation data is limited in public sources, so treat any figure you see online as a rough reference point only. Across the broader market, entry-level Frontend Engineers (0-2 years) typically earn 5-11 LPA, mid-level (3-5 years) 12-22 LPA, senior (6-9 years) 24-40 LPA, and lead or staff roles 38-58+ LPA. For more current and role-specific data, check Glassdoor or levels.fyi before your negotiation conversation.

Is the coding round online or in-person?

Candidates report that the coding component is typically conducted online, either on a shared editor during a video call or as a take-home assignment sent before or after the technical discussion round. In-person coding rounds are less common but do happen for final rounds at some locations. Ask your recruiter early so you can prepare the right setup and environment.

What framework or language does SKD SE use on the frontend?

Based on what candidates report, React is the most commonly tested framework in SKD SE frontend interviews, alongside core JavaScript (ES6 and beyond). TypeScript basics are worth preparing as well, since many product teams have adopted it as a standard. It is always worth asking your recruiter about the specific tech stack for the team you are interviewing with, as it can vary across projects.

How important is system design knowledge for a Frontend Engineer role at SKD SE?

For mid-level and above roles, candidates report being asked to design or architect a frontend feature or small application, covering component structure, state management choices, API communication, and performance considerations. For entry-level roles, the focus is more squarely on JavaScript fundamentals and React patterns. If you have more than a couple of years of experience, prepare at least one or two feature design walkthroughs before your interview.

Does SKD SE ask DSA (data structures and algorithms) questions in frontend interviews?

Candidates report that the focus is primarily on JavaScript-specific problem solving and React patterns rather than heavy algorithmic questions. That said, basic data structures such as arrays, objects, and trees for DOM manipulation, along with simple algorithm questions, do appear occasionally in the coding round. Spending a week on easy-to-medium JavaScript algorithm problems is worthwhile preparation, but do not deprioritise frontend-specific topics for it.
