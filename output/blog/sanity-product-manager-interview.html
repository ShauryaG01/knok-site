01 Overview

Overview

Sanity is a developer-first structured content platform. Its core products are Sanity Studio (a fully customisable, open-source content editor) and the Content Lake (a real-time hosted backend for structured content). The company is distributed and remote-friendly, which shapes both its culture and its PM interview process.

As of the latest knok jobradar snapshot, there are 26 open Product Manager roles listed at Sanity. PM work there sits at the crossover of developer experience, content infrastructure, and API design. You will typically work with engineers, designers, and developer-relations teams to ship roadmap items that affect how developers build with Sanity and how content authors work inside Studio.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates report a mix of product-sense, technical depth, and behavioural questions. The themes below are consistent with a developer-tools company that serves both technical and non-technical users.

  1. Sanity's primary customers are developers. How would you define and measure developer experience for Sanity Studio?
  2. Walk us through how you would prioritise between improving the Content Lake API and improving the Studio editor experience.
  3. A large enterprise customer is asking for multi-region data residency. How do you evaluate this against your current roadmap priorities?
  4. How would you design a feature that lets non-technical content editors create reusable content components without involving a developer each time?
  5. Sanity competes with other headless CMS platforms. How do you identify and defend Sanity's differentiated positioning?
  6. Describe a time you made a difficult prioritisation call with limited data. What did you decide, and why?
  7. How would you improve the onboarding experience for a developer integrating Sanity for the first time?
  8. Sanity Studio is open-source. How does that shape your go-to-market and product decisions compared to a closed SaaS product?
  9. How would you define and track the success of a new SDK or plugin in the Sanity ecosystem?
  10. A feature your team shipped is being used differently than you intended by a large segment of users. What do you do?
  11. How would you approach pricing and packaging changes for Sanity's self-serve plan without alienating the developer community?
  12. Tell us about a product you admire for its developer experience and what Sanity could learn from it.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How would you improve the onboarding experience for a developer integrating Sanity for the first time?

*Situation:* At my previous company, we had a developer-tools product with a high drop-off between trial sign-up and a developer's first working API call.

*Task:* I was responsible for reducing time-to-first-value, which we defined as a working integration completed within the first session.

*Action:* I ran usability sessions with developers who had never seen the product before and identified three blockers: confusing project-setup terminology, a schema configuration step with no sensible defaults, and documentation that assumed prior GraphQL knowledge. I worked with engineering to add smart defaults, rewrote the quickstart guide in plain language, and added an in-app progress indicator so developers always knew which step came next.

*Result:* Time-to-first-value improved in the cohort we tracked, and setup-related support tickets fell in the following quarter. I would bring the same 'observe real developers, remove the first blocker' approach to Sanity Studio onboarding.

---

Q: Describe a time you made a difficult prioritisation call with limited data.

*Situation:* My team had three requests of roughly equal size: a performance improvement for existing power users, a new integration requested by a single enterprise prospect, and a self-serve billing upgrade the growth team wanted.

*Task:* We had one sprint available and I had to choose one item.

*Action:* I built a quick scoring matrix using three dimensions: impact on retention, impact on new revenue, and engineering cost. I interviewed the sales team to understand how many deals the enterprise integration could realistically unlock, and I pulled churn survey data to check whether performance was a stated reason for cancellation. The data pointed toward the billing upgrade as the highest-impact near-term item. I flagged the performance work for the following sprint to avoid accumulating retention risk.

*Result:* The billing upgrade shipped and contributed to a measurable lift in self-serve plan upgrades that quarter. The enterprise prospect accepted a committed date for the integration in a later sprint.

---

Q: How would you design a feature that lets non-technical content editors create reusable content components without involving a developer?

*Situation:* At a media company I worked with, content editors were repeatedly asking developers to duplicate content blocks across schemas, creating bottlenecks and inconsistency across the site.

*Task:* I was asked to define what a self-serve 'block library' would look like for editors with no coding knowledge.

*Action:* I ran co-design sessions with three distinct editor personas: a solo blogger, a marketing team lead, and an enterprise content operations manager. Each had a different mental model of 'reusability.' I drafted wireframes for a 'Save as template' flow inside the editor that hid schema complexity behind a simple form, then worked with design on a prototype and got engineering input on feasibility within the existing schema architecture.

*Result:* The prototype tested well with all three personas and was approved for roadmap inclusion. The key learning was that permissions and naming needed to be flexible, because 'reusable component' meant something different to each persona.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions. Structure every story as Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep the Situation and Task brief and spend most of your time on Action and Result. Quantify the Result where you have real numbers to back it up.

A structured discovery approach for product-design questions. When asked 'how would you design X,' start by clarifying the user and the problem before generating options. Prioritise your options explicitly before detailing a solution. Avoid jumping to a feature idea in the first thirty seconds. Sanity interviewers reportedly value process as much as the final answer.

A simple scoring matrix for prioritisation questions. When asked how you would prioritise, name the dimensions you are scoring on (user impact, revenue impact, strategic fit, engineering effort), score each option, and state your recommendation with caveats. Show your thinking rather than just the conclusion.

Metrics framing for success questions. For any 'how would you measure success' question, name a primary metric (the north-star outcome), one or two guardrail metrics (things you must not harm), and a leading indicator (something you can check before the lagging metric moves). For a developer-tools company like Sanity, useful leading indicators include time-to-first-API-call, documentation engagement, and SDK download trends.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Genuine comfort with developer audiences. Sanity's customers are primarily developers. Interviewers typically probe whether you understand developer mental models, care about developer experience, and can translate between engineering constraints and user needs without losing either side.

Structured, data-informed thinking. Candidates report that Sanity interviewers push back when answers rely too heavily on intuition. Come prepared to name what data you would look for, even when the hypothetical gives you none to work with.

Ownership and low-ego collaboration. Sanity operates as a distributed company with a trust-and-autonomy culture, based on its public materials. Interviewers look for evidence that you drive outcomes independently and give credit to teammates naturally in how you tell your stories.

Strong async communication. Sanity is a remote-first company. Written communication carries real weight. If you are given a written exercise or take-home document, treat it as seriously as a live interview round.

Curiosity about content infrastructure. You do not need to be a headless CMS expert on day one, but candidates who have explored Sanity Studio, read the documentation, or built a small project typically stand out in early conversations.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week one: know the product. Create a free Sanity account, follow the quickstart, and build something small (a personal blog schema is enough). Read the Content Lake documentation and understand what GROQ is and why it exists. Note one or two things that confused you as a new user. These make authentic and specific interview talking points.

Week one: know the landscape. Research how Sanity positions itself against other headless CMS options. Read recent Sanity blog posts and changelog entries to understand what the team is shipping and the reasoning behind those choices.

Week two: prepare your stories. Map your past experience to the core themes: developer experience, prioritisation under uncertainty, cross-functional collaboration, and defining metrics. Prepare five or six STAR stories covering different competencies so you are not reusing the same example for every question.

Week two: practice out loud. Record yourself answering design and prioritisation questions. Review the recording for filler words and unclear structure. Practice with a peer if possible.

Before each round: Review the job description carefully and match your language to it. Candidates report that the process typically includes a hiring-manager conversation, a product exercise, and a cross-functional panel, though the exact structure varies. Confirm the format with your recruiter.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Giving generic 'why this company' answers. Interviewers at developer-tools companies notice immediately when a candidate's answers could apply to any SaaS PM role. Have a specific, honest answer about why structured content infrastructure interests you and what about Sanity's approach you find compelling.

Treating developers as a single user segment. 'Developers' is not one persona. Junior developers, senior architects, and solo freelancers have different needs, constraints, and mental models. Segment your users when answering any design question.

Jumping to solutions before clarifying the problem. In product-design rounds, candidates who skip clarifying questions and move straight to a feature idea often lose points even if the idea itself is good. Show your process first.

Over-indexing on business metrics alone. For a developer-tools product, purely revenue-focused answers can feel off to interviewers. Balance business outcomes with developer experience metrics such as adoption, activation, and community sentiment.

Being vague about your individual contribution. In STAR answers, say 'I decided' or 'I wrote' rather than 'we shipped.' Interviewers need to understand your personal role, not the team's collective output.

Asking shallow questions at the end. Sanity interviews typically leave time for your questions. Generic questions signal low preparation. Ask about the roadmap, how the PM team measures its own success, or how content modelling decisions are made across the organisation.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Sanity PM interview typically have?

Candidates report a process that typically includes a recruiter screen, a hiring-manager conversation focused on experience and motivation, a product exercise or case study, and a cross-functional panel. The exact number of rounds and their order can vary across teams and roles. Confirm the current format with your recruiter at the start of the process so you can prepare accordingly.

Does Sanity give a take-home assignment for PM roles?

Several candidates have mentioned a written or async exercise as part of the process, which may involve a product critique, a prioritisation document, or a short design brief. Sanity is a distributed company, and written communication is a core skill they evaluate. Treat any written submission as carefully as you would a live interview round.

Do I need a technical background to interview for a PM role at Sanity?

A computer science degree is not required, but you will need enough technical fluency to discuss APIs, SDKs, and content schemas credibly. Candidates who have read the Sanity documentation and built a small project typically feel more confident in technical discussions. Being able to discuss trade-offs in API design or schema architecture at a conceptual level matters more than writing code yourself.

What salary can I expect for a PM role at Sanity in India?

Based on knok jobradar data for the broader Indian PM market, Product Managers with 3-6 years of experience typically fall in the 24-40 LPA band, while Senior PMs are commonly in the 40-60 LPA range. Sanity-specific offers will depend on the level of the role, your experience, and negotiation. For community-reported numbers, check Glassdoor and levels.fyi.

How do I stand out as a candidate with no headless CMS experience?

Build something small with Sanity before your interview, even a basic schema with a few content types. This gives you specific and authentic talking points that most candidates will not have. Frame your past experience around transferable themes: developer audiences, API or platform products, content workflows, or structured data. Interviewers care more about how you think than about domain-specific credentials.

Is the Sanity PM role remote or based in an Indian office?

Sanity is a distributed, remote-first company, and many PM roles are open to remote candidates. However, some roles may require overlap with European or US time zones for team collaboration. Confirm the location and working-hours expectations with your recruiter early in the process, as requirements can vary by team and seniority level.
