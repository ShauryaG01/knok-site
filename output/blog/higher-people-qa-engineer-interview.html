02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These 12 questions come up most frequently in Higher People QA Engineer panels, based on what candidates typically report:

  1. Walk me through how you would test a new feature end-to-end before release.
  2. What automation frameworks have you worked with, and how did you use them in a real project?
  3. How do you decide what to automate versus what to keep as manual testing?
  4. Describe a critical bug you caught that others had missed. How did you find it?
  5. How do you handle a situation where a developer disagrees that something is a bug?
  6. What does your regression testing strategy look like when the team ships frequently?
  7. How would you write test cases for an API when there is no documentation available?
  8. Tell me about a time you had to balance thorough test coverage with a tight deadline.
  9. How familiar are you with CI/CD pipelines, and what is the QA role within them?
  10. How would you approach testing a search or recommendation feature for accuracy and edge cases?
  11. What metrics do you track to measure whether your QA process is actually working?
  12. Have you worked with performance or load testing tools? Give a specific example.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a critical bug you caught that others had missed.

*Situation:* My team was preparing to release a new payment confirmation flow. Developers had signed off and the staging build looked clean to everyone involved.

*Task:* I was responsible for the final regression pass before the release window opened that evening.

*Action:* I went beyond the standard test cases and added scenarios not in the original plan. I tested a session with an expired token mid-transaction and simulated a network timeout at the exact moment of order confirmation. Most of the team had only validated the happy path.

*Result:* I found a silent failure where the user saw a 'payment successful' screen but the transaction was never confirmed on the bank side. The fix went in before release, preventing potential duplicate charges and customer trust damage.

---

Q: How do you handle a disagreement with a developer over whether something is a bug?

*Situation:* I logged a defect on a mobile app where a submit button stayed active after a user tapped it, which could trigger duplicate order submissions.

*Task:* The developer closed my ticket as 'by design', saying the scenario 'almost never happens in practice.'

*Action:* I pulled error logs and showed actual duplicate submission events from production data. I framed the conversation around user impact rather than who was right, and I proposed a simple fix: disable the button for two seconds after each tap.

*Result:* The developer agreed once they saw the data. The fix shipped in the next sprint. I also added the scenario to the regression suite so the same class of issue would be caught automatically in future releases.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you had to balance test coverage with a tight deadline.

*Situation:* My team had a hard deadline to release a new onboarding flow tied to a marketing campaign launch. The feature reached QA two days late.

*Task:* I had roughly four hours instead of the planned two days to complete my testing.

*Action:* I did a fast risk analysis, identified the three user flows that accounted for most traffic, and prioritised tests covering critical data-storage and payment paths. I explicitly flagged the lower-risk cosmetic tests I was skipping in my sign-off note so the team had full visibility on what remained untested.

*Result:* The release went out on time with no critical defects post-launch. The skipped tests were cleared in the following sprint. The product manager specifically mentioned the transparent risk call as helpful for their go or no-go decision.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use STAR for all behavioural questions. Higher People panels typically include at least two or three situational questions. Structure each answer as: Situation (the context), Task (your specific responsibility), Action (what you personally did), Result (what happened and what you learned). Candidates often over-explain the Situation and rush through the Result. Do the opposite: spend more time on your Actions and put a concrete outcome in the Result wherever you can.

Think aloud on technical questions. When asked how you would test a feature or API, do not jump straight to a final answer. Say out loud what information you would gather first, list your assumptions, name edge cases you would check, and then describe your approach. Interviewers want to see your reasoning process, not just the conclusion.

Lead with real experience on tool questions, then give your honest take. Do not just list tool names. Say which you have used in production, what problem it solved, and where you found its limits. This signals genuine depth versus a resume that names every tool in the market.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Candidates who go far in Higher People QA panels typically demonstrate three qualities, based on what interviewers and past candidates report.

Ownership mindset. Interviewers want QA Engineers who see themselves as guardians of product quality, not ticket processors. Answers that show you proactively flagged risk, improved coverage without being asked, or pushed back on a release for good reason tend to land well.

Real automation fluency. Manual testing alone is unlikely to pass the technical round. You should be able to speak with confidence about at least one automation framework, how you structured your test suites, and how your work connected to a CI pipeline. Being caught out on a tool listed on your resume is one of the most common ways interviews go wrong.

Clear communication under pressure. QA Engineers work across engineering, product, and sometimes business teams. Candidates who can explain a defect clearly to a non-technical stakeholder, defend a test decision with data, and handle disagreement without friction stand out from the field.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Start with fundamentals and tools in your first week. Revisit your core automation framework. Write or re-run a small test suite from scratch so the syntax and structure are fresh. Review foundational QA concepts: boundary value analysis, equivalence partitioning, exploratory testing, and API testing basics.

Move to scenario practice in your second week. Take a real app you use every day, such as a food delivery or payments app, and write a test plan for one feature. Walk yourself through edge cases out loud. Record yourself answering two or three behavioural questions using STAR and check whether your answers are tight and specific.

Research Higher People before each round. Look into what products or services the company builds so you can tie your testing examples to their domain. Candidates report that showing you understand the kind of software they ship makes a strong impression in the final round.

On the day, bring examples. Have one or two real test artifacts in mind: a test plan you wrote, a notable bug report, or an automation script you built. Even if you are not asked for them directly, having them sharp in memory improves your answers.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Talking only about manual testing. If every answer focuses on manual test cases, most Higher People panels will flag this as a gap. Even if manual work is your strength, show that you understand automation and have hands-on exposure to at least one framework.

Vague bug-finding stories. Saying 'I found many bugs in my previous role' without a specific example is weak. Prepare at least two concrete bug stories with context, how you discovered the issue, its impact, and how it was resolved.

Testing only the happy path in live exercises. In hands-on rounds, candidates commonly test only the standard flow. Interviewers watch specifically for whether you naturally think about empty states, invalid inputs, network failures, and concurrent edge cases.

Overstating tool experience. If a tool appears on your resume, expect a technical follow-up. Be honest about your depth. Saying 'I used it in one project and here is what I built' is far stronger than being caught out on a tool you barely touched.

Jumping in without clarifying scope. When given a scenario to test, starting immediately without asking about user type, platform, or expected behaviour is a red flag. Asking good clarifying questions is part of what the panel is evaluating.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many interview rounds does Higher People typically have for QA Engineers?

Candidates typically report 3 to 4 rounds. These usually include a recruiter or HR screen, a technical round on QA concepts and tools, a practical or hands-on exercise, and a final round with a senior engineer or hiring manager. The structure can vary by team, so confirm the process with your recruiter after each stage so you are not caught off guard by what comes next.

What salary can I expect as a QA Engineer at Higher People?

Based on knok jobradar data for the Indian market, QA Engineer salaries currently range from 4-9 LPA at entry level (0-2 years), 9-17 LPA at mid-level (3-5 years), 17-30 LPA at senior level (6-9 years), and 28-45+ LPA at the lead level. For Higher People specifically, the actual number depends on your experience band, the team, and negotiation. Glassdoor and levels.fyi can give you a company-specific benchmark to anchor your ask.

Do I need coding skills to pass the Higher People QA Engineer interview?

You do not need to be a software developer, but basic coding ability is typically expected at this level. You should be comfortable reading and writing simple scripts in Python or JavaScript and understand how automation frameworks like Selenium or Cypress work at a structural level. Candidates who can write a basic automated test during a live exercise tend to clear technical rounds more easily than those who rely on manual testing alone.

Which automation tools should I focus on for this interview?

Focus on one tool you have genuine project experience with rather than cramming several. Selenium with Python or Java, Cypress for web testing, and Appium for mobile are the most commonly cited frameworks in Indian QA job descriptions. Knowing how your chosen tool connects to a CI system like Jenkins or GitHub Actions is a bonus that interviewers often probe for in a follow-up question.

How competitive is the QA Engineer market in India right now?

The market is active. Knok jobradar shows 459 QA Engineer openings across India as of mid-2026, with the highest concentration in Bangalore at 87 roles and Delhi at 67 roles. Higher People alone has 42 open roles, which suggests active scaling. Competition at the senior and lead levels is higher, so preparation on automation depth and system-level thinking matters more as you move up the experience ladder.

How should I talk about a past project where quality was poor or QA was not done well?

Be honest but forward-looking. Interviewers value self-awareness and expect that not every project was a success story. Describe the situation briefly, focus on what you personally learned from it, and explain what you would do differently now. Avoid blaming teammates or management. The strongest answers show that a difficult experience changed your approach to testing going forward, which signals ownership and growth rather than finger-pointing.
