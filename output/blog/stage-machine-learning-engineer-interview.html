01 Overview

Overview

Stage is a regional OTT platform that streams content in Haryanvi, Rajasthani, Bhojpuri, and other Indian languages, built for audiences in smaller cities and towns. ML Engineers at Stage typically work on problems meaningfully different from those at mainstream platforms: recommendation with sparse user history, content discovery in low-resource languages, watch-time modeling for short-form regional video, and churn prediction for mobile-first users on variable connectivity.

As of July 2026, Stage had 159 open roles tracked on knok jobradar, pointing to active hiring across engineering functions. The ML Engineer interview process candidates report typically involves a recruiter screening, an ML fundamentals and coding round, a take-home or live case study, and one or two rounds covering system design and past experience. Confirm the current structure with your recruiter, as it can vary by team and seniority level.

Because Stage operates with a lean team and serves an audience that generates different signals from mainstream users, interviewers place a premium on pragmatism, product sense, and comfort working with limited labeled data. Generic textbook answers score lower than answers that engage directly with Stage's product constraints.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions reflect Stage's product context and the challenges candidates report being asked about:

  1. How would you build a content recommendation system for a regional OTT platform where user history is thin and content is in languages with limited NLP tooling?
  2. Walk us through how you handle the cold start problem for a brand-new user who has watched nothing yet.
  3. How do you choose between collaborative filtering, content-based filtering, and a hybrid approach given Stage's niche audience?
  4. Stage users are often on slower mobile networks in Tier 2 and Tier 3 cities. How does that shape your choice of model and inference strategy?
  5. Describe how you would build a churn prediction model to flag users at risk of dropping off in their first week on the platform.
  6. How would you design and measure an A/B test to evaluate whether a new recommendation algorithm actually improves retention?
  7. How do you detect and handle concept drift in a watch-time or engagement prediction model when trending content shifts rapidly?
  8. You have very few labeled training examples for auto-tagging regional video by genre, mood, or dialect. How do you approach this?
  9. How would you build a content discovery feature for a language that has almost no pre-trained models available?
  10. Stage runs a lean engineering team. Walk us through how you decide between a simple model you can ship quickly versus a more accurate one that takes much longer.
  11. Describe a time your model performed well in offline evaluation but did not hold up in production. What did you do?
  12. How would you monitor a live recommendation model and decide when it is time to retrain?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time your model performed well in offline evaluation but did not hold up in production. What did you do?

*Situation:* At a previous company, I built a watch-time prediction model for short-form video. Offline AUC looked strong on our holdout set and the team approved the rollout.

*Task:* After deployment, the business metric we cared about, which was users who watched a video past a meaningful completion point, did not improve despite the good offline numbers.

*Action:* I investigated the feature distributions between training data and live traffic. The training set over-represented power users who watch in the evening on Wi-Fi, while production traffic was dominated by casual daytime mobile users who behave differently. I re-stratified the training data to match the production distribution, added session context features like time of day and connection type, and changed the offline evaluation metric to one aligned with actual user behavior rather than a generic ranking score. I also set up a weekly feature drift check so the team would catch similar gaps early.

*Result:* The business metric improved and the gap between offline and online scores narrowed. The lesson I took away was to always verify that your training distribution matches the people actually using the product.

---

Q: How would you build a churn prediction model for users in their first week on the platform?

*Situation:* At an edtech startup I worked at, we had high drop-off in the first week but no early-warning system to act on it.

*Task:* I was asked to build a model that could flag at-risk users early enough for the CRM team to send a targeted nudge before the user was already gone.

*Action:* I defined churn as zero sessions in the second half of the first week. I built features from the first few days of activity: number of sessions, total watch time, content categories browsed, and whether the user had shared any content. I trained a gradient boosting classifier and calibrated the probability scores so CRM could apply a threshold to build their alert list. I kept the feature pipeline as a daily batch job because we had no real-time infrastructure, and that was the right call for the team size at the time.

*Result:* The high-risk segment identified by the model was actionable enough for CRM to run a push notification experiment, which showed positive early retention signals. Candidates report Stage values this kind of end-to-end thinking, from raw features through to a concrete business action.

---

Q: Walk us through how you handle cold start for a new user.

*Situation:* A streaming platform I worked on launched a new content category and had no prior user history for it, so recommendations defaulted to generic popular content.

*Task:* I needed recommendations to be useful from the very first session, not just after the user had built up enough history for the model to learn from.

*Action:* I split the problem into two phases. In the first session, I used a short onboarding flow with a few quick taps to collect language preference and a single genre signal, then served a curated editorial playlist as a fallback ranker. From the second session onward, I blended early implicit signals (completion rate, scrolling past a card) with item popularity within the user's language group. I also built a metadata-only ranker for cases where even implicit signals were missing, so the system never fell back to purely random ordering.

*Result:* Early-session retention improved. The key insight I shared with the team was that cold start is as much a product design problem as a modeling problem. Getting even one explicit signal in the first session cuts the cold start period significantly.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For ML system design questions (for example, 'build a recommendation engine for Stage'): structure your answer in four parts. First, clarify the business goal and the metric that actually matters to the product (watch time, next-day retention, revenue per user). Second, describe the data you have and its gaps (sparse labels, cold start, limited language tooling). Third, walk through your model choice and explain why it fits the constraints of the team and infrastructure. Fourth, describe how you would evaluate and monitor the system after it is live.

For behavioral questions (for example, 'tell me about a time you improved a model'): use the STAR format. Situation (one or two sentences of context), Task (what you personally owned), Action (the specific steps you took, using 'I' rather than 'we'), Result (a concrete outcome tied to a business or model metric). Spend most of your time on Action and Result, not on setup.

For debugging and production questions (for example, 'your model failed in production'): follow a diagnosis-first pattern. Start with what signal told you something was wrong, describe how you isolated the root cause (data drift, distribution shift, label leakage, feature mismatch), explain what you changed, and close with what monitoring you put in place to prevent recurrence.

For constraint-based questions (Stage-specific, for example, 'lean team, Tier 2 users, limited labeled data'): acknowledge the constraint explicitly before proposing a solution. Interviewers at companies like Stage want to see that you size your solution to actual reality, not to a hypothetical large-company setup. A simple model you can maintain alone is often the right answer, and saying so clearly is a strength.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Stage interviewers typically look for a few qualities that reflect both the company's scale and its product focus on regional India.

Product instinct. Candidates who connect their model choices to user outcomes (retention, watch time, content discovery) rather than just technical metrics stand out. Interviewers want to see that you understand why the metric matters, not just how to move it.

Comfort with sparse and noisy data. Stage's regional content library and Tier 2 and Tier 3 user base mean labeled data is thin and behavior patterns differ from what you see on mainstream platforms. Experience with weak supervision, multilingual transfer learning, or rule-based fallbacks when data is limited is valued.

Pragmatism over sophistication. A lean engineering team cannot run or maintain a complex multi-stage neural serving pipeline. Candidates who propose the simplest solution that meets the business goal, and can defend that choice under questioning, do better than those who reach for architectures that require a large platform team to support.

End-to-end ownership. Candidates report that Stage values engineers who have taken a model from problem definition through to a production metric moving. If your experience has been mostly offline experimentation, be ready to discuss deployment, monitoring, and retraining in depth.

Clear communication. Regional OTT is not a deeply technical domain for product stakeholders. Being able to explain model decisions to a non-ML product manager, without jargon, is a quality interviewers at Stage have noted as important.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: ML fundamentals and Stage product context

Review the core concepts interviewers typically test: bias-variance tradeoff, regularization, how gradient boosting differs from neural approaches, and evaluation metrics relevant to ranking problems (precision, recall, NDCG). At the same time, spend real time in the Stage app. Watch a few videos, observe how content is surfaced, and think about what signals the recommendation system might be using. Concrete product observations make your answers land better than textbook examples.

Week 2: Recommendation systems and low-resource NLP

Study collaborative filtering, matrix factorization, and two-tower retrieval models. Read about how platforms handle cold start in practice (popularity fallbacks, onboarding flows, content-based priors). For NLP, look into multilingual transfer learning and weak supervision techniques, since regional languages have thin pre-trained tooling. Practice explaining these topics out loud to someone who is not an ML engineer.

Week 3: System design and production ML

Practice designing a recommendation or churn system end to end: data pipeline, feature engineering, model selection, serving architecture (batch versus real-time), offline evaluation, and live monitoring. Prepare STAR stories for two or three past projects, focusing on moments where you dealt with messy data, a production failure, or a constraint that forced a creative solution.

Week 4: Mock interviews and final research

Do at least two timed mock interviews, one ML design and one behavioral. Check Glassdoor and LinkedIn for recently shared interview experiences from Stage candidates. Prepare two or three questions to ask your interviewer about the current ML stack and the team's biggest open problems. 07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Over-engineering the solution. Proposing a real-time feature store and multi-stage neural ranking pipeline when a batch job and a simple gradient boosting model would meet the goal is a red flag at a startup. Always match solution complexity to team size and infrastructure reality.

Ignoring the regional content angle. Generic answers about recommendation systems that could apply to any large streaming platform miss Stage's actual context. Weave in the sparse-data, low-resource-language, and Tier 2 user constraints to show you have engaged with the product.

Optimizing for offline metrics only. Talking only about AUC or RMSE without connecting to a business outcome (watch time, retention, revenue per user) suggests you have not shipped models to production. Always close your design answers with what business metric you would track.

Using 'we' throughout your behavioral answers. Interviewers want to know what you personally did. Using 'we' throughout makes it impossible to assess your individual contribution. Switch to 'I' for your actions, even if the project was a team effort.

Not asking clarifying questions in ML design rounds. Jumping straight into a solution without asking about data availability, latency requirements, or team constraints looks like you are reciting a template. Ask one or two scoping questions first to show you think before you build.

Skipping the monitoring and retraining discussion. Many candidates stop at model training. Stage, like most product companies, cares deeply about what happens after deployment. Be ready to discuss drift detection, retraining triggers, and how you would roll back a model that degrades in production.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Stage ML Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically involves three or four rounds: a recruiter or HR screening, an ML coding or fundamentals test, a case study or take-home assignment, and a final round covering system design and past experience. The exact structure varies by team and seniority level, so confirm the details with your recruiter after you clear the first call. Some candidates report an additional conversation with the hiring manager at the end of the loop.

Is there a take-home assignment in the Stage ML interview?

Several candidates report receiving a take-home ML case study, typically involving a dataset and a problem relevant to content platforms such as ranking, classification, or recommendation. The assignment is usually time-boxed. Focus on a clear problem framing, clean code, and a written explanation of your metric choices rather than squeezing out the last fraction of model performance. Interviewers at startups typically care more about how you think than about a perfect score.

What programming tools should I prepare for?

Python is the standard expectation for ML roles at Stage. Candidates report being tested on pandas, NumPy, scikit-learn, and at least one deep learning framework (PyTorch is commonly cited for newer roles). SQL questions come up frequently, especially for feature engineering and data analysis problems. Familiarity with cloud ML services is a plus but typically not a hard requirement at the early screening stage.

What salary can I expect for this role at Stage?

Specific salary data for Stage ML Engineer roles is thin in public sources right now. Industry surveys and Glassdoor data for ML Engineers at Indian growth-stage startups commonly show wide ranges depending on years of experience and the candidate's competing offers. The practical advice is to check Glassdoor and levels.fyi for recent data points from Stage employees or comparable companies, and to enter negotiations with a competing offer in hand if possible.

Does Stage require deep learning experience, or is classical ML enough?

Based on what candidates report, Stage values practical ML over academic depth in deep learning. Classical ML, including gradient boosting, matrix factorization, and logistic regression, is core to many of the real problems the team works on. Deep learning experience with two-tower models or transformer-based NLP is a differentiator for content understanding tasks, but candidates have cleared the process without it by showing strong fundamentals and clear product thinking.

How competitive is it to land an ML Engineer role at Stage?

As of July 2026, Stage had 159 open roles across functions, suggesting the company is in an active hiring phase. ML roles tend to be more competitive at any tech company because the qualified candidate pool is smaller relative to demand. Candidates who combine hands-on recommendation or ranking system experience with a clear understanding of consumer product metrics tend to stand out in the Stage process specifically.
