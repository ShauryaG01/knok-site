01 Overview

Overview

FPT Asia Pacific Pte Ltd is the regional hub of FPT Corporation, one of Asia's largest IT services and technology groups. The company delivers digital transformation, software engineering, and managed IT services to enterprise clients across the Asia Pacific region. Their Technical Program Manager (TPM) roles sit at the intersection of client delivery, cross-team coordination, and technical roadmap ownership.

Candidates report that the interview process typically runs two to four rounds, covering a mix of technical program management concepts, behavioral questions, and situational case studies. Rounds commonly include a recruiter screen, a technical or domain discussion with a senior manager, and one or more stakeholder or leadership rounds. Process details vary by team and hiring manager, so treat any round description as a general guide only.

As of July 2026, knok job radar tracked 313 open Technical Program Manager roles across India, with the strongest concentration in Bangalore (41 openings), followed by Delhi (14), Pune (13), Hyderabad (12), and Chennai (5). FPT Asia Pacific alone had 30 open roles at that point, reflecting active hiring in this function.

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly in TPM interviews at IT services and digital transformation firms like FPT Asia Pacific. Prepare a concrete story for each one before your interview.

  1. Walk us through how you have managed a program with delivery teams spread across multiple time zones or geographies.
  1. Describe a time a client changed requirements significantly mid-delivery. How did you handle the impact on scope, timeline, and team morale?
  1. How do you identify and track cross-workstream dependencies in a large, multi-team program? What tools or methods do you use?
  1. Tell us about a technical risk you escalated to leadership or a client. How did you frame it, and what was the outcome?
  1. FPT works with large enterprise clients on long-running engagements. How do you maintain stakeholder alignment when timelines shift?
  1. How do you structure a program kick-off for a new client engagement? What do you make sure to cover in the first two weeks?

  1. How do you measure and report program health to a client who is not deeply technical?

  1. How do you handle a situation where a vendor or third-party integration is causing repeated delays to your program?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR structure (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for every behavioral question. Here are three worked examples tailored to the TPM role.

Q: Tell us about a time you managed a program with teams across multiple locations.

*Situation:* I was running a digital onboarding platform for a large financial services client. The frontend team was in Pune, the backend team was in Hyderabad, and the client's business analysts were based in Singapore.

*Task:* I needed to keep all three groups moving in sync toward a fixed go-live deadline, despite the time zone spread between India and Singapore.

*Action:* I set up a shared program tracker with daily async status updates so no one had to wait for a live call to unblock themselves. I ran one weekly call timed to overlap all three locations and kept it focused with a tight agenda. I also created a dependency log that each team lead updated every Monday, so I could spot cross-team blockers before they became critical.

*Result:* We hit the go-live date with zero critical defects at launch. The client cited the dependency tracking approach in their post-launch review as a best practice they wanted to carry into future projects.

---

Q: Describe a situation where a client changed requirements significantly mid-delivery.

*Situation:* Midway through a government portal modernisation program, the client's policy team mandated a new accessibility standard that had not been in the original scope. This affected a significant portion of the front-end components already in development.

*Task:* I had to assess the impact, communicate it honestly to the client without damaging trust, and find a path forward that did not derail the rest of the program.

*Action:* I ran a rapid impact workshop with the tech lead and design lead, then prepared a clear options paper for the client: a scope extension with a revised timeline, a phased approach where accessibility was delivered in a follow-on sprint, or a scope swap that deferred lower-priority features. I presented the options with timeline implications for each, without recommending one outright, so the client felt in control of the decision.

*Result:* The client chose the phased approach. We delivered the core platform on the original date and completed the accessibility uplift a few weeks later. The client signed a follow-on contract the following quarter.

---

Q: Tell us about a technical risk you escalated to leadership.

*Situation:* During integration testing on an ERP migration, our team discovered that the client's legacy data had inconsistent formats that our migration scripts could not handle reliably. The go-live was three weeks away.

*Task:* I needed to escalate the risk clearly to both internal leadership and the client sponsor without causing panic, and get a decision on the path forward quickly.

*Action:* I prepared a one-page risk brief that described the issue in plain language, noted the volume of data records affected, and laid out three resolution options with time and resource estimates. I requested a short call with both the delivery director and the client's project sponsor the same afternoon. I was direct about the risk to the go-live date but came with options, not just a problem.

*Result:* The client approved an additional data-cleansing sprint. We pushed go-live by two weeks, which was far better than a failed launch. The client later said the early, structured escalation increased their confidence in the team.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

A few frameworks that work well for TPM interviews at IT services companies.

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the baseline for any behavioral question. Keep the Situation and Task brief (one or two sentences each) and spend most of your time on the Action, since that is where your judgment and skills show.

RACI for stakeholder questions. When asked how you manage alignment or accountability, walk through who was Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed. This shows structured thinking without jargon overload.

The 'options paper' approach. For questions about handling blockers, scope changes, or escalations, show that you bring choices rather than just problems. Frame your answer as: here was the issue, here were the options I laid out, here is how the decision was made. This mirrors real program management under pressure.

Program health metrics. If asked how you report status, name the three or four signals you track: schedule variance, open risks and their status, dependency health, and budget burn rate (if applicable). This shows you manage by data, not gut feel.

The 'first two weeks' opener. For questions about starting a new engagement or program, describe your first actions: understand the brief, meet every key stakeholder, map dependencies, and establish a communication cadence. This shows onboarding confidence, which matters a lot in client-facing IT services roles.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

At an IT services firm like FPT Asia Pacific, the TPM role is client-facing and delivery-accountable. Interviewers are screening for a specific mix of signals.

Client communication confidence. Can you translate technical problems into business language a client sponsor understands? Can you have an uncomfortable conversation (delayed timeline, scope risk) calmly and constructively? This comes up in almost every round.

Cross-team coordination experience. FPT runs distributed programs with onshore client teams and offshore delivery teams. Interviewers want evidence that you have done this before and have a repeatable method, not just a positive attitude.

Structured thinking under pressure. TPMs at IT services companies deal with ambiguity daily. Interviewers watch for whether you break problems down logically or get flustered. Use frameworks naturally, not robotically, to show structured thinking.

Delivery ownership. There is a difference between tracking a program and owning it. Interviewers want to see that you treat the outcome as yours, not just the process.

Stakeholder management maturity. Can you manage up (to a client sponsor or internal VP) and across (to workstream leads who do not report to you) at the same time? Give examples from both directions.

Agile and hybrid delivery fluency. Candidates report that many FPT engagements use some form of agile or scaled agile. Be ready to discuss sprint ceremonies, release planning, and how you connect sprint-level work to program-level milestones.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused seven-day plan to walk into the FPT Asia Pacific TPM interview with confidence.

Day 1: Understand the company context. Read FPT Corporation's publicly available annual reports and press releases. Note the industries they serve (banking, manufacturing, logistics, public sector) and any recent partnerships or wins. This helps you tailor your examples to their client base.

Day 2: Map your experience to the role. List the five or six programs you are most proud of and write two or three STAR bullets for each. Identify which ones show distributed team management, client escalation handling, scope change response, and process improvement.

Day 3: Practice behavioral questions. Run through the 12 questions listed above. Time yourself. Aim for answers between two and three minutes each. Record yourself once and check whether your Action section is specific enough.

Day 4: Sharpen your program metrics story. Be ready to name the exact tools you used (Jira, Smartsheet, MS Project, or a custom dashboard) and describe one specific metric you introduced or improved. Vague answers here are a red flag for experienced interviewers.

Day 5: Prepare your questions for the interviewer. Two or three thoughtful questions show genuine interest. Good ones include: how do TPMs at FPT typically engage with the client delivery sponsor, what does the first three months look like for a new TPM, and what does success look like at the one-year mark.

Day 6: Case or scenario practice. Ask a colleague to give you a live delivery scenario (for example, a critical dependency has just broken two weeks before go-live) and practice talking through your response in real time. This is the closest thing to a live case round.

Day 7: Logistics and mindset. Confirm interview details, prepare a short one-page cheat sheet of your key stories for reference, and get a good night of sleep. Fatigue is the most common cause of blank-mind moments mid-interview.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Giving vague answers without outcomes. Saying 'I managed a large program' means nothing without a concrete result. Every STAR answer needs a measurable or directional outcome, even something like 'we cut the escalation rate by roughly half over two quarters'.

Confusing project management with program management. A TPM role is about managing interdependencies across multiple projects or workstreams, not just delivering a single project on time. Make sure your examples show that cross-program scope.

Skipping the client angle. At an IT services company, every program has a paying client. Interviewers notice quickly when candidates only talk about internal teams and never mention client communication or satisfaction.

Over-describing the situation and under-describing the action. Candidates often spend two minutes setting up the story and thirty seconds on what they actually did. Flip the ratio.

Using too much internal jargon from your last employer. Acronyms and internal tool names that only existed at your previous company confuse interviewers and make it harder for them to evaluate your actual skills.

Not preparing questions for the interviewer. Saying you have no questions at the end of a round is consistently read as a lack of genuine interest, especially in client-facing roles where curiosity is a daily job requirement.

Claiming full ownership of outcomes you only partially influenced. Experienced interviewers probe for your specific contribution. Saying 'I contributed to' or 'my workstream delivered' builds more trust than overstating your role.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the FPT Asia Pacific TPM interview typically have?

Candidates report that the process typically runs two to four rounds. This commonly includes a recruiter or HR screen, a technical or domain discussion with a senior manager or delivery lead, and one or more rounds with leadership or the hiring panel. The exact structure varies by team and location, so confirm the format with your recruiter after the first call.

Does FPT Asia Pacific ask technical coding or system design questions for TPM roles?

TPM interviews at IT services companies are generally not hands-on coding rounds. Candidates report that the technical discussion focuses on your ability to understand and communicate technical concepts, manage technical risks, and work credibly with engineering teams. You may be asked how you handled a specific technical problem or architecture tradeoff, but you are not expected to write code or solve algorithmic puzzles.

What salary can a TPM expect at FPT Asia Pacific in India?

FPT Asia Pacific does not publish salary bands publicly. Glassdoor and industry surveys for TPM roles at mid-to-large IT services firms in India show a wide range depending on years of experience, city, and the size of programs managed. Bangalore typically shows higher compensation than other cities, consistent with the higher job concentration in the data. Use Glassdoor or levels.fyi to benchmark current ranges before entering any negotiation.

How should I prepare for a program management case or scenario question?

Treat a scenario question like a real client conversation. Think out loud, ask one or two clarifying questions before answering, and then walk through your response with a clear structure: what information you would gather first, what risks you would flag, and what options you would present to stakeholders. Interviewers are evaluating your thinking process as much as your conclusion. Practice with a colleague giving you an unexpected scenario the day before your interview.

Is prior experience in FPT's client industries important?

Domain knowledge in banking, manufacturing, logistics, or public sector (common FPT client verticals) is a plus but is rarely a hard requirement for the TPM role. Interviewers typically care more about your ability to ramp up quickly and manage clients in unfamiliar domains. Showing that you have done this before, for example moving from a retail program to a fintech program at a previous employer, is a strong signal of adaptability.
