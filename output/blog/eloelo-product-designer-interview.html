03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use these as templates. Replace the specifics with your own projects, but keep the structure: situation, task, action, result.

Q: Tell me about a time you improved retention on a consumer app.

*Situation:* I was working on a short-video app where users were dropping off after their first session. Data showed most people left quickly when they opened the app for the second time.
*Task:* My goal was to redesign the second-session experience to give returning users a clear reason to stay.
*Action:* I ran a short diary study with a small group of users to understand what they expected when they came back. I found they wanted to see new content from creators they had already engaged with, not a generic feed. I redesigned the home screen to surface 'continue watching' and 'creator updates' above the fold, and worked with engineers to make sure this state persisted across sessions.
*Result:* After the redesign launched, the team tracked a meaningful improvement in second-session duration, which the product lead cited in the next review cycle. I documented the research findings so future features could build on the same insight.

Q: How do you design for users in Tier 2 and Tier 3 cities?

*Situation:* At a previous company, we were building a live quiz feature and assumed most users were on fast connections. A support spike from smaller cities told us something was wrong.
*Task:* I was asked to audit the experience under low-bandwidth conditions and propose fixes.
*Action:* I set up a throttled testing environment and walked through every screen as if I were on a slow connection. I identified several points where the UI froze waiting for data. I redesigned those states to show skeleton loaders and cached the last-known leaderboard so users always saw something useful. I also reduced the animations that fired on load.
*Result:* The engineering team shipped the changes over a couple of sprints. Support feedback for that feature dropped noticeably, and we added a low-bandwidth testing step to our design review checklist going forward.

Q: Describe a time engineering constraints changed your design.

*Situation:* I had designed a real-time notification system for a gaming app that showed personalised alerts based on user behaviour. The engineering team said this approach would add too much latency for a live product.
*Task:* I needed to deliver a useful experience within the technical limits available.
*Action:* I spent a day with the lead engineer to understand what was feasible client-side versus server-side. Based on that, I simplified the notification logic to use a small set of fixed categories instead of dynamic personalisation, and used local state to make the UI feel instant even when the server was slightly behind. I also added a 'you missed this' summary screen that loaded asynchronously so nothing blocked the live game view.
*Result:* The feature shipped on time. Users did not notice the difference between the original design and the simplified version, confirming that the dynamic personalisation was nice-to-have, not critical.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Having a repeatable structure helps you stay calm under pressure and gives interviewers a clear signal that you think systematically.

For product thinking questions (how would you design X): Start with the user. Name the user segment, describe their goal, and name one or two pain points. Then describe the experience you would create, the tradeoffs you considered, and how you would measure success. Interviewers at companies like eloelo care about whether you connect design decisions to user behaviour and business impact, not just whether the screens look good.

For portfolio walkthrough questions: Use a simple arc: context (what was the problem and who did it affect), process (how you approached it, including dead ends), decision (what you shipped and why), outcome (what changed and what you learned). Avoid narrating slides. Tell the story.

For 'tell me about a time' questions: Use STAR: Situation (brief context), Task (your specific responsibility), Action (what you personally did, in detail), Result (what changed because of your work). Keep Situation and Task short. Spend most of your time on Action and Result.

For metric and success questions: Name a north-star metric tied to user behaviour, not vanity metrics like app installs. Then name one or two supporting metrics that tell you whether the north star is moving for the right reasons. For a retention feature on eloelo, you might track daily active users as the north star and session length plus creator follow rate as supporting signals.

For constraint and tradeoff questions: Acknowledge the constraint clearly, describe how you diagnosed it, explain the option you chose and why, and name what you gave up. Interviewers want to see that you are comfortable making decisions under ambiguity, not that you always find a perfect answer.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

eloelo is a live, real-time product with a large audience in smaller Indian cities. Based on what candidates report, interviewers are typically looking for a few specific qualities.

Deep empathy for the Indian user. Can you talk about Tier 2 and Tier 3 users without being prompted? Do you treat low-end devices, regional languages, and data-light experiences as defaults, not edge cases?

Comfort with live and real-time UX. Designing for a live game is different from designing a static feed. Interviewers want to see that you understand latency, error states, and the emotional stakes of something happening in real time.

A clear design process, not just polished outputs. They want to understand how you get from a problem to a solution. Bring portfolio work that shows your thinking, including the wrong turns.

Product judgment. Product Designers at eloelo are expected to have opinions about what to build, not just how to build it. Be ready to talk about prioritisation, tradeoffs, and success metrics without being prompted.

Collaboration under pressure. Live products ship fast. Interviewers look for candidates who can work with engineers and PMs without needing complete information, and who can simplify a design without losing the core insight.

Genuine curiosity about the product. Use the app before your interview. Have a point of view on what works and what could be better. Candidates who have spent real time in the product stand out in the first few minutes of any conversation.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused two-week plan based on what candidates report from eloelo interviews.

Week 1: Product immersion and portfolio prep

Spend real time in the eloelo app. Play games, watch creators, go through onboarding as a new user, and note every friction point and moment of delight. Identify a few things you would change and be ready to defend your reasoning. In parallel, select a handful of portfolio projects that show engagement, retention, or real-time design challenges. For each, prepare a short verbal walkthrough: the problem, your process (including dead ends), the decision you made, and the outcome.

Week 2: Practice and design exercises

A common interview format at product-first companies is a live design exercise where you are given a prompt and asked to think out loud. Practice this out loud, not just in your head. Pick a feature you would add to a social gaming app and work through it: who is the user, what is their goal, what are a few possible solutions, which do you pick and why, how do you measure success. Also prepare for questions about Tier 2 and Tier 3 users, low-bandwidth design, and real-money trust signals.

Research to do before your interview: Look up publicly reported news about eloelo's product direction and growth milestones. Understand how the platform earns revenue. Know who the competitors are and what makes eloelo different. This context makes your answers land with more authority.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

These are the patterns that typically hurt candidates in Product Designer interviews at companies like eloelo.

Showing only polished final designs. If your portfolio only shows beautiful screens, interviewers cannot see how you think. Bring the messy middle: sketches, rejected directions, constraints you worked around.

Designing for an abstract user. Vague answers like 'the user wants simplicity' signal shallow thinking. Be specific: name the segment, describe their context, and cite something you observed or tested.

Skipping the metric. Every design decision should connect to a measurable outcome. If you cannot say how you would know whether your design worked, the answer feels incomplete to the interviewer.

Narrating instead of deciding. Some candidates walk through a long process and never land on a recommendation. Interviewers want to see you make a call, even under ambiguity. Pick a direction and own it.

Not using the product. It is obvious in the interview when a candidate has not used eloelo. You will struggle to answer questions about specific flows, and you will miss the chance to show genuine curiosity.

Overcomplicating the design exercise. Under time pressure, candidates sometimes pile on features to show range. A simple, well-reasoned solution beats a complex one that is half-explained. Pick a direction, defend it, and name the tradeoffs clearly.
