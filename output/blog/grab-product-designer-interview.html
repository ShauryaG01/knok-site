01 Overview

Overview

Grab is Southeast Asia's leading super-app, with products spanning ride-hailing, food delivery, digital payments, and more across eight markets. Their design team is large and cross-functional, and they look for designers who can think beyond aesthetics to solve real problems for diverse, multilingual user bases.

Candidates typically report 3-4 interview rounds: a recruiter or HR call, a portfolio walkthrough with the design team, a live case study or design challenge, and a final round with senior designers or cross-functional partners. The process typically spans 3-6 weeks from first contact to offer.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Questions below reflect what candidates report seeing in Grab product design interviews. They skew toward systems thinking, cross-cultural design, and collaboration skills.

  1. Walk us through a product or feature you designed end-to-end. What was the problem, and how did you measure success?
  2. Grab serves users across Southeast Asia with very different digital habits and literacy levels. How do you design for that kind of diversity?
  3. Tell us about a time you disagreed with a PM or engineer over a design decision. What happened, and what did you learn?
  4. Describe your process when requirements are vague or keep changing mid-project.
  5. How do you decide when you have done enough user research to move forward?
  6. Walk us through a design you shipped that you were not fully satisfied with. Why did you ship it, and what did you do next?
  7. How do you handle stakeholder feedback when multiple people want different things?
  8. Tell us about a time data or user research forced you to completely change your design direction.
  9. How do you balance user needs with business goals when they pull in opposite directions?
  10. Pick any feature in the Grab app. What would you change about it, and why?
  11. Describe a time you had to simplify a complex user flow. What trade-offs did you make?
  12. How do you communicate design decisions to engineers or non-design stakeholders who are skeptical of your approach?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for behavioral questions. Keep your story under 3 minutes when spoken aloud.

Q: Tell us about a time data or research forced you to change your design direction.

*Situation:* I was redesigning the checkout flow for a fintech app. Our initial design used a multi-step wizard, based on patterns we had seen work in Western markets.

*Task:* Before launch, we ran usability tests with users in tier-2 cities, our primary growth segment.

*Action:* Sessions showed users felt anxious when they could not see all the fields at once. They wanted to review everything before submitting. I proposed replacing the wizard with a single scrollable page and a sticky 'Confirm and Pay' button. To get PM buy-in, I framed the change as a drop-off risk, not a design preference, and backed it with session recordings.

*Result:* The revised flow performed better in our A/B test, with a publicly reported improvement in completion rate on that screen. The PM said the risk-framing approach was what got the change approved quickly.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you disagreed with a PM about a design decision.

*Situation:* We were building an in-app notification center. The PM wanted a single undifferentiated list to ship faster.

*Task:* I believed this would make high-priority alerts easy to miss, which was a safety concern for our driver users.

*Action:* Instead of pushing back in a group meeting, I ran a quick 'what does failure look like' exercise, mapping out user scenarios where the flat list caused harm. I shared this async with the PM before the next sprint planning session, giving them time to think.

*Result:* The PM agreed to add a simple priority flag to alerts. We shipped a version that was fast to build but addressed the core risk. The PM later said the scenario mapping made the issue concrete in a way a design review alone would not have.

---

Q: Describe a design you shipped that you were not fully happy with.

*Situation:* We had a tight deadline for a feature tied to a marketing campaign. My preferred design required a new UI component that engineering said would add two weeks to the timeline.

*Task:* I had to decide between asking for a delay or shipping a simpler version.

*Action:* I audited the existing component library and found a pattern close enough to work with minor copy changes. I documented what was compromised, why we made the call, and logged it as design debt with a specific proposed fix attached.

*Result:* We hit the campaign date. Three months later, I referenced the debt log and got the proper version built in the next planning cycle. The habit of documenting trade-offs meant no one had to rediscover why the interim design existed.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For portfolio walkthroughs: Structure each project as: what was the problem, who were the users, what constraints existed, what options you explored, what you shipped, and what you learned. Grab interviewers typically probe the 'why you chose this over that' moment, so know your key decision points cold.

For live case studies: Use a simple Problem, Explore, Define, Deliver structure. Start by asking clarifying questions (who is the user, what does success look like, what are the constraints). Do not jump to solutions. Candidates report that Grab interviewers pay close attention to how you frame the problem before you sketch anything.

For behavioral questions: STAR works well. Keep Situation and Task brief (combined well under a minute) and spend most of your time on Action and Result. If you do not have a metric for the Result, describe a qualitative signal instead, such as stakeholder reaction, user feedback, or what changed in how the team worked afterward.

For design critique (if asked to review the Grab app): Lead with the user goal the feature is trying to serve. Then describe what works, describe what could be better and why, and propose one concrete change you would test. Avoid pure opinion statements like 'this feels cluttered.' Tie every observation to a user behavior or a business outcome.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Systems thinking over polished screens. Grab is a complex product with many user types: riders, drivers, merchants, and financial services customers. Interviewers want to see that you think about how a design decision in one part of the product affects another.

Cross-cultural empathy. Grab's users range from tech-savvy urban professionals to first-time smartphone users in rural markets. Showing that you have actually designed for low literacy, low bandwidth, or low-end devices will stand out.

Clear decision-making. Every design involves trade-offs. Interviewers are not evaluating only what you built. They are evaluating how you chose between options and whether you can articulate that clearly under pressure.

Strong collaboration with non-designers. Candidates report that Grab places high value on how well you work with PMs and engineers. Stories about co-owning problems with cross-functional partners land better than stories where design worked in isolation.

Ownership beyond handoff. Did you stay involved through implementation? Did you measure what happened after launch? Grab interviewers typically ask follow-up questions to check whether you treat shipping as the end or the beginning.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Polish your portfolio. Pick your 3-4 strongest case studies. For each, write out the problem statement, the constraints, the key decision points, and the result in plain language. Practice telling each story in under 3 minutes. Remove slides that show only final screens with no context.

Week 2: Know Grab's product deeply. Use the Grab app daily if you can. Read publicly available writing from Grab's design and engineering teams. Note which markets they operate in and what user challenges each market presents. Think through what the super-app model creates in terms of design complexity that a single-product company would not face.

Week 3: Practice live case studies. Work through a Grab-relevant prompt in a single sitting, talking out loud: for example, 'How would you improve the first-time experience for Grab Pay users in a market where digital payments are new?' Practice narrating your thinking, not just sketching in silence.

Week 4 (and ongoing): Build your behavioral story bank. List 6-8 stories from your career covering: disagreement with a stakeholder, research changing your direction, shipping under constraints, designing for users very different from yourself, and receiving hard feedback. Map each to STAR before your first interview.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Showing only final screens. Interviewers at Grab want to see your process. If your portfolio shows only polished end states, they cannot assess how you think.

Jumping to solutions in case studies. Candidates who start sketching before asking clarifying questions typically score lower. The first few minutes of a case study should be questions, not wireframes.

Vague results in STAR answers. 'The team was happy with the outcome' is weak. Even without metrics, describe what changed: what did users do differently, what did the PM say, what would you do differently on the next project?

Ignoring Grab's specific context. Candidates who give generic answers about 'users' without acknowledging that Grab's user base is very different from a Silicon Valley audience miss a key signal interviewers are listening for.

Treating interviewer pushback as criticism. If an interviewer challenges your design in a live case study, they are often not saying you are wrong. They are watching how you respond to challenge. Acknowledge the concern, explore it, and adapt if it makes sense.

Not asking questions at the end. Candidates report that Grab interviewers respond well to genuine curiosity about the team, the product challenges, and how design fits into the org. Use your time at the end of each round to learn, not just to close.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Grab Product Designer interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically has 3-4 rounds: a recruiter or HR screen, a portfolio review with the design team, a case study or design challenge round, and a final conversation with senior design or cross-functional stakeholders. Some candidates report an additional culture or values conversation at certain levels. The total timeline is typically 3-6 weeks, though this can vary by team and location.

Does Grab give a take-home design challenge or a live case study?

Candidates report seeing both formats depending on the team and level. Some interview tracks include a take-home exercise where you redesign a feature or solve a product problem over a set timeframe. Others involve a live case study done over video call. It is worth asking your recruiter which format to expect so you can prepare accordingly. Either way, your process and reasoning matter more than the visual output.

What should my Grab design portfolio include?

Focus on 3-4 projects that show your full design process, not just final screens. Include the problem context, who the users were, the constraints you worked under, the options you explored, the decisions you made and why, and what happened after you shipped. Grab interviewers are particularly interested in how you handled ambiguity and worked with non-designers, so make sure at least one case study shows that clearly.

What salary can I expect as a Product Designer at Grab?

Based on knok jobradar data (as of July 2026), Product Designer salaries in India range from 6-12 LPA at entry level (0-2 years), 14-24 LPA at mid-level (3-5 years), 26-40 LPA at senior level (6-9 years), and 36-55+ LPA at Lead or Principal level. Total compensation at Grab typically also includes performance bonuses and equity. Candidates report these components can be meaningful at senior levels, so always ask about the full package during negotiations.

How important is it to know Grab's product before the interview?

Very important. Candidates who have clearly used the Grab app and thought about its design challenges stand out early in the process. You do not need to be a Southeast Asian user: read publicly available product and design writing from Grab, explore features available in your region, and think through what the super-app model means for design complexity. Coming in with one specific, well-reasoned observation about the product is a strong way to open a conversation.

Does Grab hire Product Designers based in India, or are roles only in Southeast Asia?

Grab does hire for India-based design roles, with the most openings in Bangalore and Delhi. knok jobradar data shows 62 open roles in Bangalore and 33 in Delhi across all Grab roles as of July 2026. If you are applying for a role that involves designing for Southeast Asian markets, expect the interview to probe your ability to research and design for user contexts outside your own direct experience.
