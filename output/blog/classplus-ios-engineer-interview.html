03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How did you debug a memory leak in a production iOS app?

*Situation:* At my previous company, we noticed that our video player screen was not being deallocated after users navigated away, causing memory to creep up over extended sessions.

*Task:* I was asked to find the root cause and fix it before the next release, as users on older iPhones were reporting crashes after long periods of use.

*Action:* I used Instruments (Leaks and Allocations tools) to confirm the object was staying alive. I found a closure inside our player delegate that was capturing 'self' strongly, creating a retain cycle between the ViewController and the AVPlayerLayer wrapper. I replaced the strong capture with '[weak self]' and added 'deinit' log statements to confirm deallocation during QA.

*Result:* Memory growth flattened in testing. Post-release, crash reports related to memory pressure dropped noticeably over the following weeks.

---

Q: How would you architect an offline-first feature for a low-connectivity region?

*Situation:* At a previous role, we had to build a homework submission flow for students in areas where connectivity drops frequently.

*Task:* I needed to design a solution that saved submissions locally when offline and synced them reliably once the network returned.

*Action:* I used Core Data to persist submission drafts locally with a sync-status flag. I wrote a background sync service using a URLSession background configuration so uploads could complete even if the app went to the background. I also added a conflict-resolution layer to handle cases where the server already had a newer version of the same submission.

*Result:* Failed submission complaints dropped significantly in user feedback, and our PM reported that engagement in Tier 2 cities improved in the quarter after launch.

---

Q: Walk me through how you write unit tests for a ViewModel.

*Situation:* Our codebase had minimal test coverage on business logic, and bugs in ViewModel state management were causing intermittent UI issues that were hard to reproduce.

*Task:* I took ownership of adding tests for the most critical ViewModels in our fee-payment flow.

*Action:* I introduced a protocol-based dependency injection pattern so the ViewModel could accept a mock API client. I wrote tests covering the happy path (successful fee payment), error states (network failure, invalid card), and edge cases (double-tap prevention). I used XCTest and a lightweight expectations helper for async calls.

*Result:* We caught two regression bugs during the next sprint that would have reached production. The pattern was adopted by the rest of the team for new ViewModels going forward.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For iOS technical questions, start with the 'why' before the 'how'. Interviewers want to see that you understand the problem, not just the API. For example, when asked about GCD vs Operation queues, first explain what problem concurrency solves, then describe the trade-offs.

For system design questions, use a simple structure: clarify requirements, define components, explain data flow, then discuss failure modes. For a live-class feature, walk through signalling (how clients connect), media delivery (HLS vs WebRTC), state management on the client, and what happens when the connection drops.

For behavioural questions, use STAR: Situation (one sentence of context), Task (what you specifically needed to do), Action (your concrete steps, not 'we'), Result (measurable or observable outcome). Keep Situation and Task short. Spend most of your time on Action and Result.

For trade-off questions (UIKit vs SwiftUI, MVVM vs MVC), give a direct answer first, then explain the context that would change your choice. Saying 'it depends' without a follow-up reads as evasive.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Classplus builds a product that needs to work across a wide range of devices, from flagship iPhones to older models used by students in smaller cities. Interviewers look for engineers who think about real-world constraints, not just ideal conditions.

Product empathy matters here. Candidates who connect their technical choices to user impact, for example saying 'I chose this approach because it reduces load time for users on 4G', consistently stand out based on what candidates report.

Depth over breadth. You do not need to know every Apple framework, but you should know your core areas (memory, concurrency, networking, UI performance) thoroughly. Expect follow-up questions that go two or three levels deep.

Ownership mindset. Classplus is a fast-moving startup. Interviewers tend to favour candidates who describe taking initiative: finding the bug no one else was looking at, proposing the architectural change, shipping the fix without being asked.

Communication. You will be asked to explain your thinking out loud, especially in design rounds. Practice talking through trade-offs step by step rather than jumping straight to a solution.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Swift and iOS fundamentals
Revisit ARC, retain cycles, value vs reference types, and Swift concurrency (async/await, actors). Write small test programs to confirm your mental models. Review URLSession, Codable, and Core Data basics.

Week 2: Architecture and system design
Practice designing mobile features end-to-end: a video player, an offline sync system, a payment flow. For each, write out components, data flow, and failure modes on paper before checking any reference. Study MVVM and clean architecture patterns as they apply to iOS.

Week 3: Classplus-specific prep
Download and use the Classplus app as a student and as a tutor. Note the features you would be most excited to build or improve. Think about the technical challenges behind each screen. Prepare two or three stories from your past work that map to Classplus's domain (ed-tech, content delivery, payments, live features).

Week 4: Mock interviews and gaps
Do at least two mock technical interviews with a friend or on a practice platform. Record yourself answering behavioural questions and review for filler words and clarity. Fix any weak areas you discover.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping the 'why'. Many candidates describe what they did without explaining why they made that choice. Interviewers at Classplus want to see reasoning, not just familiarity with APIs.

Saying 'we' instead of 'I' in STAR answers. When you say 'we fixed the memory leak', the interviewer cannot tell what you personally contributed. Always describe your specific role and actions.

Not knowing the Classplus product. Candidates who have never opened the app struggle to connect their technical skills to real problems. Spend time with the product before your rounds.

Over-engineering design answers. Jumping to a distributed architecture for a feature that will serve a small initial user base can seem out of touch with startup constraints. Start simple, then scale your design when the interviewer pushes you.

Leaving bugs unspoken. If you spot a flaw in your own solution while explaining it, say so. Interviewers value self-awareness. Silently hoping they do not notice rarely works.

Freezing on unknown questions. If you do not know something, say what you do know and reason from there. 'I have not used that API directly, but based on how URLSession handles background tasks, I would expect...' shows a stronger engineering mind than a blank pause.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Classplus iOS interview typically have?

Candidates typically report 3-4 rounds: a recruiter or HR call, one or two technical rounds covering Swift fundamentals and system design, and a final round with a hiring manager or senior team member. The exact structure can vary by team and role level. It is fine to ask your recruiter for the expected format after you clear the first call.

Is LeetCode-style DSA a big part of the Classplus iOS interview?

Candidates report that Classplus iOS rounds lean more toward practical iOS knowledge (memory management, architecture, debugging) than pure algorithmic puzzles. That said, basic data structures and complexity analysis can come up, especially in early rounds. Focus the majority of your prep on iOS-specific topics, but do not ignore fundamentals entirely.

What salary can I expect for an iOS Engineer at Classplus?

Classplus does not publish salary bands publicly. For mid-senior iOS roles at comparable Indian ed-tech companies, compensation is commonly cited in the 18-35 LPA range on Glassdoor and levels.fyi, with variation based on experience and role level. Come prepared with a number based on your current CTC, market data from these platforms, and the value you bring.

Does Classplus allow remote work for iOS Engineers?

Classplus is headquartered in Delhi, and most iOS Engineer roles are based there. Candidates report that the company currently prefers in-office or hybrid arrangements, though policies can change. Clarify the work-from-home policy with the recruiter during your first call so there are no surprises later.

How should I prepare for the system design round?

Focus on mobile system design: how you would build features like live video streaming, offline sync, deep linking, or a payment flow. Practice talking through requirements, components, data flow, and failure modes out loud. Connecting your design choices to real constraints (battery life, bandwidth, older devices) shows the kind of thinking Classplus interviewers value.

Is there a coding assignment or take-home test in the process?

Some candidates report receiving a take-home assignment, usually a small iOS project to complete within a day or two. Others go straight to live coding rounds. Ask your recruiter what format to expect so you can prepare accordingly. If there is an assignment, treat it as a production-quality submission: clean code, meaningful commits, and a short README explaining your decisions.
