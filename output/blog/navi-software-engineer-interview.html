01 Overview

Overview

Navi is a Bengaluru-based fintech company building lending, insurance, and investment products for everyday Indians. It is known for an ambitious engineering roadmap and a startup culture where engineers own problems end to end.

As of July 2026, knok jobradar tracked 60 open Software Engineer roles at Navi, sitting inside a much larger national pool of 5,395 Software Engineer openings. Bangalore accounts for 776 of those national openings, making it the clear hiring hub.

Navi's interview process typically runs across multiple stages: an online coding test, one or two technical rounds covering data structures, algorithms, and system design, and a final round that often touches on culture fit and ownership mindset. Candidates report the process is rigorous but conversational, with interviewers who want to understand how you think, not just whether you can produce a correct answer.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions are drawn from patterns candidates report across Navi interviews. They reflect the company's focus on scale, reliability, and product ownership.

  1. Walk me through a system you designed from scratch. What trade-offs did you consciously make?
  2. How do you ensure data consistency when multiple services are writing to the same record?
  3. Describe a production incident you personally helped resolve. How did you find the root cause?
  4. How would you design a loan disbursement system that needs to handle sudden traffic spikes?
  5. Tell me about a time you disagreed with your tech lead or manager on a technical decision.
  6. How would you build a notification service that sends alerts to millions of users without dropping messages?
  7. What does writing testable code mean to you? Give a concrete example from your own work.
  8. Describe a feature you owned end to end, from design to deployment. What was the hardest decision?
  9. How do you decide when to optimise early versus ship and observe?
  10. Have you used message queues or event-driven patterns? Walk me through a real use case.
  11. Your primary database is becoming a bottleneck. How do you diagnose and fix it without a full rewrite?
  12. Tell me about a time you improved the reliability of a system that was failing customers.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use these as templates. Swap in your own projects, tech stack, and outcomes.

Q: Describe a production incident you personally helped resolve.

*Situation:* At my previous company we ran a loan repayment service. On a Monday morning, a subset of customers started seeing failed payment confirmations even though their bank accounts had already been debited.

*Task:* I was on-call and had to find the root cause quickly, since every minute of confusion eroded customer trust on a payments product.

*Action:* I pulled logs across several nights of traffic and spotted a pattern: our idempotency key was colliding for transactions processed in a specific time window after a recent deployment. I traced it to a clock-skew issue between two services updated at slightly different times. I wrote a one-line config fix, tested it on staging, and got it reviewed by a senior engineer before merging.

*Result:* The fix went live within the same shift. We added a regression test to our CI pipeline so the same class of bug could not slip through again. The incident became an internal case study on safe deployment sequencing.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you disagreed with a technical decision.

*Situation:* My team was about to embed a third-party analytics SDK directly into our core payments flow. I felt this introduced an unacceptable latency and data-privacy risk.

*Task:* I needed to raise the concern without blocking the sprint or coming across as obstructive.

*Action:* I wrote a short one-page doc comparing two approaches: embedding the SDK inline versus firing analytics events asynchronously to a queue that a separate consumer would process. I shared latency benchmarks from a local load test and flagged the data residency question with our security lead before the next sprint planning session.

*Result:* The team aligned on the async approach. The feature shipped on time, and the analytics pipeline ran independently without touching the critical payment path. My manager later cited this as a good example of raising a concern with a solution attached, not just a complaint.

---

Q: Walk me through a system you designed from scratch.

*Situation:* We needed an in-app document verification flow for loan applications. Users had to upload ID proofs, and we needed to run OCR and fraud checks before approving the loan.

*Task:* I was the lead engineer for this feature and had to design it to be reliable, asynchronous, and easy to extend with new verification vendors later.

*Action:* I designed a pipeline using an event queue: the upload service published a document-received event, and a set of worker services consumed it, ran checks in parallel, and wrote results to a state machine. Each worker was stateless so we could scale them independently. I chose an optimistic locking pattern on the state machine to prevent race conditions during concurrent updates.

*Result:* The system handled document volumes during our largest marketing campaign without a single processing failure. Adding a new verification vendor later required only a new consumer service, with no changes to existing code.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For system design questions, follow a structure both candidates and interviewers find clear:

  1. Clarify scope: ask about scale, SLA requirements, and what 'done' looks like.
  2. Sketch the high-level components before going deep on any one part.
  3. Call out your trade-offs explicitly (consistency vs. availability, SQL vs. NoSQL, sync vs. async).
  4. Address failure modes: what happens when a service goes down or a message is lost?
  5. End with how you would monitor and alert on the system in production.

For behavioural questions, the STAR format keeps your answer tight:

  • *Situation:* Set the context in two or three sentences.
  • *Task:* State what you personally were responsible for.
  • *Action:* Describe what YOU did, not what the team did. Use 'I', not 'we'.
  • *Result:* Share the outcome. If you have a supporting metric, use it. If not, describe the qualitative impact clearly.

For coding rounds, think aloud before writing. State your brute-force solution first, then optimise. Narrate edge cases you are considering as you go. Navi candidates report that interviewers pay close attention to how you reason, not just whether you reach the optimal solution.

For debugging questions, show a methodical approach: reproduce the issue, isolate variables, form a hypothesis, test it, apply the fix, and add a safeguard so it does not recur.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Ownership over delegation. Navi is a startup. Interviewers want to see that you have taken full responsibility for outcomes, not just your slice of the code. Talk about decisions you made, not just tasks you completed.

First-principles thinking. When faced with a design question, do not jump to a framework or a buzzword. Show that you understand why a particular pattern solves the problem. If you mention Kafka, explain why a queue fits better than a direct API call in this specific scenario.

Comfort with ambiguity. Expect questions where the requirements are deliberately vague. Interviewers are checking whether you ask the right clarifying questions or whether you barrel ahead on assumptions.

Reliability mindset. Navi's products involve real money. Candidates who can speak naturally about idempotency, retries, circuit breakers, and graceful degradation stand out. This is especially true for backend and platform roles.

Clear communication. Fintech products involve legal, compliance, and business stakeholders. Interviewers assess whether you can explain a technical decision to a non-engineer without dumbing it down or overcomplicating it.

Curiosity and learning velocity. Given how fast Navi moves, candidates who ask good questions and show a pattern of picking up new domains quickly are valued over those with deep expertise in only one narrow stack.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Strengthen fundamentals

Revise core data structures (trees, graphs, heaps, hash maps) and practise at least one problem per day on a coding platform. Focus on array manipulation, sliding window, and graph traversal patterns, which candidates report appearing frequently in the early rounds.

Week 2: System design for fintech

Study how payment systems, notification pipelines, and document processing flows are typically built. Practise designing them on paper: draw components, label data flows, and write down failure scenarios. Focus on idempotency, distributed transactions, and event-driven architecture as core topics.

Week 3: Behavioural prep and Navi context

Write down five to six stories from your career using the STAR format. Cover at least: a production incident you owned, a technical disagreement you navigated, a feature you designed end to end, and a time you learned something under pressure. Research Navi's public product updates and any engineering content they have published so you can speak to why you want to join specifically.

Week 4: Mock interviews and gap filling

Do at least two timed mock interviews, one coding and one system design. Review any weak areas that surface. On the day of the interview, re-read the job description and match your best stories to the role's stated responsibilities.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping the clarifying questions in system design. Jumping straight into drawing boxes and arrows without asking about scale, consistency requirements, or user flows is the most commonly reported way candidates lose points in design rounds.

Saying 'we' instead of 'I' in behavioural answers. Interviewers want to understand your specific contribution. If you keep saying 'we decided' or 'we built', they cannot assess your individual impact or ownership.

Only coding, not thinking aloud. Fintech companies like Navi care about how you reason through a problem. Staying silent while you code can make a correct solution look like a guess.

Ignoring edge cases. Not handling null inputs, empty arrays, or concurrent writes in your solution signals that you have not thought about production realities, which matters a lot at a company handling financial transactions.

Generic answers about why you want to join. Saying 'I want to work at a fast-growing startup' is forgettable. Connecting your interest to a specific Navi product, a customer problem they are solving, or a technical challenge you find genuinely interesting is far more convincing.

Under-preparing for debugging and reliability questions. Many candidates over-prepare DSA and under-prepare for questions about production incidents, monitoring, and failure recovery. These questions are common at product-focused fintechs and often carry significant weight.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Navi Software Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report a process that typically includes an online coding assessment, one or two technical interview rounds covering algorithms and system design, and a final round focused on culture fit and ownership mindset. The exact number of rounds can vary by role and seniority level. It is best to confirm the structure with the recruiter at the start of the process so you can prepare accordingly.

Is the Navi coding interview very hard?

Candidates describe the coding rounds as moderately challenging, with a focus on problem-solving clarity rather than trick questions. Topics commonly reported include graph traversal, dynamic programming, and array manipulation. Thinking aloud and explaining your reasoning clearly is as important as reaching the optimal solution, since interviewers are evaluating your thought process.

What system design topics should I focus on for a Navi interview?

Given Navi's focus on lending, insurance, and investments, candidates report that payment systems, notification pipelines, and document processing flows come up frequently. Core concepts to know well include idempotency, event-driven architecture, distributed consistency, and database scaling patterns. Practise drawing these systems on paper and articulating trade-offs out loud before your interview.

What salary can I expect as a Software Engineer at Navi?

Navi does not publicly publish salary bands, so exact figures are difficult to verify independently. Based on knok jobradar data for Software Engineers across India, mid-level engineers (3-5 years of experience) typically see offers in the 15-25 LPA range, while senior engineers (6-9 years) tend to fall in the 28-45 LPA range. Stock options and performance bonuses may also be part of the total package, so it is worth asking the recruiter about compensation structure early in the process.

How should I prepare for Navi's behavioural interview?

Write down five to six career stories in advance and structure each one using the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result). Focus on stories that show full ownership, handling conflict with data and empathy, and learning quickly under pressure. Practise telling each story in under three minutes. Generic answers about wanting growth or impact tend to land poorly; specificity about what you personally did and why is what stands out to interviewers.
