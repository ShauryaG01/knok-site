01 Overview

Overview

toddle is an EdTech SaaS platform built for schools and teachers, focused on curriculum planning, lesson design, student assessment, and parent communication. Its users are educators across international and independent schools in multiple countries. As of mid-2026, the knok job radar tracks 393 open Product Designer roles across India, with Bangalore leading at 62 openings. toddle itself has 24 open roles, reflecting active growth across the company.

A Product Designer at toddle typically works on teacher-facing tools: planning flows, assessment builders, reporting dashboards, and collaboration features. The user base includes educators with a wide range of digital comfort, so the design challenge is often about depth versus approachability, giving experienced users power without overwhelming newer ones.

The role rewards designers who understand how a classroom actually works. If you have used any kind of school management or curriculum tool, or have done primary research with teachers, that context will come through in your interviews and portfolio discussions.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates report the process typically includes a portfolio review, a take-home design assignment, and two or more rounds of interviews covering design thinking, collaboration, and product sense. The questions below reflect themes that come up frequently based on what candidates share publicly.

  1. Walk us through a product or feature you designed end-to-end. What was the problem, who were the users, and how did you reach the final design?
  2. How do you approach designing for users with low digital literacy, such as teachers in smaller schools or Tier 2/3 cities?
  3. toddle serves both teachers and school administrators. How do you handle a situation where their needs conflict within a single interface?
  4. Describe your user research process when you have limited access to actual end users.
  5. How do you decide when a design is 'good enough' to ship versus when it needs more iteration?
  6. Tell us about a design decision that did not work out after launch. What did you do next?
  7. How do you work with engineers when they say a design is too complex to build within the given timeline?
  8. What is your experience with design systems? How have you contributed to or maintained one?
  9. How do you measure whether a design change actually improved the user experience?
  10. How would you approach redesigning a core feature in a mature product where users are already familiar with the old design?
  11. How do you handle feedback from multiple stakeholders (product managers, engineers, customer success) that pulls your design in different directions?
  12. What do you know about toddle's product, and what is one area where you think the experience could be improved?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell us about a design decision that did not work out after launch.

*Situation:* I was designing a progress-tracking dashboard for teachers at a mid-sized EdTech startup.

*Task:* The goal was to give teachers a single-screen overview so they would not have to click through multiple reports.

*Action:* I consolidated everything into one dense dashboard with filters and tabs. I ran usability tests with internal stakeholders and got positive feedback, so we shipped it. After launch, session recordings showed teachers spending a lot of time on the filter panel, often giving up before reaching the data they needed.

*Result:* Follow-up interviews with a small group of teachers revealed the filters were too granular for their mental model. I redesigned with a simpler 'class view first' approach, putting advanced filters behind a secondary panel. Engagement improved and support tickets related to the report section dropped in the following weeks.

---

Q: How do you collaborate with engineers when they push back on a design?

*Situation:* At a previous company, I designed an animated onboarding flow for a new feature. The engineering lead flagged it shortly before the sprint deadline, saying the animation would add significant effort.

*Task:* I had to decide quickly whether to hold on the animation or find a middle ground that let us ship on time.

*Action:* I set up a short call with the engineer to understand which parts were costly. The core animation was manageable, but one specific transition required a custom component. I mocked up two alternatives within an hour: a standard slide transition and a simple fade. We agreed on the fade, which needed no custom work.

*Result:* We shipped on time. The flow still felt polished, and the engineer appreciated being brought into the solution. After that, I made it a habit to flag technically complex interactions with engineering early in the design process.

---

Q: How do you design for users with low digital literacy?

*Situation:* I was working on a feature for a school management platform used by administrative staff at budget schools, many of whom had limited experience with software beyond basic spreadsheets.

*Task:* We needed to build a fee collection module that staff could use without any formal training.

*Action:* I started with contextual interviews, visiting two schools and observing staff as they handled the process manually on paper. I mapped each step of their existing workflow and used it as the skeleton for the digital version. I avoided icons without labels, kept mobile forms to one field per screen, and added confirmation steps at every action that involved money. I tested prototypes with the same staff before development started.

*Result:* The feature launched without a training session. Customer success reported that schools were onboarding and processing collections within their first session. The design was later used as a reference pattern for two other modules in the product.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For 'walk me through your process' questions: Use a four-part structure: start with the problem and who it affected, explain how you gathered insight (the research method, even if lightweight), describe the key design decision you had to make, and end with what you shipped and what you observed after. Avoid listing every step of a textbook design process. Interviewers want to see judgment, not a checklist.

For 'tell me about a failure' questions: The STAR format works well, but the most important part is the Result step. toddle interviewers, like most product companies, look for evidence that you learn and adapt. Name the specific thing you changed in your process or thinking, not just 'I communicated better next time.'

For 'how do you measure success' questions: Connect design decisions to user behaviour metrics (task completion, time on task, drop-off rates) and, where possible, to business outcomes (retention, support ticket volume). If you do not have metrics from a past project, be honest about why, and explain what you would instrument if you were designing it today.

For 'how do you handle conflict' questions: Show that you distinguish between disagreements about taste versus disagreements about user needs. Taste-based pushback is negotiable: if someone says 'I just prefer the old layout,' you can move on. If someone says 'teachers won't understand this,' that is a research question, and you should propose a way to test it.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

toddle's product serves educators, and the design team typically looks for candidates who show genuine empathy for how teachers work, not just surface-level familiarity. Candidates who have used a classroom tool, worked with educational institutions, or done primary research with teachers tend to stand out.

Beyond empathy, interviewers commonly look for design thinking that accounts for real constraints: time-poor users, varied digital comfort levels, and the fact that a teacher cannot afford to look confused in front of students. Designs that work within these constraints signal practical maturity.

Portfolio quality matters, but so does how you talk about your work. Candidates report that interviewers probe for your actual contribution in team projects. Be specific about what you owned versus what was collaborative.

Systems thinking is valued, especially for mid-level and senior roles. If your portfolio includes work on a design system, component libraries, or cross-product consistency, highlight it clearly.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week one (research and portfolio): Use toddle's product yourself if you can access it through their website. Map out the core flows teachers use and identify one or two areas where you have a genuine observation about the experience. Pick two or three portfolio projects that show work relevant to complex user journeys or tools built for non-technical users. Write a short narrative for each that follows the four-part structure in the answer frameworks section.

Week two (craft and practice): Prepare for a design assignment, since candidates typically report receiving a take-home task. Practice talking through your portfolio out loud. Ask a peer to interrupt you with questions like 'why did you choose that over the alternative?' to simulate real interview conditions. Review the job description and map each listed responsibility to a specific project in your portfolio.

Day before the interview: Re-read your portfolio narratives and note any specific metrics or outcomes you can cite. Prepare two or three questions for the interviewer about the team's design process, how decisions get made, and what the biggest challenge on the current roadmap looks like. 07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Not knowing toddle's product is the fastest way to end a process early. Interviewers expect candidates to have explored the product or at minimum reviewed public demos and help documentation. Arriving with a generic 'I would love to learn more about your product' signals low preparation.

Describing your design process in abstract steps ('I empathize, I define, I ideate...') without connecting each step to a real project outcome. Process talk without evidence reads as inexperience.

Taking full credit for team work without clarifying your specific contribution. If an interviewer probes and the story does not hold up, it damages trust quickly.

Treating the take-home assignment as a final deliverable rather than a starting point for discussion. Candidates report that interviewers often ask 'what would you do with more time?' or 'what did you leave out?' A strong answer shows you kept thinking after you submitted.

Not asking questions. A designer who asks nothing about the users, the team, or the product signals low curiosity, which is a red flag for a research-driven role.

Overloading the portfolio with visual work and underweighting the thinking behind each decision. toddle is a functional product for working professionals, not a visual showcase. Craft matters, but reasoning matters more.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the toddle Product Designer interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically includes a portfolio review call, a take-home design assignment, and two or more rounds covering design critique, product thinking, and team fit. The exact structure varies by team and role level. Ask your recruiter at the start of the process so you can plan your preparation accordingly.

Is a take-home assignment always part of the process?

Most candidates report receiving a take-home task at some stage, usually after an initial screening call. The brief typically involves designing a feature or solving a specific UX problem. Focus on showing clear thinking and reasoning rather than pixel-perfect output, since interviewers generally want to discuss your choices as much as review the deliverable.

What kind of portfolio does toddle look for?

Portfolio work that demonstrates research, iteration, and real outcomes tends to resonate more than visual-only showcases. Projects involving complex user journeys, tools for non-technical users, or education-related products are particularly relevant. Be ready to talk in detail about your specific contribution in any collaborative project.

What salary can I expect as a Product Designer at toddle?

Exact compensation figures are not publicly reported by toddle. Based on industry surveys, Product Designer roles in India typically fall in the 14-24 LPA range for mid-level experience (3-5 years) and 26-40 LPA at senior levels (6-9 years). Negotiate based on your specific experience, the scope of the role, and the full package including equity and benefits.

How should I prepare for the design critique part of the interview?

Use toddle's product before the interview and form genuine opinions about specific flows. Interviewers are not looking for praise; they want to see that you can identify real user problems and propose grounded improvements. Support your critique with reasoning about user needs rather than personal preference, and be ready to discuss trade-offs in any change you suggest.

Does toddle hire remotely for Product Designer roles?

Job data shows most Product Designer openings in India are concentrated in Bangalore, with additional openings in Delhi and other cities. Remote policies vary by role and team, so check the specific job listing for location requirements. If the posting is unclear, ask the recruiter directly during your first call.
