01 Overview

Overview

Deepgram is a voice AI company whose flagship product is a real-time speech recognition API used by contact centres, developer tools, and enterprise applications globally. Their infrastructure processes high volumes of audio in milliseconds, which means DevOps engineers here work at the intersection of GPU-accelerated model serving, Kubernetes-native deployments, and low-latency streaming pipelines.

Deepgram currently has 68 open roles across the company, signalling an active hiring phase. Candidates report that the interview process typically runs 3-4 rounds: a recruiter or hiring-manager call, a technical phone screen on cloud and Kubernetes fundamentals, one or two deeper rounds covering system design and past experience, and occasionally a short take-home exercise. The overall bar is high for infrastructure depth, especially around GPU workloads and observability. Many Deepgram DevOps roles are remote-friendly, which opens them to engineers across India.

For broader market context, knok jobradar tracked 811 active DevOps Engineer openings across India as of July 2026, with Bangalore accounting for 187 of those roles, making it the strongest hiring market for this profile.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up most often, based on what candidates report and the nature of Deepgram's technical stack:

  1. Walk us through how you would design a Kubernetes cluster to serve a real-time transcription API at peak load.
  2. How have you managed GPU node pools in a cloud environment, and what scheduling challenges did you encounter?
  3. Describe your CI/CD setup for deploying ML models. How did you handle model versioning, canary releases, and rollback?
  4. How would you build observability (logs, metrics, traces) for a high-throughput audio streaming service?
  5. Walk us through a cost-optimisation strategy you applied on AWS or GCP while keeping latency low for real-time workloads.
  6. Describe a production incident you owned end-to-end: how you detected it, diagnosed the root cause, and what you changed afterward.
  7. How do you manage secrets across microservices? Walk us through your preferred toolchain and why you chose it.
  8. Deepgram's API must be highly available. How would you design a multi-region failover strategy for a transcription service?
  9. How have you scaled stateful services, such as session buffers or audio queues, inside Kubernetes?
  10. Walk us through how you would onboard a new cloud service from scratch using infrastructure-as-code (Terraform or Pulumi).
  11. How would you reduce cold-start latency for GPU inference pods when traffic spikes suddenly?
  12. Tell us about a time you meaningfully improved developer experience, for example by cutting deploy times or simplifying local environment setup.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe your CI/CD setup for deploying ML models, including how you handled rollback.

*Situation:* At my previous company we deployed a speech-processing model to production every two weeks. Each release required updating the model weights, the inference server config, and the Kubernetes deployment spec simultaneously, and a bad release once caused a significant service outage.

*Task:* I was asked to redesign the pipeline so that we could roll back any of those three components independently and very quickly.

*Action:* I introduced a GitOps workflow using ArgoCD. Model weights were versioned in an S3 bucket with semantic tags; Kubernetes manifests referenced those tags explicitly. I added a canary stage that sent a small slice of traffic to the new model version while comparing error rates and latency against the baseline using Prometheus. I also wrote a one-command rollback script that pointed the ArgoCD application back to the previous tag.

*Result:* Our next three releases went live with zero downtime. The canary stage caught a latency regression in one release before it reached all users, and the team rolled back within minutes. The same pattern was adopted for two other model services.

---

Q: Describe a production incident you owned end-to-end.

*Situation:* During a high-traffic period, our audio transcription service began returning timeouts for a significant portion of requests. Alerts fired in the middle of the night.

*Task:* As the on-call engineer I was responsible for restoring service and writing the post-mortem.

*Action:* I started with Grafana dashboards and noticed GPU node memory utilisation had spiked near capacity. The Kubernetes event log showed the HPA had not scaled out because the custom metric it relied on had stopped being emitted after a Prometheus scrape config change earlier that day. I applied a manual scale-out immediately, which restored normal latency within minutes. I then fixed the scrape config and added an alert for 'metric not received for more than two scrape intervals.'

*Result:* User-facing impact was contained to a short window. The post-mortem produced a new rule: any change to Prometheus scrape configs must be validated in staging before merging. We had no repeat of that class of incident over the following six months.

---

Q: How would you reduce cold-start latency for GPU inference pods when traffic spikes suddenly?

*Situation:* A SaaS product I worked on saw sharp traffic spikes every morning as users in different time zones started their day. GPU pod cold starts were taking well over a minute, causing queued requests to time out.

*Task:* I needed to cut that cold-start time and protect against future spikes without massively over-provisioning GPU capacity at all times.

*Action:* I configured Kubernetes Cluster Autoscaler with a warm pool of pre-initialised GPU nodes, using a node-readiness probe that pre-pulled the model image. I set a minimum replica count of two for the inference deployment so we always had at least one warm pod. For model weights, I replaced a remote S3 pull at container startup with a pre-populated local volume using an init container that ran during node provisioning.

*Result:* Cold-start time dropped dramatically. Spike-related timeouts fell to near zero during the two weeks of post-deployment monitoring, and the team formalised the warm-pool pattern in our infrastructure runbook.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For system-design questions (infrastructure and architecture): Start by clarifying requirements, specifically throughput, latency targets, and availability SLAs. Walk through components in order: compute layer, networking and load balancing, data and state management, then observability. State tradeoffs explicitly. Interviewers at a company like Deepgram want to see that you think in terms of failure modes, not just the happy path.

For behavioral questions: Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result). Keep Situation and Task brief (two to three sentences each) and spend most of your time on Action and Result. Quantify results wherever you can, using numbers from your own experience.

For tool-choice questions (e.g., 'why Terraform over Pulumi?'): State your choice, give one or two concrete reasons tied to team size or workflow, and acknowledge when the other option would be better. Dogmatic answers read as inexperience.

For 'how would you debug X?' questions: Think out loud. Name the signals you would check first (metrics, logs, traces), explain what each tells you, and describe how you would isolate the variable. Deepgram's stack is real-time and latency-sensitive, so a methodical, signal-driven approach matters more than jumping straight to a solution.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Deep Kubernetes and cloud fluency. Deepgram's core product is a high-throughput, low-latency API. Interviewers want to see that you have operated Kubernetes clusters under real load, not just set them up following a tutorial. Be ready to discuss HPA, VPA, node affinity, and pod disruption budgets from direct experience.

GPU infrastructure awareness. Because Deepgram's models run on GPUs, they look for candidates who understand GPU node scheduling, resource limits, and how to manage a mixed CPU/GPU cluster. If you have not worked with GPUs directly, be honest and show that you understand the concepts and have a concrete plan to build that skill.

Ownership mindset. Candidates report that Deepgram values engineers who take end-to-end ownership of incidents and improvements, not ones who hand off problems at team boundaries. Frame your experience to show you stayed involved from detection through post-mortem and follow-up action.

Observability as a habit. Questions about monitoring and alerting come up in almost every round. Interviewers want to see that you instrument systems proactively, not just reactively when something breaks.

Clear communication under ambiguity. Several system-design questions will be deliberately open-ended. Asking clarifying questions and stating your assumptions out loud is seen as a strength, not a weakness.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Core foundations
Review Kubernetes internals: scheduler, controllers, HPA, and networking (CNI, service mesh basics). Practice drawing architecture diagrams for a real-time API service from scratch. Revise Terraform or Pulumi by writing a small module end-to-end to make sure you can talk through every block confidently.

Week 2: Deepgram-specific depth
Read Deepgram's engineering blog and developer docs to understand their API architecture and the kinds of workloads their infrastructure serves. Study GPU scheduling in Kubernetes: NVIDIA device plugin, node selectors, and resource limits. Review AWS services most relevant to real-time workloads: EKS, ElastiCache, Kinesis, and CloudFront.

Week 3: Behavioral prep and mock interviews
Write out five to six STAR stories from your own experience covering incident response, CI/CD improvements, cost reduction, cross-team collaboration, and a technical decision you drove. Practice saying them aloud at a natural pace. Do at least two mock system-design interviews with a peer or on a practice platform.

Week 4: Final polish
Review your resume against the job description and match specific examples to each key requirement listed. Prepare two or three thoughtful questions to ask the interviewer about Deepgram's infrastructure roadmap and on-call culture. If the role is remote, test your video and audio setup in advance.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating Kubernetes as a black box. Many candidates say 'I used Kubernetes' without being able to explain what happens when a pod is evicted or how the scheduler decides where to place a workload. Deepgram interviewers probe beneath the surface, so vague answers hurt you.

Skipping tradeoffs in system design. Giving a single 'correct' architecture without discussing alternatives makes you sound junior. Always mention what you are trading off (cost vs. latency, simplicity vs. flexibility) and why you made that call given the constraints.

Vague incident stories. Saying 'we had an outage and I fixed it' is not enough. Interviewers want to know your specific role, the signals you used, the exact change you made, and what you put in place to prevent recurrence.

Ignoring GPU-specific concerns. If you apply to a company whose models run on GPUs and you never mention GPU scheduling, resource isolation, or warm pools, you signal that you have not researched the role or the company's stack.

Not asking questions at the end. Candidates who ask nothing signal low interest. Prepare two or three genuine questions about the team's on-call load, current infrastructure migration plans, or how DevOps and ML engineering collaborate day to day.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does Deepgram's DevOps interview typically have?

Candidates report a process that typically runs 3-4 rounds: a recruiter screen, a technical phone round covering cloud and Kubernetes fundamentals, one or two deeper rounds on system design and past experience, and sometimes a take-home task or live coding session. The exact structure can vary by team and role level, so confirm the format with your recruiter at the start of the process.

What salary can I expect as a DevOps Engineer at Deepgram?

Deepgram is a US-based company that hires globally. For Indian market benchmarks, knok jobradar data shows DevOps Engineer salaries broadly at 6-12 LPA for entry level (0-2 years), 15-28 LPA for mid level (3-5 years), 30-50 LPA for senior level (6-9 years), and 45-70+ LPA for lead or staff roles. For a US-headquartered AI company, publicly reported figures on Glassdoor and levels.fyi for similar roles suggest compensation can also include equity and benefits beyond base salary, so it is worth clarifying the full package during the offer stage.

Does Deepgram hire DevOps engineers remotely from India?

Many of Deepgram's engineering roles are listed as remote-friendly, and candidates from India have applied and interviewed for infrastructure roles. Your clearest signal is the specific job description: check whether it says 'remote' or lists a required office location. If it specifies 'remote, US only,' that typically rules out India-based applicants, and it is worth emailing the recruiter to confirm before investing time in the process.

Do I need GPU infrastructure experience to apply?

GPU experience is a strong differentiator for Deepgram DevOps roles because their models run on GPU clusters. However, not every role at the company demands deep hands-on GPU expertise. If you do not have direct GPU experience, be transparent about it and show that you understand the concepts (NVIDIA device plugin, resource limits, node affinity) and have a concrete plan to build that skill. Strong Kubernetes and cloud fundamentals can still carry you through the early rounds.

What cloud platforms does Deepgram primarily use?

Based on publicly available engineering blog posts and job descriptions, Deepgram primarily uses AWS, with roles frequently mentioning Kubernetes on EKS, infrastructure-as-code, and observability tooling. Familiarity with AWS core services (EKS, S3, Kinesis, IAM, CloudWatch) and tools like Terraform or Pulumi will serve you well across most rounds of the interview.

How should I prepare if I have never worked at an AI or ML company before?

Core DevOps skills transfer directly: Kubernetes, CI/CD, infrastructure-as-code, incident response, and observability are universal. What you should add is a working understanding of ML model serving concepts, specifically how model weights are stored and loaded, what inference servers do, and why latency matters more for real-time AI APIs than for batch workloads. Deepgram's public developer documentation and engineering blog are good free starting points to build this context quickly without needing prior AI company experience.
