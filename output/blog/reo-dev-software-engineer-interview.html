03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for behavioural questions. Here are three worked examples.

Q: Tell us about a performance bottleneck you identified and resolved.

*Situation:* Our API response times had been creeping up over several months and users were reporting timeouts during peak hours.

*Task:* I was asked to investigate and fix the issue before the next major release.

*Action:* I profiled the service and found that a single database query inside a loop was the culprit, running hundreds of times per request. I rewrote it as a single batched query, added an index on the relevant column, and introduced a short-lived cache for data that changed infrequently.

*Result:* Response times dropped significantly and timeouts stopped completely in our monitoring. The fix shipped within the same sprint.

---

Q: Describe a time you disagreed with a product decision.

*Situation:* The product team wanted to launch a feature that skipped input validation in order to meet a release deadline.

*Task:* As the engineer responsible for that module, I knew this would introduce a real security risk.

*Action:* I documented the specific risks with concrete examples, proposed a minimal validation layer that would take one extra day, and brought both options to the team lead with a clear recommendation.

*Result:* The team agreed to the extra day. The feature shipped safely, and the approach I proposed became part of our standard checklist for new endpoints.

---

Q: Tell us about a conflict with a team member.

*Situation:* A senior engineer and I had different views on the right architecture for a new microservice, and code review discussions were becoming tense.

*Task:* We needed to converge quickly because the sprint deadline was approaching.

*Action:* I asked for a short sync call, came prepared with a written comparison of both approaches covering trade-offs on complexity, performance, and future maintenance, and invited them to challenge my reasoning directly.

*Result:* We landed on a hybrid approach that both of us felt confident about. The friction also led our team to adopt a lightweight RFC (request for comments) process for future architectural decisions.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions
Start with the Situation (brief context), then the Task (your specific responsibility), then the Action (what you personally did, using 'I' not 'we'), and finish with the Result (measurable outcome or clear learning). Keep the Situation short and spend most of your time on Action and Result.

Problem-solving walkthrough for coding rounds
Before writing a single line of code: restate the problem in your own words, clarify constraints (input size, edge cases, duplicates), state your brute-force approach and its complexity, then improve it. Talk through your thinking as you go. Interviewers value your reasoning process as much as a correct final answer.

System design structure
Start by clarifying requirements and scale (reads vs. writes, peak load, consistency needs). Sketch a high-level design verbally. Then go deeper on the components the interviewer asks about. Discuss trade-offs explicitly: SQL vs. NoSQL, caching strategy, sync vs. async processing. Finish by identifying the weakest point in your design and how you would address it.

Handling 'Why reo-dev?' questions
Be specific. Research their product, tech stack, or any recent company news before the interview. Connect one concrete detail about reo-dev to a skill or genuine interest of yours. Generic answers like 'great culture, good growth' are immediately forgettable to an interviewer.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Engineering fundamentals: Expect to write code in a language of your choice and explain data structures, algorithms, and time/space complexity. Candidates report that the bar is solid but fair on average. Focus on clarity and correctness rather than tricks or clever one-liners.

Ownership and depth: Interviewers typically dig into past projects with follow-up questions like 'Why did you choose that approach?' or 'What would you do differently now?' Have two or three projects you can discuss in depth, including decisions you would revisit with hindsight.

Communication under uncertainty: System design rounds test how you think when a problem is underspecified. Asking good clarifying questions and making your assumptions explicit signals engineering maturity.

Collaboration signals: Behavioural rounds look for evidence that you can work across teams, handle disagreement constructively, and accept feedback without becoming defensive. Use specific examples with real details, not vague generalisations.

Genuine motivation: reo-dev's process typically ends with a conversation about your motivations and working style. Be honest about the environment where you do your best work, and have a specific reason for wanting to join.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Fundamentals and coding
Revise core data structures (arrays, hashmaps, trees, graphs) and common patterns (sliding window, two pointers, BFS/DFS, dynamic programming). Practice solving problems at easy-to-medium difficulty, and explain your approach out loud as you code, as if you were in a live interview.

Week 2: System design
Study two or three system design problems in depth (for example: URL shortener, rate limiter, notification service). Understand horizontal vs. vertical scaling, CAP theorem basics, database indexing, and message queues. Practice drawing and explaining diagrams out loud without notes.

Week 3: Behavioural and company research
Prepare five or six STAR stories covering ownership, conflict, failure, and measurable impact. Research reo-dev: what they build, their tech stack if publicly available, and recent news. Prepare a crisp, specific answer to 'Why reo-dev?'

Week 4: Mock interviews and review
Do at least two full mock interviews with a friend or on a practice platform. Review your weak areas from earlier weeks. Re-read your STAR stories the day before and get a full night of sleep.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping to code without clarifying the problem. Candidates who start typing before clarifying constraints often waste time solving the wrong version of the problem. Spend two or three minutes asking questions first.

Saying 'we' when asked what you did. Interviewers want to understand your individual contribution. If you built something as part of a team, describe your specific part using 'I'.

Vague STAR answers. Saying 'I improved system performance' without context or outcome is weak. If you cannot share exact figures due to confidentiality, use relative terms: 'reduced latency by more than half' or 'cut the error rate to near zero'.

Treating system design as a monologue. Some candidates lecture through a design without checking in with the interviewer. Pause regularly, ask if you should go deeper on any component, and treat the whole session as a conversation.

Not asking questions at the end. Candidates who ask nothing signal low interest. Prepare two or three genuine questions about the team, the tech stack, or what success looks like in the first few months.

Under-preparing for 'Why this company?' This question appears in almost every final round. A generic answer wastes goodwill. A specific, well-researched answer stands out immediately.

Frequently asked

How many rounds does reo-dev's Software Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report three to four rounds in most cases. This typically includes an initial screening call, one or two technical rounds covering coding and system design, and a final round focused on behavioural questions or a discussion with a senior team member. The exact structure can vary by role and seniority, so ask your recruiter upfront what to expect.

What coding languages does reo-dev accept in technical interviews?

Candidates typically report being allowed to choose their preferred language for coding rounds. Python, Java, and JavaScript are the most commonly mentioned choices. Pick the language you are most fluent in rather than trying to impress with a less familiar one. Clarity and correctness matter more than language choice.

How long does the reo-dev hiring process take from application to offer?

Candidates report the process typically takes two to four weeks from the first screening call to an offer, though this can stretch longer depending on team availability and scheduling. Following up with your recruiter after each round is normal and expected. Keep your job search active in parallel since timelines are often unpredictable.

Is there a take-home assignment in reo-dev's interview process?

Some candidates report receiving a take-home coding task as part of the screening stage, while others go straight to live coding rounds. This varies by role and hiring manager preference. If you receive a take-home assignment, treat it with the same care as a live round: write clean, well-structured code and add comments where the logic is not immediately obvious.

How should I prepare differently for a senior versus an entry-level Software Engineer role at reo-dev?

For entry-level roles, focus on coding fundamentals, problem-solving patterns, and basic system design concepts. For senior roles, interviewers will probe deeper into past projects, architectural decisions, and your ability to influence technical direction across a team. Senior candidates are also expected to ask sharper questions about the role and demonstrate clear personal ownership in their STAR answers. The behavioural and leadership bar rises significantly at senior and lead levels.
