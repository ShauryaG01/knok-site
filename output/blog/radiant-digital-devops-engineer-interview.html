02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up consistently in DevOps interviews at companies like Radiant Digital, based on candidate reports and the nature of their enterprise-focused delivery work.

  1. Walk us through a CI/CD pipeline you built or significantly improved. What tools did you pick and why?
  2. How do you respond when a production service goes down? Take us through your incident response steps.
  3. Describe your Kubernetes experience. How do you handle cluster upgrades or rolling deployments without downtime?
  4. Which infrastructure-as-code tool do you prefer (Terraform, Pulumi, Ansible) and what drove that choice in a real project?
  5. How do you make sure security is part of the pipeline from the start, rather than checked only at release time?
  6. Enterprise clients often have strict SLA requirements. How have you worked with SLOs and error budgets in practice?
  7. What is your experience with multi-cloud or hybrid-cloud setups? How do you handle environment parity?
  8. Walk us through how you would monitor a microservices architecture. What tools would you use, and how would you set alerting thresholds?
  9. Tell us about a time you had to convince developers or another team to adopt a new DevOps practice. How did you handle resistance?
  10. How do you manage secrets and credentials across dev, staging, and production without putting them in version control?
  11. Describe a deployment that caused a production incident. What did you do immediately, and what changed afterward?
  12. How do you keep cloud infrastructure costs in check while still allowing the team to scale on demand?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for experience-based questions. These examples show the structure and depth Radiant Digital interviewers typically expect.

Q: Walk us through a CI/CD pipeline you built from scratch.

*Situation:* At my previous company, releases were fully manual. Developers packaged builds and pushed them to production via FTP. Every deployment was stressful and error-prone.

*Task:* I was asked to replace this process with an automated pipeline before the team's next major product release.

*Action:* I set up a GitLab CI/CD pipeline with separate stages for build, unit testing, integration testing, and deployment. I containerised the application using Docker, stood up a staging environment that closely mirrored production, and added a manual approval gate before any production push. I also wrote a runbook so the rest of the team could operate the pipeline without me.

*Result:* Releases that previously took a full working day became automated, same-day pushes. Team confidence in deployments improved noticeably, and we had no production rollbacks in the first quarter after going live.

---

Q: Describe a production incident you handled.

*Situation:* A misconfigured Kubernetes resource limit allowed a memory leak to go undetected during a weekend deployment. By Monday morning, several pods had crashed and a client-facing service was unavailable.

*Task:* As the on-call engineer that morning, I needed to restore service quickly and then trace the root cause so it would not repeat.

*Action:* I immediately rolled back the deployment to the last stable version. Once the service was healthy again, I used our Prometheus and Grafana setup to trace the memory spike back to the specific change introduced in the weekend release. I wrote a post-mortem, proposed adding a resource-limit review step to our standard PR checklist, and created an alerting rule to flag similar memory trends early.

*Result:* Service was restored within our SLA window. The post-mortem was shared across the team and the PR checklist update was adopted in the same sprint, which teammates found useful in subsequent reviews.

---

Q: How have you handled secrets management in a past role?

*Situation:* When I joined my previous team, database passwords and third-party API keys were stored in plaintext inside .env files committed to the repository.

*Task:* I needed to introduce a proper secrets management approach without disrupting work already in flight.

*Action:* I evaluated HashiCorp Vault and AWS Secrets Manager. Since the team was already on AWS, I chose Secrets Manager for easier integration. I migrated credentials in batches, updated application config to pull secrets at runtime, and ran a short workshop for developers explaining the change. I also added a git pre-commit hook that detected and blocked accidental credential commits before they reached the remote.

*Result:* All secrets were rotated and moved within a single sprint with no service disruption. The pre-commit hook caught two accidental credential commits from teammates in the following weeks, confirming the value of the extra guard.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the most reliable structure for experience questions. Keep each element concise: one to two sentences for Situation and Task, the bulk of your time on Action, and a clear observable or measurable Result.

For technical concept questions, use a three-part pattern: state what the thing is, explain how it works, then give a real example from your experience. Interviewers at companies like Radiant Digital value depth over breadth, so going deep on one clear example beats listing every tool you have touched.

For system design or architecture questions, start by clarifying the constraints (scale, latency requirements, team size, existing stack). Then walk through your design out loud, explaining trade-offs as you go. Radiant Digital's enterprise focus means reliability and observability decisions often matter as much as the core design itself.

For 'how would you handle X' questions, briefly state your default approach, then note one or two factors that would change it. This signals that you think in context rather than applying the same solution everywhere.

Talking about failures: Interviewers want ownership and learning. State what went wrong clearly, take responsibility for your part, explain what you changed, and end on the improvement. Avoid blaming tools or teammates.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Reliability mindset over tool name-dropping. Radiant Digital serves enterprise clients where downtime carries real consequences. Interviewers look for candidates who think in terms of SLAs, rollback strategies, and post-mortems, not just candidates who can list tools.

Hands-on depth. Be ready to go into specifics: the exact Kubernetes config you wrote, how you structured a Terraform module, how you debugged a failing pipeline stage. Vague answers like 'I used Kubernetes' without substance typically do not land well.

Cross-team communication. DevOps at an enterprise-focused company means working closely with development teams, QA, security, and sometimes client-side IT. Candidates who can explain how they handled friction with developers or navigated a stakeholder conversation tend to stand out.

Security awareness. Shift-left security (SAST, secret scanning, image scanning in CI) comes up often. You do not need to be a security specialist, but you should know the basics and have applied them in practice.

Ownership and learning from incidents. Candidates who clearly own their mistakes in post-mortems and describe concrete changes they made as a result tend to rate well. Interviewers are wary of candidates who blame circumstances or tools without reflecting on their own decisions.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week one: solidify your fundamentals.
Review the core technologies most relevant to the role: Kubernetes (pods, deployments, resource limits, ingress), Terraform (state management, modules, workspaces), and at least one major cloud platform (AWS or Azure is most common in Indian enterprise delivery). Be able to explain your choices, not just describe your actions.

Week two: prepare your stories.
Write out five to six real situations from your career using the STAR format. Cover at least one incident response, one pipeline build or improvement, one cross-team collaboration challenge, and one security-related decision. Practice telling each story out loud in under three minutes.

Before the interview:
Look up Radiant Digital's recent client work and any publicly available case studies. If you can connect your experience to the kind of enterprise delivery they do, your answers will feel relevant rather than generic. Review the job description carefully and note which specific tools or cloud platforms they mention.

On the day:
For technical questions, think out loud. Interviewers want to follow your reasoning, not just hear your conclusion. If you do not know something, say so directly and explain how you would find out. That is better than guessing.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Talking about tools without talking about decisions. Saying 'I used Terraform' is not enough. Interviewers want to know why you chose it, what trade-offs you considered, and what problems came up. Always connect your tool choices to the context and the outcome.

Treating the incident question as a trap. Candidates sometimes try to minimise the incident or deflect responsibility. This backfires. Interviewers are looking for ownership. Pick a real incident, describe what went wrong honestly, and focus most of your answer on what you did and what changed afterward.

Being vague about results. 'It improved things' is a weak ending to a STAR answer. Be specific about what changed: deployment frequency, rollback rate, time to restore service, team adoption, or feedback from stakeholders. Even qualitative results ('the team no longer feared release days') are more convincing than vague gestures.

Not asking clarifying questions in system design. Jumping straight into a design without understanding constraints suggests you apply the same pattern everywhere. Ask about scale, team size, existing tooling, and budget before drawing out an architecture.

Underestimating the collaboration questions. DevOps interviews at enterprise-focused companies often spend as much time on how you work with developers and other teams as on your technical depth. Prepare at least one solid story about a cross-team challenge.
