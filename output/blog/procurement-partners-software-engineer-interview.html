02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates who have interviewed at Procurement Partners typically report questions across three areas: core engineering, domain knowledge in procurement or healthcare tech, and behavioural fit. Here are the questions most worth preparing for:

  1. Walk me through how you would design a purchase-order management system that supports thousands of concurrent hospital users.
  2. How have you worked with third-party ERP integrations (such as SAP or Oracle) in a previous role?
  3. Describe your experience with RESTful API design. How do you handle versioning and backwards compatibility?
  4. How would you approach debugging a performance bottleneck in a service that processes large supplier catalogues?
  5. Tell me about a time you had to ensure data accuracy in a critical workflow, such as financial or inventory data.
  6. How do you write unit and integration tests for code that interacts with external vendor APIs?
  7. Procurement data often includes sensitive pricing and contract terms. How have you handled security and access control in past work?
  8. Describe a situation where you had to refactor legacy code while keeping the system live for end users.
  9. How do you prioritise tasks when product requirements change mid-sprint?
  10. What does 'clean code' mean to you, and how do you apply it in a team setting?
  11. Have you worked in an Agile environment? How do you handle scope disagreements with a product manager?
  12. Why do you want to work on procurement software, and what do you know about how hospitals manage their purchasing process?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time you debugged a critical performance issue in a production service.

*Situation:* Our order-processing microservice was timing out during peak hours, affecting a large number of vendors on the platform.
*Task:* I was responsible for identifying the root cause and deploying a fix without taking the service offline.
*Action:* I added structured logging to trace slow database queries and found that a missing index on the supplier-ID column was causing full-table scans. I worked with the DBA to add the index during a low-traffic window and added a cache layer for frequently-read supplier records.
*Result:* Response times improved noticeably and the team saw no further timeouts in the following two weeks.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you had to integrate with a third-party system that had poor documentation.

*Situation:* We needed to pull contract pricing data from a legacy ERP system whose API docs were outdated and incomplete.
*Task:* My job was to build a reliable data-sync pipeline with that system as the source of truth.
*Action:* I wrote a thin wrapper with detailed error logging, ran exploratory calls against the sandbox environment to map the actual response shapes, and built a test suite covering edge cases I found along the way. I documented my findings in the team wiki.
*Result:* The integration went live on schedule, and the documentation became the team reference for all future work with that vendor.

---

Q: Give an example of a time you pushed back on a product requirement.

*Situation:* The product team asked us to build a real-time bidding feature for supplier quotes within a two-week sprint.
*Task:* I had to communicate the technical complexity and propose a workable alternative.
*Action:* I put together a quick breakdown showing that real-time bidding required WebSocket infrastructure we did not yet have, and proposed a near-real-time polling approach as a phased first step, with estimated effort and risk clearly stated.
*Result:* The team agreed on the phased approach, we shipped the polling feature on time, and the full real-time feature was planned into the next quarter with proper infrastructure support.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions: Every behavioural question calls for a Situation, Task, Action, Result structure. Keep Situation and Task brief (two to three sentences), spend most time on Action (what you specifically did, not what 'we' did), and close with a concrete Result.

Component sketch for system design: When asked to design a system, start with clarifying questions about scale, consistency requirements, and user types. Then sketch the major components and how they interact before going deep on any one part. For Procurement Partners specifically, think about how your design handles concurrent users, data integrity for financial records, and third-party integrations.

'Problem, approach, trade-off' for coding questions: State what the problem is asking, describe your chosen approach and why, then name at least one trade-off or alternative. This shows structured thinking before you write a single line of code.

'Impact first' for opening a story: Lead with the outcome when starting a behavioural answer, then explain what you did to reach it. Interviewers process information better when they know where the story is heading.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Procurement Partners builds software that healthcare organisations depend on for day-to-day purchasing, so interviewers typically look for a few things above all else.

Reliability and correctness: Procurement data touches real money and contract obligations. Interviewers want evidence that you write careful, well-tested code and take data integrity seriously.

Integration experience: The platform connects with ERPs, supplier catalogues, and GPO systems. Prior work with APIs, event-driven pipelines, or data sync jobs is a strong signal.

Clear communication: The team serves non-technical stakeholders such as hospital procurement staff. The ability to explain technical constraints in plain terms is valued.

Ownership: Candidates who describe taking a problem from an unclear requirement through to a shipped feature, including testing and documentation, tend to perform well in final evaluations.

Domain curiosity: You do not need to be a procurement expert. Showing that you have read about how hospital supply chains work, or asking smart questions about the product during the interview, signals genuine interest rather than just job-hunting.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Technical foundations
Revise data structures, algorithms, and SQL. Focus on query optimisation (indexes, joins, query plans), since procurement systems are data-heavy. Review REST API design principles and at least one integration pattern such as webhooks, polling, or event queues.

Week 2: System design and domain reading
Practice designing a mid-complexity system such as an order management service, a supplier catalogue with search, or a contract-tracking module. Read publicly available product pages or case studies about how procurement platforms handle data security and audit trails.

Week 3: Behavioural prep and mock interviews
Write out five to six STAR stories covering: a bug you fixed under pressure, a time you improved a process, a conflict with a teammate or stakeholder, a project you are proud of, and a time you learned from a mistake. Do at least two mock interviews out loud so your answers feel natural, not rehearsed.

Before the interview
Research Procurement Partners' product, customers, and any recent news. Prepare two or three thoughtful questions about the engineering team, tech stack, and how the team handles on-call or incident response. 07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping clarifying questions in system design: Jumping into an architecture without asking about scale, consistency needs, or user types is one of the most common mistakes. Interviewers want to see structured thinking before any solution.

Vague STAR answers: Saying 'we improved performance' without specifying what you personally did, or what the measurable outcome was, weakens your answer considerably. Be specific and own your contribution.

Ignoring the domain: Treating this like a generic software interview and not connecting your answers to procurement, data integrity, or healthcare context misses a clear opportunity to stand out from other candidates.

Over-engineering simple coding problems: Starting with the most complex solution rather than a working one first signals poor judgement. Get to a working solution, then optimise if time allows.

Not asking questions at the end: Candidates who ask nothing leave the impression they are not genuinely interested. Prepare at least two specific questions about the role or team.

Forgetting to mention testing: For a company handling financial and procurement data, explaining how you test your code, including unit tests, integration tests, and edge cases, matters and is often left out by otherwise strong candidates.
