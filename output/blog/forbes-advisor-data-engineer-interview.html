02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Forbes Advisor interviews for Data Engineers typically focus on financial data processing, pipeline reliability, and modern cloud tooling. Here are the questions candidates most commonly encounter:

  1. Walk me through a data pipeline you built end-to-end. What tools did you use and what problems did you solve?
  2. How do you handle late-arriving or out-of-order data in a streaming pipeline?
  3. Describe a time when a data pipeline you owned broke in production. How did you diagnose and fix it?
  4. How would you design a data warehouse schema for tracking user engagement on a financial content site?
  5. Forbes Advisor relies heavily on SEO and traffic data. How would you build a pipeline that ingests and processes web analytics data at scale?
  6. What is the difference between a star schema and a snowflake schema? When would you choose one over the other?
  7. How do you ensure data quality and catch bad data before it reaches downstream consumers?
  8. Describe your experience with orchestration tools like Airflow or Prefect. How do you handle task dependencies and retries?
  9. How have you optimised a slow SQL query on a large table? Walk me through your process.
  10. What is your approach to monitoring and alerting for data pipelines in production?
  11. How would you handle a situation where business stakeholders and analysts disagree on the definition of a key metric?
  12. Tell me about a time you worked with a messy or poorly documented data source. How did you approach it?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk me through a data pipeline you built end-to-end.

*Situation:* My previous employer ran a content platform with user-behaviour data sitting in raw logs across several cloud storage buckets, with no consistent schema or processing in place.

*Task:* I was asked to build a pipeline that would transform these raw logs into a clean, queryable dataset for the analytics team, who needed daily reporting on content performance.

*Action:* I designed an ingestion layer using Apache Kafka to stream events in near-real-time, wrote PySpark jobs to clean and validate the data, and loaded results into a BigQuery data warehouse. I used Apache Airflow to orchestrate daily batch jobs and set up data quality checks using Great Expectations, so bad records were quarantined and flagged rather than silently corrupting downstream tables. I also documented the schema and ran a short onboarding walkthrough with the analytics team.

*Result:* The pipeline ran reliably without missed SLAs for well over a year after launch, and the analytics team could self-serve their reporting instead of raising ad hoc data requests.

---

Q: Describe a time when a data pipeline you owned broke in production. How did you diagnose and fix it?

*Situation:* An Airflow DAG that loaded daily financial summary data into our data warehouse started failing silently. Downstream dashboards were showing stale numbers, and by the time the team noticed, a full day of data had not been processed.

*Task:* I needed to find the root cause, restore the missing data, and prevent the same failure from going undetected in future.

*Action:* I pulled the Airflow task logs and found that an upstream API had changed its response schema without notice, causing the parsing step to drop all rows silently rather than raising an error. I patched the parser, ran a backfill job to reprocess the affected period, and then added schema validation at the ingestion step so that unexpected field changes would fail loudly. I also set up Slack alerts for any DAG failure or significant data volume drop.

*Result:* The team restored full data coverage, and the new alerting caught several similar upstream schema changes in the months that followed, before they could affect any dashboards.

---

Q: How would you handle a situation where business stakeholders and analysts disagree on the definition of a key metric?

*Situation:* At a previous company, the marketing team and the finance team were both pulling 'monthly active users' from different tables and arriving at different numbers, which caused confusion in leadership reviews.

*Task:* I was brought in to resolve the discrepancy and establish a single source of truth.

*Action:* I mapped out both definitions by reading the queries each team was using. Marketing counted any user who loaded a page, while finance counted only users who completed a transaction. I organised a short meeting with both teams and a product manager to agree on a canonical definition, then created a certified metrics layer in the warehouse using dbt with clear documentation for each metric. I retired the ad hoc queries both teams had been using.

*Result:* Leadership reports moved to a single agreed number, and the metrics layer became the reference point for all future metric definitions across the company.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use STAR for behavioural questions. STAR stands for Situation, Task, Action, Result. Every story you tell should have a clear problem (Situation and Task), a specific thing you personally did (Action), and a concrete outcome (Result). Avoid vague endings like 'it went well.' State what actually changed.

Use a structured design approach for system design questions. When asked to design a pipeline or data warehouse, walk through: requirements clarification, data sources and ingestion method, transformation logic and tools, storage layer and schema design, orchestration, and monitoring. This signals end-to-end thinking rather than jumping straight to a tool name.

For SQL and coding questions, narrate as you go. Interviewers at media companies like Forbes Advisor care as much about how you think as whether you get the exact syntax right. Say what you are doing and why, ask about edge cases before you start, and check your own output before declaring it done.

For data quality questions, use a three-layer model. Cover: (1) validation at ingestion, catching bad records early; (2) transformation-layer checks, ensuring business logic is applied correctly; (3) output monitoring, verifying that downstream tables look as expected by volume, distribution, and freshness. This structure shows you think about data quality as a system, not a one-time check.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Forbes Advisor's data engineering team sits at the intersection of content, advertising, and financial data. Interviewers are typically looking for a few specific things.

Reliability first. Financial and editorial data feeds directly into business decisions and public-facing content. Candidates who can speak to how they have made pipelines robust, monitored them in production, and recovered quickly from failures consistently stand out.

Cloud and modern stack fluency. Expect questions that assume comfort with tools like BigQuery or Redshift, Airflow or a similar orchestrator, dbt for transformations, and Spark or Kafka for scale. You do not need expertise in every tool, but you should be able to explain trade-offs between them.

Business curiosity. Forbes Advisor publishes financial comparison content and relies on data to understand what performs well. Candidates who ask good questions about the business context of the data they are working on, rather than treating it as a purely technical exercise, tend to progress further in the process.

Clear communication. Data engineers at media companies work closely with editorial, product, and analytics teams. The ability to explain a technical limitation or a data quality issue in plain language, without jargon, is valued as much as technical depth itself.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: core SQL and pipeline fundamentals.
Practise window functions, CTEs, and query optimisation on platforms like LeetCode or StrataScratch. Review how you would explain query plans and index usage. Revise the difference between batch and streaming pipelines and when each approach makes sense.

Week 2: system design and tooling.
Sketch out a few pipeline designs from scratch: a web analytics ingestion pipeline, a financial data warehouse, and a real-time alerting pipeline. For each, be ready to discuss tool choices, failure modes, and monitoring approach. Review Airflow DAG design and common pitfalls like zombie tasks and backfill behaviour.

Week 3: behavioural stories and company research.
Prepare several STAR stories covering: a pipeline you built, a production incident you handled, a cross-functional collaboration, and a time you improved data quality. Research Forbes Advisor's products such as rate tables, comparison tools, and editorial rankings, then think about what data problems a team like theirs would face day to day.

Day before the interview.
Review your STAR stories out loud. Confirm the interview format with the recruiter if you have not already. Prepare questions to ask the interviewer about the team's current data stack and what success looks like in the first few months.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping to a tool before understanding the problem. Saying 'I would use Kafka' before establishing data volume, latency requirements, and team context signals shallow thinking. Always clarify requirements first.

Giving vague results in STAR answers. 'The team was happy' is not a result. Describe what changed operationally: pipelines ran reliably, analysts could self-serve, a business decision was unblocked. If you cannot share specific figures, describe the qualitative outcome clearly.

Treating data quality as an afterthought. Candidates who only mention data quality when directly asked miss a major opportunity. Weave it into every pipeline design you discuss.

Ignoring the business context of Forbes Advisor. This is a financial content company. Talking about data engineering in a vacuum, without connecting it to content performance, advertising revenue, or financial data accuracy, makes you seem like a generic candidate who did not research the company.

Not asking questions at the end. Candidates who ask nothing leave the impression that they are not genuinely interested in the role. Prepare at least a couple of thoughtful questions about the team's challenges, tech debt, or roadmap.
