02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These are the questions candidates report hearing most often in Falcon India PM interviews. Prepare a clear, structured answer for each before you walk in.

  1. Walk me through a product you use every day and tell us specifically how you would improve it.
  2. How would you define and track success metrics for a new feature Falcon India is launching?
  3. Tell me about a time you made a product decision with incomplete or conflicting data.
  4. How do you prioritize a backlog when engineering capacity is constrained?
  5. Describe a product you shipped end-to-end. What went wrong, and what did you learn?
  6. A core engagement metric has dropped noticeably after a recent release. How do you diagnose the cause?
  7. How do you approach building a roadmap for a new zero-to-one product versus a mature existing one?
  8. Tell me about a time you disagreed with an engineering lead. How did you resolve it?
  9. How do you gather and validate user insights before writing a requirements document?
  10. If you could add one feature to a product in Falcon India's space, what would it be and why?
  11. How do you measure product-market fit for a B2B product?
  12. You have just joined as a PM. Walk us through your first month and your three-month plan.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a product you shipped end-to-end. What went wrong?

*Situation:* I was the sole PM for a mobile onboarding revamp at my previous company. The goal was to reduce user drop-off in the early days after sign-up.

*Task:* I owned the full cycle: discovery, writing the requirements document, sprint planning, and launch coordination. I had to align design, a small engineering team, and customer success before a fixed quarterly deadline.

*Action:* I ran several user interviews, mapped the friction points, and wrote a phased requirements document. Mid-sprint, engineering flagged that one core feature would need significant extra time to build correctly. I made the call to cut it from version one and launch the leaner build on schedule, documenting the cut scope clearly so nothing was lost.

*Result:* We shipped on time. Post-launch analytics showed a meaningful reduction in early drop-off. The cut feature shipped in the next sprint with better specs because we had real user feedback to inform the design.

---

Q: How do you prioritize a backlog when engineering capacity is constrained?

*Situation:* At a startup, our engineering team shrank significantly after a restructuring. We had a large backlog of items that had been promised to enterprise clients.

*Task:* I needed to re-prioritize quickly and communicate the changes to clients without damaging trust.

*Action:* I scored every item on a simple Impact vs. Effort grid, then layered in revenue risk by flagging items with contractual commitments. I held a joint session with the CTO and sales lead to align on the final stack rank. For each deprioritized item, I personally emailed the relevant client with a revised timeline and a clear explanation of why.

*Result:* We shipped all top-priority items within the quarter. Only one client escalated, and the transparency in communication kept the relationship intact. This framework became the team's standard approach to quarterly planning.

---

Q: A core metric has dropped after a release. How do you diagnose it?

*Situation:* Our weekly active users metric dropped noticeably the week after we pushed a major UI refresh.

*Task:* My job was to identify whether the drop came from the release, an external factor, or a data pipeline issue, and recommend corrective action very quickly.

*Action:* I started by checking the data pipeline for anomalies (it was clean). I then segmented the drop by platform, region, and user cohort. The drop was concentrated on users running an older Android version. I pulled the release notes and found a known rendering bug affecting that OS version. I worked with engineering to push a hotfix and set up a pre-release checklist for OS version coverage going forward.

*Result:* The metric recovered within a week of the fix. The new pre-release checklist caught similar issues in the following quarter before they reached production.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For product design and improvement questions, use the 'User, Problem, Solution' flow: name the user segment clearly, articulate the core unmet need, then propose solutions ranked by impact. Avoid jumping to features before you have defined the problem out loud.

For metric drop questions, candidates report that a structured diagnostic approach impresses Falcon India interviewers. Start with data validity (is the drop real?), then segment by time, platform, geography, and user cohort. Eliminate external factors such as holidays or competitor launches before blaming the product.

For prioritization questions, a simple Impact vs. Effort matrix works well in the room. You can add a third axis such as strategic alignment or revenue risk if the interviewer pushes back. Name your assumptions explicitly: it shows you understand the framework as a tool, not a formula.

For stakeholder conflict questions, STAR pairs well with a 'Disagree and Commit' close. Show that you raised your concern clearly, backed it with data or reasoning, and then fully committed once a decision was made. Falcon India interviewers typically look for candidates who navigate disagreement without escalating every small friction point.

For 'what would you build' questions, ground your answer in a user insight or a market gap before naming a feature. Frame your answer as a hypothesis: 'I believe X users struggle with Y, so building Z and measuring W would validate this.' This keeps the conversation structured and signals genuine product thinking rather than a feature wishlist.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Candidates report that Falcon India PM interviewers probe on several things above all else.

Clear thinking under ambiguity. Interviewers will ask vague questions deliberately. They want to see you ask clarifying questions, state your assumptions out loud, and structure your answer before diving in. Thinking out loud is not a weakness here: it is the expected behavior.

Ownership and follow-through. Candidates at Falcon India note that interviewers frequently ask 'what happened after?' and 'what would you do differently?' Answers that stop at the launch are a red flag. Be ready to discuss post-launch metrics, iterations, and lessons learned.

Cross-functional empathy. PM roles at Falcon India require close coordination with engineering and design. Interviewers typically look for evidence that you have built trust with non-PM stakeholders, not just directed them. Use examples where you listened to engineering constraints and adapted your plan accordingly.

User-first language. Framing every answer around user outcomes rather than business metrics alone signals product maturity. You can mention revenue or growth, but lead with the user problem you were solving.

Concise communication. Candidates report that long, rambling answers score poorly. Structure your answer clearly, then pause and invite the interviewer to probe. This mirrors how PMs present in real team settings.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know the company and the product. Research Falcon India's core business, target users, and main product lines. Write down a few things you would change or improve, backed by user or market reasoning. Read any available news or blog posts to understand their strategic direction.

Week 2: Practice core PM frameworks out loud. Record yourself answering several questions from the 'most asked' list above. Watch the recordings and cut every sentence that does not add information. This is uncomfortable but it removes filler and sharpens your structure.

Week 3: Run mock interviews. Find a peer PM or a structured mock-interview partner. Focus on the metric diagnostic and prioritization questions, as candidates report these come up most at Falcon India. Time yourself to a few minutes per answer to build pacing.

Week 4: Polish and research the role. Re-read the job description and map each requirement to a story from your experience. Prepare a few thoughtful questions for the interviewer that show you have considered Falcon India's specific challenges, not generic PM topics.

Day before: Review your top stories, situations you can adapt across multiple question types. Sleep well. Bring a printed copy of your resume even for virtual rounds, as candidates report some Falcon India interviewers prefer to work from it during the conversation.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping to solutions before defining the problem. This is the most common PM interview mistake. Interviewers at Falcon India, like most product-focused companies, want to see you sit with the problem before proposing a fix. Pause, restate the user need, then propose a solution.

Vague impact claims. Saying 'we improved engagement' without any qualifier sounds unsubstantiated. Always tie your impact to a metric, even a directional one. Phrases like 'meaningful reduction' or 'per our analytics' are better than nothing.

Ignoring engineering in your answers. PMs who talk only about user needs without acknowledging technical constraints come across as out of touch. Mention a time you adjusted your plan based on an engineering constraint: it shows you understand the real trade-offs.

Memorised answers. Candidates who clearly rehearsed a single canned answer often get caught when the interviewer probes with 'and then what?' Keep your stories flexible, not scripted.

Not asking enough clarifying questions. When a question is vague, candidates often rush to answer. At Falcon India, asking a few clarifying questions before structuring your response is typically seen as a positive signal, not hesitation.

Over-explaining the situation, under-explaining the result. In STAR answers, spend most of your time on Action and Result. Interviewers understand context quickly: what they want to hear is what you did and what actually happened.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Falcon India PM interview process typically have?

Candidates report the process typically runs across multiple rounds, though the exact number varies by level and team. Rounds generally cover a phone screen, a product case, an analytical or metric question round, and a final culture or leadership discussion. Some candidates also report a take-home assignment for senior roles. Always confirm the exact structure with your recruiter before your first call.

What salary can I expect for a PM role at Falcon India?

Falcon India has not published salary bands publicly. Knok jobradar tracks general market bands for PM roles in India: Associate PM at 12-20 LPA, PM with 3-6 years of experience at 24-40 LPA, Senior PM at 40-60 LPA, and Group or Principal PM at 55-90+ LPA. For Falcon India specifically, check Glassdoor or levels.fyi for community-reported numbers, and negotiate based on your total experience and the level you are interviewing for.

Does Falcon India ask technical questions in the PM interview?

Candidates report that Falcon India PM interviews are not heavily technical in the coding sense. You are more likely to face product analytics questions or system-level discussions such as explaining an API to a non-technical stakeholder. You do not need to write code, but familiarity with how products are built will help you sound credible when discussing engineering trade-offs with the team.

How important is domain knowledge for a Falcon India PM role?

Candidates report that structured thinking and product sense matter more than prior domain experience, especially for mid-level PM roles. That said, showing genuine curiosity about Falcon India's market and users during the interview makes a strong impression. Research the company's product, identify a few real user pain points, and come prepared with a thoughtful observation about their space.

Should I prepare a product critique for the interview?

It is a good idea to have a prepared critique of Falcon India's own product (or a closely related product in their space) even if you are not explicitly asked. Interviewers typically appreciate when candidates show they have used the product and thought critically about it. Keep your critique balanced: identify a real user pain point, propose a directional solution, and acknowledge what the team likely already does well.

How soon should I expect to hear back after the final round?

Candidates report hearing back within a couple of weeks after the final round, though timelines vary during busy hiring periods. If you have not heard back within a reasonable time, a polite follow-up email to your recruiter is entirely appropriate.
