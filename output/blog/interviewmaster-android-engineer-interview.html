02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly in InterviewMaster Android Engineer interviews, based on what candidates typically report for product-focused Android teams:

  1. Walk me through the architecture you chose for your last major Android project and why.
  2. How does the InterviewMaster product work from a user's perspective, and where do you see room to improve the Android experience?
  3. Explain the Activity and Fragment lifecycle. How have you handled configuration changes in production?
  4. How do you manage background tasks in Android? Compare WorkManager, JobScheduler, and Foreground Services.
  5. Describe how you would implement offline support for a key feature in an app like InterviewMaster.
  6. How do you approach unit testing and UI testing in Android? Which frameworks have you used?
  7. The app's startup time increased after a recent release. How do you diagnose and fix it?
  8. How does Jetpack Compose differ from the XML View system, and have you migrated any screens to Compose?

  10. How do you handle API failures and poor network conditions gracefully in your app?
  11. Describe a time you had to refactor a large, legacy Android codebase. What was your approach and what did you learn?
  12. How would you design the data layer for a feature that syncs user progress across multiple devices?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk me through the architecture you chose for your last major Android project and why.

*Situation:* My previous team had a news-aggregator app with tightly coupled Activities, making it very hard to write tests or add new features without breaking existing ones.

*Task:* I was asked to lead the architecture migration for a core module before a major feature launch.

*Action:* I proposed MVVM with clean-architecture layering: a UI layer using Fragments and ViewModels, a domain layer with UseCases, and a data layer with Repositories backed by Room and Retrofit. I introduced Hilt for dependency injection, ran a team knowledge-sharing session, and wrote the first migrated module as a reference for others to follow.

*Result:* The migrated module became much easier to test, the team shipped the new feature on schedule, and the pattern was later adopted across the rest of the app.

---

Q: The app's startup time increased after a recent release. How do you diagnose and fix it?

*Situation:* After we shipped a new analytics SDK integration, QA flagged that cold start felt noticeably slower on mid-range devices.

*Task:* I owned Android performance on our team, so diagnosing and fixing this was my responsibility within one sprint.

*Action:* I used Android Studio's App Startup profiler and Systrace to identify that the analytics SDK was initialising synchronously on the main thread inside Application.onCreate. I moved initialisation to a background thread using the Jetpack App Startup library, lazy-loaded two other libraries that did not need to run at launch, and removed a redundant database pre-population call.

*Result:* Cold start time dropped measurably on the devices we tested, and I added a startup-time benchmark to our CI pipeline to catch future regressions.

---

Q: Describe a time you had to refactor a large, legacy Android codebase.

*Situation:* I joined a startup where the Android app had no separation of concerns, God classes everywhere, and zero tests. The team wanted to add a subscription flow but was afraid to touch the payment code.

*Task:* I needed to make the payment module safe to modify without rewriting the entire app at once.

*Action:* I used the 'strangler fig' pattern: I defined the boundary of the payment module, wrapped the existing code behind an interface, wrote characterisation tests to document its current behaviour, and then rewrote the internals one class at a time. I kept PRs small so code review stayed manageable and documented every decision in the PR description.

*Result:* The module was refactored across three sprints without any production incidents, the new subscription flow launched successfully, and the team gained real confidence in that part of the codebase.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep the Situation and Task brief, spend most of your time on the Action, and always close with a concrete Result.

'Define, Explain, Trade-off' for Android concept questions: First define the concept clearly, then explain how it works internally, then discuss when you would and would not use it. For example, if asked about LiveData vs. StateFlow, define both, explain the threading model, then state which you prefer for new projects and why.

4-step approach for system design:

  1. Clarify scope (which features, offline or online only, which Android versions to support).
  2. Sketch the data model and API contract.
  3. Design the Android-side architecture: data layer, domain layer, UI layer.
  4. Address edge cases: error states, caching strategy, background sync, and testing approach.

For debugging and performance questions: Always describe your diagnostic process before jumping to solutions. Interviewers want to see that you reach for profiling tools (Systrace, Android Profiler, LeakCanary) rather than guessing.

For product questions about InterviewMaster: Use the app before your interview. Note what works well and one or two things you would improve. Frame improvements as hypotheses to validate, not as criticisms of the existing team.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

InterviewMaster is a product company in the edtech-adjacent space, so the Android team typically values a few things beyond raw coding ability.

Product empathy. They want engineers who understand why the app exists and who think about user experience while writing code. Candidates who have used the product and can speak about it specifically tend to stand out.

Clean, testable code. Expect questions or live coding tasks where the interviewer watches how you structure code, not just whether it compiles. A well-structured ViewModel with proper separation of concerns matters more than memorising every API.

Ownership mindset. Interviewers ask about past projects to see whether you drove decisions or simply executed tickets. Use STAR answers to show initiative and independent judgment.

Android fundamentals depth. Lifecycle management, threading, Jetpack libraries, and memory management are non-negotiable. Candidates report that surface-level answers on these topics do not clear technical rounds.

Clear communication. Remote interviews are common. Speaking through your thought process clearly, especially during debugging or design tasks, signals that you will be easy to collaborate with on a distributed team.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Core Android fundamentals

Revise Activity and Fragment lifecycles, ViewModel, LiveData, StateFlow, and Coroutines. Write small code samples from scratch for each concept. Practise explaining them out loud as if teaching someone who has never used Android.

Week 2: Architecture and Jetpack

Build or revisit a personal project using MVVM, clean architecture, Room, Retrofit, and Hilt. Make sure you can explain every layer and the reason it exists. Add at least a few unit tests using JUnit and MockK.

Week 3: Performance, testing, and system design

Practise one Android system design question each day (offline sync, media player, real-time quiz flow). Use Android Studio's profiling tools on your own apps. Review common memory leak patterns and how to detect them with LeakCanary.

Week 4: Company-specific prep

Download and use the InterviewMaster app thoroughly. Read any recent product updates or blog posts you can find. Prepare two or three observations about the app you could share in the interview. Run at least two mock interviews with a peer or practise your answers out loud.

Ongoing: Review one LeetCode medium problem daily, focusing on arrays, trees, and graphs, as these appear in Android technical screens. 07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping the basics because of seniority. Even experienced candidates get tripped up on lifecycle questions. Interviewers at product companies use these to calibrate fundamentals before going deeper.

Writing code that works but is not structured. During live coding, putting everything in an Activity or skipping error handling signals that you do not write production-quality code in real projects.

Not using the product before the interview. Candidates who have never opened the InterviewMaster app struggle with 'What would you improve?' questions. Spend at least an hour with it before you walk in.

Jumping into design answers without clarifying scope. Skipping the clarification step (offline support? which API versions? real-time or eventual consistency?) is a red flag, especially for senior roles.

Saying 'we' instead of 'I' in behavioural answers. Interviewers want to know your specific contribution, not the team's. Use 'I' to describe what you personally did, designed, or decided.

Ignoring Kotlin and Jetpack Compose. If your experience is primarily in Java or older View-based UI, brush up on Kotlin idioms and the basics of Compose before the interview, as these come up more often in 2026 interviews.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the InterviewMaster Android Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates typically report two to four rounds: an initial HR or resume screening, one or two technical rounds covering Android concepts and live coding, and a final round with a manager or product leader. The exact number can vary by team and seniority level. Remote rounds are common and candidates report reasonably quick scheduling once shortlisted.

Is Jetpack Compose knowledge required for the interview?

Candidates report that product-focused Android teams increasingly ask about Compose even if their production app still uses the View system. You do not need to be an expert, but you should be able to explain the core mental model (composable functions, state hoisting, recomposition) and how it differs from XML layouts. Being able to build a simple screen in Compose is a strong positive signal.

What salary can I expect as an Android Engineer at InterviewMaster?

InterviewMaster has not publicly disclosed its pay bands. For Android Engineer roles in India, Glassdoor and levels.fyi commonly cite a range that varies significantly by experience level and city. Research the company specifically on these platforms and check recent posts in communities like TeamBlind for the most current data before you negotiate an offer.

How long does the InterviewMaster hiring process take from application to offer?

Candidates typically report a process that takes two to four weeks from first contact to offer, though this depends on team bandwidth and how quickly you move through rounds. A polite follow-up after each round is acceptable and shows genuine interest. If you have not heard back within a week of completing a round, a brief check-in is reasonable.

Should I prepare for a DSA (Data Structures and Algorithms) round?

Yes, most Android Engineer interviews at product companies include at least one DSA problem, typically at medium difficulty. Focus on arrays, strings, trees, and graphs, as these appear most often. You are generally expected to code in Kotlin or Java, so practise writing clean, idiomatic Kotlin solutions rather than pseudocode or Java-style Kotlin.

Is there a system design round for Android Engineers?

Candidates report that system design questions appear more often for mid-level and senior roles. These are typically mobile-specific: designing an offline-first feature, a video streaming component, or a real-time quiz flow. Practise the 4-step approach (clarify scope, data model, architecture layers, edge cases) rather than memorising fixed solutions.
