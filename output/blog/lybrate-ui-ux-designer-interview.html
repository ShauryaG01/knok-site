01 Overview

Overview

Lybrate is a digital health platform that connects patients with doctors for online consultations, health queries, and medicine information. As a UI/UX Designer at Lybrate, your work sits at the intersection of healthcare and consumer technology, with a user base that spans metro professionals and first-time app users from smaller cities.

The design role here is not just about making screens look good. Lybrate's core challenge is building trust and clarity for users who are anxious about their health and often not very tech-savvy. Designers are expected to serve two very different users: patients who want quick, reassuring answers, and doctors who need efficient, distraction-free workflows.

Lybrate currently has 3 open UI/UX roles. The interview process typically spans a portfolio review, a take-home design assignment, and 2-3 discussion rounds. Candidates report that interviewers focus heavily on your reasoning process, not just your final designs. Being able to explain 'why you made a choice' matters more than having a polished Figma file.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These are questions candidates commonly report facing in Lybrate UI/UX interviews. Prepare a specific example for each.

  1. Walk us through a project where you improved a complex user flow from start to finish.
  2. How would you design a consultation screen for users in Tier-2 cities who may have low digital literacy?
  3. Lybrate serves both patients and doctors. How do you balance two very different user needs on the same platform?
  4. Tell us about a time you received strong criticism on a design. How did you handle it?
  5. How do you approach designing for trust when users are sharing sensitive health information?
  6. You receive a vague brief from a product manager. What do you do before opening your design tool?
  7. How would you redesign the appointment booking flow to reduce drop-off at the payment step?
  8. What design patterns do you use for progressive disclosure in health content (for example, showing diagnosis details without overwhelming a user)?
  9. How have you collaborated with engineers, doctors, or legal teams when their requirements conflicted with your design?
  10. How do you validate a design quickly when you cannot run a full user research study?
  11. What accessibility guidelines do you follow, and can you share a specific example of applying them?
  12. Tell us about a design decision that did not work as expected. What did you change, and what did you learn?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through a project where you improved a complex user flow.

*Situation:* I was working on a telemedicine app where patients had to complete a lengthy intake form before reaching a doctor. Drop-off was high, especially on mobile.

*Task:* My goal was to reduce friction in the pre-consultation flow without removing any clinically required fields.

*Action:* I audited each step to classify fields as 'must-have before booking' versus 'can be collected during consultation.' I restructured the flow into 3 steps for the patient and moved several optional fields to a post-booking screen. I tested two prototypes with 5 users each (a small sample, so treat results as directional), then refined based on where they paused or made errors.

*Result:* The shorter flow was approved by the product manager and shipped in the next sprint. Exact drop-off improvement was hard to measure due to limited analytics at the time, but user feedback in app reviews improved noticeably in the following weeks.

---

Q: How do you balance two very different user needs in a single product?

*Situation:* On a health platform I worked on, doctors complained the interface was cluttered while patients said it lacked enough detail.

*Task:* I needed a design approach that served both without building two separate products.

*Action:* I ran separate interviews with a small group of doctors and patients (directional insight, not statistically significant). Doctors needed speed and scanability; patients needed reassurance and guidance. I proposed role-based view states, where the same underlying screen adapted its information density based on who was logged in. I documented the design rationale in a shared Figma file so product managers and engineers could follow the reasoning.

*Result:* Both groups reported the updated design felt 'made for them' in follow-up feedback sessions. The engineering team appreciated having the logic documented upfront, which reduced back-and-forth during handoff.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you received strong criticism on a design.

*Situation:* A lead doctor reviewed my proposed redesign of a prescription summary screen and said it looked 'too much like a marketing page' and could cause medication errors.

*Task:* I needed to revise the design while understanding exactly what the clinical concern was.

*Action:* Instead of defending my choices, I asked the doctor to walk me through a real prescription they had reviewed recently. That conversation revealed I had deprioritised the drug dosage field because it looked visually heavy. I redesigned the hierarchy to make dosage and frequency the dominant elements, then brought it back for a second review.

*Result:* The doctor approved the revised version and called it 'much closer to what a pharmacist would expect.' This taught me that in health design, clinical accuracy overrides visual elegance.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For design process questions (the most common type at Lybrate), use this four-part structure: Problem, Insight, Solution, Outcome. Start by stating what problem you were solving and for whom. Share one key insight from research or observation that shaped your direction. Describe the solution and why you made the specific choices you did. End with what happened: a shipped feature, a validated prototype, or a lesson learned.

For 'how would you design X' questions (common in design assignments and live problem rounds), use a discovery-first approach. Open with: 'Before jumping to wireframes, I would want to understand...' and list 2-3 user or business questions. This signals to interviewers that you think in problems, not just pixels.

For behavioural questions (collaboration, conflict, feedback), use the STAR format: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task brief, one or two sentences each. Spend most of your time on Action, specifically what you personally did, not what your team did. End with a concrete result, even if it is qualitative.

For trade-off questions (balancing user needs, time versus quality), name the trade-off explicitly before picking a side. Interviewers at product companies like Lybrate want to see that you understand real constraints, not just that you have good design taste.

For portfolio walkthroughs: lead with the problem and the user, not the tool you used or the number of screens you made. Save Figma screenshots for the 'here is what I built' moment, not the opening.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Lybrate interviewers, based on what candidates typically report, look for a specific combination of skills and mindset.

Health domain empathy. You do not need a medical background, but you must show you understand that health decisions carry emotional weight. A user searching 'chest pain symptoms' at midnight is not in the same headspace as someone browsing an e-commerce app. Designs that treat health content the same as generic content tend to get pushback.

Simplicity over cleverness. Lybrate's user base is broad. Interviewers want to see that your default instinct is to remove friction, not add features. If you have examples of designs where you deliberately left something out, share them.

Ability to defend choices without being defensive. Design reviews at Lybrate reportedly involve doctors, product managers, and sometimes senior leadership. You need to hold your reasoning firmly while staying genuinely open to correction when domain expertise challenges your assumptions.

Cross-functional fluency. UI/UX at Lybrate means working closely with engineers on handoff and with medical content teams on information architecture. Candidates who can speak the language of both groups tend to advance further in the process.

Data awareness, even without heavy analytics experience. You do not need to be a data analyst, but you should describe how you would measure whether a design change worked. Even qualitative signals count: session recordings, support tickets, and user feedback from app reviews.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know the product.
Download the Lybrate app and use it as both a patient and, where accessible, as a provider. Note at least 3 flows where you see friction or inconsistency. These become your 'unsolicited redesign' talking points, which interviewers appreciate as evidence of genuine interest in the company.

Week 2: Sharpen your portfolio.
Select 2-3 projects that best show your process, not just your final output. For each, prepare a 5-7 minute verbal walkthrough using the Problem, Insight, Solution, Outcome structure. If any project involved health, accessibility, or complex information architecture, lead with that one.

Week 3: Practise live problem-solving.
Ask a friend or peer to give you a vague design brief (for example, 'redesign the search experience for a health app'). Practise thinking out loud, asking clarifying questions, and sketching rough ideas on paper or a whiteboard before going digital. Lybrate rounds reportedly include live design exercises.

Before the interview:
Review Lybrate's recent product updates or any publicly available information about their platform. Prepare your 'why Lybrate' answer around the health mission, not just the job description. Have 3-4 specific examples ready covering: a design win, a failure, a collaboration challenge, and a time you changed your mind based on feedback.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping to solutions without framing the problem. A common pattern in Lybrate design interviews is candidates opening Figma or describing screens before establishing who the user is and what problem they have. Interviewers specifically look for problem framing first.

Generic health UX answers. Saying 'I would use calming colours and clear typography' is table stakes. Be specific: which font size for readability on low-end Android devices, which interaction pattern for users who may be distracted or anxious during a consultation.

Ignoring the two-sided platform reality. Lybrate has both patients and doctors as primary users. Candidates who only speak to the patient experience signal a limited understanding of the product.

Overstating sample sizes or research rigour. If you tested with a handful of users, say so. Do not imply it was a statistically significant study. Interviewers in health tech are sensitive to false confidence in data.

Not asking questions during live design rounds. Silence during ambiguous exercises reads as being stuck. Asking 'what does success look like for this feature?' or 'who is the primary user here?' shows design maturity.

Showing only polished final screens in your portfolio. Lybrate interviewers want to see your thinking: sketches, early wireframes, iterations, and the reasoning behind changes. A well-annotated process document is more convincing than a pixel-perfect file with no story.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Lybrate UI/UX interview typically have?

Candidates typically report 2-4 rounds at Lybrate for UI/UX roles. This usually includes an initial screening call, a portfolio review or take-home assignment, and one or two rounds with the product or design team. Some candidates also report a final round with a senior stakeholder. The exact number can vary based on the seniority of the role you are applying for.

Is there a design assignment, and how much effort should I put into it?

A take-home design assignment is commonly part of the Lybrate process. Candidates report being given a problem related to health information or consultation flows, typically with a few days to complete it. Focus your effort on the thinking and documentation, not just polished visuals. A clearly annotated wireframe with well-reasoned rationale is typically valued over a pixel-perfect prototype that lacks explanation.

Do I need prior healthcare experience to get a UI/UX role at Lybrate?

No prior healthcare experience is required, but you should demonstrate genuine curiosity about the domain. Candidates who have done even a few hours of self-directed research (using the app, reading about patient-provider communication, studying health accessibility) tend to stand out. The key signal interviewers look for is that you understand health decisions carry higher stakes than most consumer products.

What design tools should I be comfortable with for a Lybrate interview?

Figma is the most commonly mentioned tool by candidates across Indian health tech companies. Knowing how to create interactive prototypes and share annotated design files is important. Familiarity with user research methods, even lightweight ones like usability testing or interview guides, is also valued. Depth in Figma plus a clear research process is more than enough; you do not need to master every tool available.

How should I handle salary discussion during the interview process?

Research current UI/UX salary benchmarks for health tech companies on Glassdoor or LinkedIn Salary before your interview. Be prepared to give a range rather than a single number, and anchor it to your experience level and the responsibilities of the specific role. You can also ask the recruiter for the approved band early in the process to avoid misaligned expectations on either side.
