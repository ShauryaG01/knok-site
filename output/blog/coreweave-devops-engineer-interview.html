01 Overview

Overview

CoreWeave is a GPU-cloud provider built to handle large-scale AI training and inference workloads. They currently have 309 open roles, making them one of the most active infrastructure hirers in this space right now. Their stack combines bare-metal GPU servers, custom high-speed networking, and Kubernetes at a scale most companies never reach.

Candidates typically report a process of 4-5 rounds: a recruiter call, a technical screen, a live or take-home coding exercise, a system design round, and a final culture conversation. The exact format can vary, so confirm the structure with your recruiter before you begin.

As a DevOps Engineer here, you own infrastructure end to end: provisioning bare-metal nodes, managing Kubernetes clusters for GPU workloads, writing Terraform and Helm, building CI/CD pipelines, and taking on-call shifts when things break. The technical bar is high, especially on Kubernetes internals, Linux networking, and infrastructure-as-code.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

CoreWeave interviews focus heavily on Kubernetes at scale, bare-metal infrastructure, GPU-specific challenges, and incident ownership. These are the questions candidates have commonly reported across rounds:

  1. Walk us through how you would design a multi-tenant Kubernetes cluster to run GPU workloads safely for several teams at once.
  2. How have you handled a large-scale node failure in Kubernetes? What monitoring flagged it first, and how did you recover?
  3. CoreWeave runs bare-metal GPU nodes. How would you automate OS provisioning and configuration management across thousands of servers?
  4. Describe your Helm chart strategy. How do you version charts and manage environment-specific values across dev, staging, and production?
  5. How would you build a CI/CD pipeline for a machine learning training job that needs GPU resources?
  6. Walk us through a production incident you personally owned, from the first alert to the post-mortem.
  7. CoreWeave's network uses high-bandwidth, low-latency fabrics for GPU-to-GPU communication. What do you know about RDMA or InfiniBand, and how would you debug a network bottleneck?
  8. How do you manage Terraform state when multiple engineers are working in parallel? What failure modes do you watch for?
  9. Explain how Kubernetes resource requests and limits interact with GPU scheduling. What happens when a pod requests more GPU memory than is available on any node?
  10. How would you implement observability for a fleet of GPU nodes: what metrics, logs, and alerts would you prioritize first?
  11. Describe a time you reduced infrastructure cost meaningfully. What changes did you make and how did you measure the result?
  12. How do you handle secrets management in Kubernetes at scale? Which tools and practices do you rely on?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use STAR for every behavioral and scenario question: Situation (context), Task (your specific responsibility), Action (what you did step by step), Result (what changed and how you measured it). Practice each answer out loud until it flows naturally in a couple of minutes.

---

Q: Walk us through a production incident you owned.

*Situation:* Our Kubernetes ingress controller started dropping a significant share of requests during peak hours on a Friday evening, affecting several client-facing APIs.

*Task:* I was the on-call engineer. My job was to diagnose the root cause, keep stakeholders informed, and restore service as quickly as possible.

*Action:* I pulled logs from the ingress pods and saw connection timeouts to upstream services. Node CPU and memory both looked healthy. Checking network metrics, I found the load balancer health-check interval had been changed in a recent Terraform apply, marking pods as unhealthy too aggressively. I rolled back that specific change, applied a corrected config, and monitored traffic for several minutes until it normalized. I updated the incident log in real time and sent a clear summary to the team once things settled.

*Result:* Service fully recovered shortly after the first alert fired. I added a CI policy to flag health-check config changes for peer review before any future apply.

---

Q: How would you design a multi-tenant Kubernetes cluster for GPU workloads?

*Situation:* At my previous company, several product teams shared a single Kubernetes cluster and all needed GPU access for model training and inference.

*Task:* I was asked to design a namespace and RBAC structure that isolated teams, prevented resource starvation, and gave the platform team full visibility into GPU consumption.

*Action:* I created one namespace per team with ResourceQuota objects capping GPU requests, CPU, and memory. I set LimitRange defaults so pods without explicit requests still landed within safe limits. I used node taints and tolerations to direct GPU workloads exclusively to GPU node pools. I configured Prometheus with namespace-scoped dashboards so each team could monitor their own usage, while the platform team had an aggregate view across all namespaces.

*Result:* GPU utilization improved because teams stopped over-provisioning out of fear of starvation from neighbors. There were zero cross-team resource conflicts over the following months of operation.

---

Q: Describe a time you reduced infrastructure cost.

*Situation:* We were running staging and dev Kubernetes namespaces around the clock, even though engineers only used them during business hours.

*Task:* My manager asked me to cut the cloud bill without hurting developer productivity.

*Action:* I wrote a script triggered by a Kubernetes CronJob that scaled all non-production deployments down to zero replicas each evening and back up each morning. I also identified several oversized node groups by analyzing actual CPU and memory utilization over a month and right-sized them to match real demand. For long-running batch jobs, I migrated them to spot instances after confirming each job had proper retry logic in place.

*Result:* Staging environment spend dropped meaningfully over the following quarter, confirmed in the team's budget review. The freed capacity went toward additional GPU nodes in production.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is your default for any question starting with 'tell me about a time' or 'walk us through.' Keep the Situation brief (a few sentences), spend most of your time on Action since that is what the interviewer is actually evaluating, and always close with a concrete Result.

For system design questions, use a three-part structure: (1) clarify requirements and constraints before drawing anything, (2) walk through your design component by component with reasoning for each choice, (3) address failure modes and trade-offs explicitly. CoreWeave interviewers reportedly pay close attention to whether you think about failure scenarios, not just the happy path.

For debugging and incident questions, use a Situation-Diagnosis-Fix-Prevention structure. Describe the symptom, explain how you isolated the root cause, walk through the fix, then close with what you put in place so it does not happen again. Answering 'I would just restart the pod' without diagnosing root cause is a red flag at this level.

For 'why CoreWeave' or values questions, anchor your answer in something specific about their infrastructure scale or the GPU-cloud space. Candidates who have read their engineering blog posts or watched their public talks tend to stand out clearly over those giving generic answers about liking challenging problems.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Deep Kubernetes knowledge, not just usage. Interviewers want to see that you understand what happens inside the control plane, not just that you have run kubectl commands. Be ready to explain scheduler behavior, resource QoS classes, and how the kubelet interacts with the container runtime.

Comfort with bare-metal and Linux internals. CoreWeave is not a pure cloud-native company. They run physical servers. Questions about PXE booting, IPMI, kernel parameters for GPU workloads, and network driver tuning come up regularly. If your background is entirely cloud-managed Kubernetes, spend time filling this gap before you interview.

Ownership and incident mindset. CoreWeave serves AI companies with demanding uptime requirements. They want engineers who naturally think about failure modes, monitoring, and post-mortems, not engineers who treat incidents as someone else's problem.

Infrastructure-as-code discipline. Terraform and Helm should feel second nature. Interviewers will probe whether you understand state management, plan reviews, and how to structure IaC safely for large teams working in parallel.

GPU and ML workload awareness. You do not need to be an ML engineer, but you should understand GPU scheduling in Kubernetes, the role of device plugins, and why GPU workloads have different network and storage requirements than typical web services.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Kubernetes deep dive. Review the Kubernetes documentation on the scheduler, resource management, and control plane architecture. Practice explaining what happens when a pod is created, step by step from API request to running container. If you have not worked with GPU device plugins before, read the official docs and set up a small test cluster.

Week 2: Infrastructure-as-code practice. Write or review Terraform modules with remote state, locking, and workspace management. Build or rebuild a Helm chart from scratch, including lifecycle hooks and environment-specific values files. Practice explaining your IaC design decisions out loud to a non-technical audience first, then a technical one.

Week 3: Networking and observability. Review Linux networking basics: iptables, network namespaces, CNI plugins. Read about RDMA and InfiniBand at a conceptual level so you can speak to why CoreWeave relies on them for GPU workloads. Set up a Prometheus and Grafana stack locally and write several custom alert rules end to end.

Week 4: Behavioral prep and mock interviews. Write STAR stories covering: a production incident you owned, a time you improved reliability, a time you reduced cost, a time you disagreed with a technical decision, and a time you mentored or unblocked a teammate. Practice each one out loud until the answer feels natural and confident. Run a few full mock interviews with a peer who can ask follow-ups.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating Kubernetes as a black box. Many candidates can deploy an app with Helm but cannot explain what the scheduler actually does or why a pod is stuck in 'Pending.' CoreWeave interviewers go deep on internals. If your answers stop at 'I ran kubectl apply,' expect follow-ups you will not be prepared for.

Skipping the 'why' in system design. Saying 'I would use Istio for service mesh' without explaining the trade-offs signals shallow knowledge. Always say what problem a tool solves and what complexity or overhead it adds.

Vague incident stories. 'We had an outage and I helped fix it' is not a STAR answer. Interviewers want your specific actions, the tools you used, and the concrete outcome. If you cannot be specific, choose a different example.

Ignoring GPU-specific context. Some candidates prepare a solid general DevOps interview and forget that CoreWeave is fundamentally a GPU company. Not knowing what a Kubernetes device plugin does, or why GPU workloads have specific network bandwidth requirements, will stand out as a gap.

Not clarifying requirements in system design. Jumping straight to a solution without scoping the problem is one of the most common mistakes. Interviewers are evaluating how you think, not just what you know, so ask about scale, team size, and constraints before you start designing.

Underestimating the on-call question. Candidates sometimes treat incident response as a formality. At CoreWeave, being comfortable with late-night pages and owning problems end to end is a core expectation, not a nice-to-have.

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the CoreWeave DevOps interview typically have?

Candidates typically report 4-5 rounds: a recruiter screen, a technical phone screen, a live or take-home coding exercise, a system design session, and a final fit conversation. The exact format can vary by role level, so confirm with your recruiter at the start of the process. Some candidates report that rounds are combined or reordered depending on the hiring team.

Do I need GPU or ML experience to pass the DevOps interview at CoreWeave?

You do not need to be an ML engineer, but you should understand GPU scheduling in Kubernetes, what a device plugin does, and why GPU-to-GPU communication has different network requirements than typical web services. Candidates coming from general-purpose Kubernetes backgrounds should spend dedicated time on GPU-specific topics before interviewing. Showing genuine curiosity and a clear willingness to learn goes a long way if your hands-on GPU experience is limited.

What tools and technologies should I focus on when preparing?

Focus on Kubernetes internals, Terraform, Helm, Prometheus, Linux networking, and CI/CD pipelines. CoreWeave's stack leans heavily on Kubernetes for orchestration and bare-metal Linux for the underlying infrastructure. Familiarity with configuration management tools like Ansible is also useful for questions about provisioning at scale. RDMA and InfiniBand concepts come up in networking rounds, so at least a conceptual understanding is worth building before your interview.

How should I approach the system design round?

Always start by clarifying requirements and constraints before drawing any architecture. Walk through your design component by component, explaining why you chose each piece, not just what it is. End by addressing failure scenarios and trade-offs, since CoreWeave interviewers are specifically interested in whether you think about what happens when things go wrong. Jumping straight to a solution without scoping the problem first is one of the most common mistakes candidates make in this round.
