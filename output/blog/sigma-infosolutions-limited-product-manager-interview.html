03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time you had to prioritise competing features under tight constraints.

*Situation:* I was PM for a B2B SaaS platform serving logistics clients. Several enterprise clients each wanted a different feature in the next sprint, and the engineering team had capacity for only one.

*Task:* I needed to make a defensible prioritisation call that kept clients satisfied and aligned with our quarterly revenue targets.

*Action:* I ran an impact-effort scoring exercise with the team, mapped each feature to a specific revenue or retention risk, and held individual calls with each client stakeholder to explain the trade-off. I set a clear checkpoint at an agreed future date to revisit all the requests and shared that timeline with each client in writing.

*Result:* We shipped the highest-impact feature on time. Two of the three clients accepted the revised schedule without escalation. The third requested a compensatory minor fix, which we delivered in the following sprint. No contracts were at risk.

---

Q: Tell me about a product decision that turned out to be wrong.

*Situation:* I approved adding a self-serve onboarding flow to reduce support load for a mid-market product.

*Task:* The goal was to cut onboarding support tickets and improve time-to-value for new users.

*Action:* I relied on usage data from power users to design the flow, not realising our mid-market segment had a very different comfort level with self-service. I did not run a pilot with actual mid-market users before full rollout.

*Result:* Onboarding completion dropped in the first month after launch. I paused the rollout, ran user interviews over the following weeks, and redesigned several key screens. Completion recovered after the revised version went live. The lesson: validate assumptions with the actual target segment, not the loudest users.

---

Q: How do you handle a situation where engineering and business teams disagree on scope?

*Situation:* The sales team at my previous company had committed a feature to a client that the engineering lead flagged as technically risky and under-scoped.

*Task:* I had to broker an agreement that kept the client commitment intact without burning the engineering team.

*Action:* I facilitated a joint session where both sides mapped out the full scope, risks, and a phased delivery option. I helped translate business impact into engineering language and vice versa, then co-authored a revised scope document both sides signed off on.

*Result:* We delivered a phase one version on the original deadline that satisfied the client's core need. Phase two followed in the next cycle. The engineering lead said later that the structured session saved significant back-and-forth.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the most reliable format for behavioural questions. Lead with context in one or two sentences, state what was specifically on you to solve, walk through what you actually did (not what the team did), and close with a concrete outcome.

For product sense questions, use a structured approach: clarify the goal, identify the user segment, list problems they face, propose solutions, prioritise one, and define success metrics. Interviewers want to see structured thinking, not just a clever idea.

For prioritisation questions, a simple impact-effort or RICE-style reasoning (Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort) works well. You do not need to name the framework explicitly; just show the logic clearly.

For metrics questions, anchor on a North Star metric first, then break it into leading indicators. If the goal is retention, the North Star might be weekly active users, with leading indicators like feature adoption rate and support ticket volume.

One general tip: take a brief moment to frame the problem before proposing a solution. Say something like 'Let me clarify the goal first' before diving in. Interviewers specifically notice structured thinkers.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates report from IT-services PM roles, interviewers at companies like Sigma Infosolutions typically look for these qualities:

Clarity under ambiguity. IT services PMs often operate where client requirements are vague or shifting. Show that you can structure a problem even when you do not have full information.

Stakeholder fluency. You will likely work with clients, internal engineering, and business heads simultaneously. Interviewers probe for whether you can hold multiple perspectives at once without losing your own point of view.

Data-backed decisions. Gut feel is fine as a starting point, but interviewers want to see that you reach for data before committing to a direction. Know your metrics, know what they mean, and know their limits.

Ownership. Questions about mistakes or failures are specifically designed to see if you take responsibility or shift it onto the team and the process. Candidates who own their decisions, even bad ones, come across as more trustworthy.

Communication quality. In an IT services context, PMs are often the translation layer between client-facing teams and engineering. Interviewers pay close attention to how clearly and concisely you explain complex trade-offs.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping straight to solutions. Many candidates hear a product question and immediately pitch a feature. Take a brief moment to frame the problem, clarify the user, and state the goal before proposing anything. Interviewers specifically notice this pattern.

Vague STAR answers. Saying 'we improved the product' is not a result. Quantify where you can, and if you cannot share exact numbers, describe the directional outcome clearly, for example: 'support tickets dropped noticeably in the month after launch.'

Blaming the team or process. When asked about failures or conflicts, candidates sometimes shift responsibility to unclear requirements or a difficult stakeholder. Own your part of the problem. It reads as maturity, not weakness.

Ignoring the IT services context. Sigma is not a pure-play product company. If your answers assume a consumer product or startup environment, they may not land. Frame your experience in terms of client relationships, delivery timelines, and managing expectations across multiple stakeholders.

Not asking questions. At the end of rounds, candidates who have nothing to ask the interviewer come across as uninterested. Prepare a question about the team's current priorities or how success is measured in the PM role.

Over-engineering frameworks. Using a framework is good; narrating the framework name out loud and mechanically filling in boxes is not. Let the structure be invisible and let the thinking be visible.
