01 Overview

OpenEvidence is an AI-powered clinical decision-support platform that gives doctors fast, evidence-based answers at the point of care. It is a venture-backed startup that processes medical queries at low latency, making reliability and infrastructure quality central to the product. 02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up frequently in DevOps interviews at companies like OpenEvidence, based on candidate reports and the nature of the product:

  1. Walk me through a CI/CD pipeline you designed end to end. What tradeoffs did you make?
  2. OpenEvidence handles real-time medical queries. How would you architect a system to keep API latency low and uptime high?
  3. Describe your experience with Kubernetes. How do you handle pod autoscaling and resource limits in production?
  4. How do you manage secrets and credentials securely across multiple environments?
  5. The product is AI-heavy. Have you built or maintained infrastructure for GPU workloads or model inference pipelines?
  6. How would you set up observability (logs, metrics, traces) for a new microservice from scratch?
  7. Tell me about a production incident you owned. How did you handle the response and what changed afterward?
  8. How do you approach infrastructure as code? Describe a Terraform or Pulumi module you wrote.
  9. How would you handle a zero-downtime database migration for a high-traffic service?
  10. We are a startup. How do you balance moving fast with keeping infrastructure stable and auditable?
  11. What IAM access control model do you prefer for cloud environments, and why?
  12. Describe a time you reduced infrastructure costs without hurting reliability or developer experience.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk me through a CI/CD pipeline you designed end to end.

*Situation:* My previous team was deploying a monolith manually using shared SSH credentials. Deployments were slow and introduced frequent rollbacks.

*Task:* I was asked to modernise the delivery pipeline for a high-traffic Node.js API.

*Action:* I moved the codebase to GitHub Actions and added lint, unit test, and security scanning stages. I containerised the app and pushed images to ECR. I wrote Terraform modules to manage ECS task definitions and auto-scaling policies, and introduced environment promotion gates so no code moved to production without passing staging.

*Result:* Deployment time dropped to under 10 minutes. Rollbacks became automated via ECS deployment circuit breakers, and the team shipped more frequently and with more confidence.

---

Q: Tell me about a production incident you owned.

*Situation:* Our main API was returning errors for a significant share of requests after a routine certificate rotation.

*Task:* I was the on-call engineer and needed to identify and fix the issue quickly.

*Action:* I checked our monitoring dashboards and saw elevated errors tied to one service. I traced the issue to a misconfigured secret ARN in the ECS task definition that was still referencing the old certificate. I updated the ARN, forced a new deployment, and added a runbook for certificate rotation to our internal wiki.

*Result:* Errors dropped to baseline within minutes. The post-mortem identified a gap in our change-management checklist, which we updated to include secret ARN verification after any certificate rotation.

---

Q: How have you reduced infrastructure costs without hurting reliability?

*Situation:* Our cloud bill had grown steadily and leadership flagged it ahead of a funding review.

*Task:* I was asked to audit spending and propose cuts that would not affect availability.

*Action:* I used AWS Cost Explorer and Trusted Advisor to identify over-provisioned RDS instances, idle NAT Gateways in dev environments, and unattached EBS volumes. I right-sized the RDS instances during a low-traffic window, deleted unused resources, and set up budget alerts to catch runaway spend early.

*Result:* Monthly cloud spend dropped noticeably with zero production incidents. We also introduced a monthly cost-review ritual so the savings persisted.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For behavioral questions: Use STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result). Keep Situation and Task brief (one or two sentences each) and spend most of your time on Action and Result. Quantify results using real numbers from your own experience.

For system design questions: Start by clarifying constraints (scale, latency requirements, budget). Walk through components in order: compute, storage, networking, observability, and security. Candidates report that OpenEvidence interviewers care about how you handle failure scenarios, not just the happy path.

For incident questions: Interviewers typically want to see that you moved quickly to mitigate, communicated clearly, and followed up with a lasting fix. Avoid framing incidents as someone else's fault.

For cost and efficiency questions: Show that you understand the tradeoff between cost and reliability. Mention tools you actually used (Cost Explorer, Infracost, Kubecost) rather than speaking in abstractions.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Ownership over outcomes. OpenEvidence is a startup, so they typically look for engineers who treat infrastructure as a product, not just a support function. Candidates report that interviewers respond well to examples where you proactively identified and fixed a risk rather than waiting to be asked.

Cloud-native depth. Expect detailed questions on Kubernetes, Terraform, and at least one major cloud provider. Surface-level answers tend to lead to follow-up probes that dig deeper.

Security and compliance awareness. Because the platform is used in healthcare contexts, interviewers appear to value engineers who can speak to IAM least-privilege models, audit logging, and secrets management without being prompted.

AI infrastructure familiarity. OpenEvidence is built on large language models. Even if you have not managed GPU clusters, knowing how model inference pipelines are deployed (via containers, auto-scaling, load balancing) shows relevant context.

Communication and incident culture. Strong candidates tell clear incident stories: what broke, what they did, and what changed after. Blameless post-mortem language is a positive signal.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Being vague about tools. Saying 'I used Kubernetes' without explaining your specific setup (cluster size, how you handled scaling, networking) signals surface-level experience. Name the actual tools and configurations you used.

Skipping the why. Interviewers at product-focused startups often ask why you chose a particular approach. If you picked ECS over Kubernetes, or Pulumi over Terraform, be ready to explain the tradeoff in that specific context.

Treating security as an afterthought. Many candidates describe their architecture and only mention security at the end when prompted. At a company handling medical data, weaving security into your design answer from the start is a stronger signal.

Underestimating the startup context. Describing a process that requires a large specialist team or a months-long rollout does not fit a startup hiring context. Show that you can build and iterate quickly with a small team.

Overloading the incident story. Some candidates spend too long on the technical details of what broke and not enough on how they communicated during the incident and what changed afterward. Interviewers want the full arc, not just the debugging.

Not preparing questions for them. Leaving the 'do you have questions?' moment blank at a startup is a missed signal. Ask about the on-call rotation, current infrastructure challenges, or how DevOps collaborates with ML engineers.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the OpenEvidence interview process typically have?

Candidates report a process that typically includes a recruiter call, a technical phone screen, and one or more system design or live-coding rounds. The exact number of rounds can vary by role level and team. It is worth confirming the format with your recruiter at the start so you can prepare accordingly.

Is there a take-home or coding assignment?

Some candidates report a take-home task involving infrastructure design or a small Terraform or scripting exercise, though this is not confirmed across all hiring cycles. Treat any take-home as an opportunity to show clean, well-structured code and a clear README. If you are unsure, ask your recruiter whether a technical task is part of your specific process.

What cloud platform does OpenEvidence primarily use?

OpenEvidence has not publicly disclosed a single preferred cloud provider, so it is worth checking recent job postings for specific tool mentions. AWS and GCP are the most common platforms at AI startups of this size, based on publicly reported industry patterns. Prepare examples from whichever cloud you know best and be ready to explain how your experience transfers.

Should I focus more on Kubernetes or CI/CD for this interview?

Both areas matter, but candidates report that system reliability and observability tend to come up more often in startup DevOps interviews where the team is small and on-call coverage is shared. That said, CI/CD is foundational and you should be able to walk through a complete pipeline in detail. The safest approach is to prepare one strong, detailed example for each.

Does OpenEvidence ask about HIPAA or healthcare compliance in the interview?

OpenEvidence operates in a healthcare-adjacent space, so interviewers may ask how you handle sensitive data, access controls, and audit logging even if they do not use the word HIPAA directly. Demonstrating awareness of least-privilege IAM, encrypted secrets, and audit trails is a stronger signal than reciting compliance acronyms. Focus on practical controls you have actually implemented.
