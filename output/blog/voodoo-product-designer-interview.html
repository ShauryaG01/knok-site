01 Overview

Overview

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates who have interviewed at Voodoo for Product Designer roles report questions across three areas: portfolio deep-dives, design process, and behavioral situations. Here are the questions to prepare for:

  1. Walk us through a product or feature you designed end-to-end. What problem were you solving and how did you know you had solved it?
  2. How do you approach designing for mobile-first experiences, especially on small screens with one-thumb interaction?
  3. Tell me about a time you received tough feedback on a design. How did you respond and what changed?
  4. How do you balance visual appeal with usability when designing game UI?
  5. Walk me through your process when you have little or no user research to start with.
  6. Describe a situation where you had to push back on a stakeholder or PM to protect the user experience.
  7. How do you handle designing across different screen sizes and device constraints?
  8. Tell me about a collaboration with engineers that got complicated. How did you navigate it?
  9. How do you approach iterating on a design after launch, using data or player feedback?
  10. What makes a great first-time experience (onboarding) in a mobile game or app?
  11. Describe a time you had to cut features or simplify a flow under pressure. What trade-offs did you make?
  12. How do you keep up with trends in mobile design and gaming UX?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for every behavioral question. Keep context brief and spend most of your answer on what you did and what happened.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you received tough feedback on a design. How did you respond?

*Situation:* At my previous company, I had designed a new onboarding flow for a fitness app. After presenting to the full product team, the head of growth said the screens felt 'too polished' and users in our early tests were skipping steps.

*Task:* I needed to rethink the flow quickly. We had a release window coming up and I could not start from scratch.

*Action:* I went back to the session recordings and noticed users were tapping past the value-proposition screens without reading them. I stripped out three screens, replaced text-heavy slides with a single interactive moment, and ran a quick guerrilla test with five colleagues playing the role of new users.

*Result:* The revised flow reduced drop-off in the onboarding funnel noticeably, according to our analytics after launch. I also learned to prototype interactions, not just static screens, before a big review.

---

Q: Describe a situation where you had to push back on a stakeholder to protect the user experience.

*Situation:* A marketing lead wanted to add a full-screen interstitial ad after level two in a mobile game I was designing. The game was in soft launch and retention numbers in the first week were already fragile.

*Task:* My job was to flag the risk without creating conflict, and to offer an alternative that met the monetisation goal.

*Action:* I put together a short brief showing how intrusive interstitials at early levels typically correlate with lower return rates, citing publicly reported industry findings. I proposed a rewarded-ad placement instead, triggered voluntarily after level five, and mocked up both options side by side so the decision was easy to visualise.

*Result:* The team agreed to test the rewarded placement first. After two weeks of soft-launch data, voluntary ad engagement was solid and the marketing lead felt the approach was worth continuing.

---

Q: Walk me through a project where you had little research data to start with.

*Situation:* We were building a new casual game feature with a tight three-week deadline. There were no prior user interviews and no comparable feature in our analytics.

*Task:* I had to make confident design decisions without the safety net of existing data.

*Action:* I ran rapid design cycles: mapped assumptions, sketched five rough concepts, and ran short five-person playtests using paper prototypes in the first week. I treated each playtest as a learning loop, not a validation exercise. By week two I had a working Figma prototype grounded in observed behaviour rather than guesses.

*Result:* The feature shipped on time. Post-launch session data showed players engaging with it consistently in the first few sessions, which gave the team confidence to invest in a second iteration.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR is your core structure. Situation and Task set up context briefly. Action is where you show your thinking and craft. Result closes with what actually happened, ideally something measurable or at least observable.

For portfolio walkthroughs, use a simple three-part structure: (1) the problem and who it affected, (2) the decisions you made and why, (3) what you learned or what changed after launch. Avoid narrating every screen. Interviewers want to understand your judgment, not just your output.

For design process questions, be specific about what you actually do, not what 'good designers' do in theory. Saying 'I always do user research' without a real example is a red flag. Ground every answer in a real project.

For disagreement or pushback questions, show that you separate your ego from your work. The strongest answers show that you used data, examples, or structured options to move a conversation forward rather than just winning an argument.

When you practise, record yourself and listen back. If you are still setting up context when you feel like you should be getting to the action, cut the backstory earlier. The action and result are what interviewers remember.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Voodoo moves fast and builds products at scale, so interviewers are typically looking for designers who can operate with speed and clarity rather than perfection.

Mobile-first instincts. Voodoo's products live on phone screens. Candidates who naturally think about thumb zones, load times, and one-tap interactions stand out over those who treat mobile as a smaller desktop.

Portfolio depth over breadth. Candidates report that interviewers dig into two or three projects rather than scanning a portfolio quickly. Know every decision in your featured work. Be ready to say what you would change today.

Comfort with ambiguity. Hypercasual and hybrid-casual games often have tight timelines and evolving requirements. Interviewers want to see that you can make a confident decision with incomplete information and then adjust based on what you learn.

Collaboration without friction. Voodoo teams are cross-functional and typically lean. Interviewers look for candidates who make engineers' and PMs' jobs easier, not harder. Stories about smooth handoffs, clear specs, and proactive communication land well.

Data awareness. You do not need to be an analyst, but candidates who can talk about how they used player feedback, session data, or test results to improve their designs are viewed more favourably than those who design purely on intuition.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Portfolio and case study prep.
Pick your two or three strongest projects and write a clear narrative for each: problem, decisions, outcome. If any of your work touches mobile gaming, gaming UI, or consumer apps, lead with those. Record yourself walking through a case study and listen back. If you are still setting up backstory when you feel like you should be getting to the action, cut it shorter.

Week 2: Voodoo-specific research.
Download and play two or three of Voodoo's current games. Note the onboarding, the UI patterns, the moment-to-moment interactions. Come prepared to talk about what works and what you would approach differently. Look for individual Voodoo designers on LinkedIn to understand how the team is structured.

Week 3: Practice rounds.
Do at least two mock interviews with a friend or fellow designer. Practice STAR answers for the questions listed in this guide. For design challenges, time yourself and practice presenting your thinking out loud, since many take-home exercises include a follow-up presentation.

Day before: Prepare two or three questions to ask the interviewer. Good options: how does the design team collaborate with game producers, how do you measure success for a UI change, what does the first month look like for a new designer joining the team.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Showing screens without showing thinking. Candidates who open their portfolio and say 'here is what I designed' without explaining why they made each decision are quickly filtered out. The process matters as much as the output.

Claiming research you did not actually do. Interviewers probe. If you say 'we did user research' and cannot say who the users were, what you learned, or how it changed your design, it reads as padding. Be honest about the constraints you worked under.

Ignoring mobile-specific constraints. General UX principles are a baseline, not a differentiator. Candidates who do not think about tap targets, gesture conflicts, or notification patterns during design discussions signal that they have not shipped real mobile products.

Defensive reactions to critique. Voodoo interviewers may poke at your design decisions on purpose. Candidates who defend every choice rigidly come across as difficult to collaborate with. Show that you can engage with a challenge, consider it honestly, and either update your view or explain clearly why you would not.

Under-preparing for the take-home. Candidates report that Voodoo typically includes some form of design exercise. Rushed or template-looking outputs stand out for the wrong reasons. Spend real time on the exercise and make sure your file is clean, annotated, and easy to follow without you narrating it.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Voodoo Product Designer interview typically have?

Candidates report a process that typically includes an initial HR or recruiter screen, a portfolio review with a design lead or hiring manager, a take-home or live design challenge, and a final culture or team-fit conversation. The exact number of rounds varies by team and location. Budget for four to five touchpoints spread across a few weeks.

Is there a take-home design exercise, and how involved is it?

Many candidates report receiving a take-home brief as part of the process, typically with a few days to complete it. The brief usually involves designing a feature or flow for a mobile product. You are generally expected to show your thinking, not just a polished final screen, so annotate your decisions and document your rationale clearly.

What salary can I expect as a Product Designer at Voodoo?

Voodoo's compensation for India-based roles is not publicly reported in detail. For context, knok data shows Product Designer salaries in India ranging from 6-12 LPA at entry level up to 36-55+ LPA at Lead/Principal level. Actual offers at Voodoo will depend on your level, location, and negotiation. Check Glassdoor and levels.fyi for any company-specific data points before your negotiation.

Does Voodoo require gaming experience, or is a general product design background enough?

Candidates report that gaming-specific experience is a plus but not a hard requirement. What matters more is comfort with mobile-first design, fast iteration, and consumer product thinking. If you do not have gaming experience, spending time playing and critically analysing Voodoo's own titles before your interview will help you speak their language convincingly.

How important is the portfolio compared to behavioral questions?

Both matter, but candidates consistently report that the portfolio review is the most weighted part of the process. Your ability to explain your decisions clearly, handle probing questions about your work, and show depth on two or three projects is what moves you forward. Behavioral questions are used mainly to assess collaboration style and how you handle pressure or ambiguity.
