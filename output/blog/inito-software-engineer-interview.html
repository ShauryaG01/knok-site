01 Overview

Overview

Inito is a Bangalore-based health tech startup that builds a hormone-tracking fertility monitor. The device reads four key hormones from urine test strips and syncs results to a companion iOS and Android app, giving users a clear picture of their fertility cycle. Engineering at Inito spans mobile (iOS and Android), backend services, data science, and embedded firmware, so interviews often probe more than one of these layers.

As of July 2026, Inito has 10 open Software Engineer roles listed on job sites. Candidates report the process typically runs 3-4 rounds: a recruiter or hiring-manager screening call, one or two technical rounds covering data structures, algorithms, and system design, and a final round focused on culture fit and startup mindset. The full loop typically takes 2-4 weeks end to end.

Salary benchmarks from the knok jobradar data for Software Engineers in India are: Entry (0-2 years) 6-12 LPA, Mid (3-5 years) 15-25 LPA, Senior (6-9 years) 28-45 LPA, and Lead/Staff (10 years+) 40-65+ LPA. For Inito-specific self-reported numbers, Glassdoor carries candidate-submitted figures for the company.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions reflect publicly reported interview patterns for health tech startups at a similar stage and role profile. Expect a mix of coding, system design, and product-thinking questions.

  1. Design the backend for a hormone-tracking app that syncs readings from a Bluetooth device to the cloud. How do you handle offline syncing and conflict resolution?
  2. How would you architect a notification system that tells a user when her fertility window is opening, based on real-time hormone data?
  3. Walk me through a time you optimised a slow API or a heavy database query. What did you measure, what did you change, and what was the outcome?
  4. How do you ensure data integrity when a physical device can disconnect mid-sync or send duplicate readings?
  5. Inito handles sensitive health data. How do you approach security and privacy at the code level, not just the policy level?
  6. Describe your approach to writing tests for a feature where a bug could cause a user to receive incorrect health information.
  7. Tell me about a time you shipped a feature under pressure. How did you balance speed and quality?
  8. How would you design a data pipeline that ingests a large volume of hormone readings daily and makes them queryable for both the app and the data science team?
  9. Describe a situation where you disagreed with a product or technical decision. How did you handle it?
  10. How do you approach building an offline-first mobile app? What are the hardest edge cases?
  11. Inito is a small, fast-moving team. Tell me about a time you took ownership of something outside your usual scope.
  12. How would you instrument and monitor a critical health feature in production to catch issues before users report them?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a time you optimised a slow API or database query.

*Situation:* At my previous company, the main dashboard API was taking several seconds to load for users with more than a year of historical data. Support tickets about slow load times were increasing each week.

*Task:* I was asked to investigate and bring the response time down significantly without changing the underlying data model.

*Action:* I profiled the query using EXPLAIN ANALYZE and found a missing composite index on the user_id and created_at columns. I also discovered the API was fetching every column for each row when the frontend only needed three fields. I added the composite index, rewrote the SELECT to return only the required columns, and added a cache layer for the most recent month of data since that window was queried on every page load.

*Result:* Response time dropped from several seconds to well under a second. Support tickets related to slow dashboards fell noticeably in the sprint after the change shipped, and the fix held up as the user base grew.

---

Q: How have you handled sensitive user data in a past project?

*Situation:* I worked on a telehealth platform where we stored patient consultation notes and lab results. A security audit flagged that some personal information was being logged in plaintext in our application logs.

*Task:* I needed to audit all logging calls across the backend, remove personal data from logs, and put in a mechanism to prevent future leaks.

*Action:* I wrote a custom log sanitiser that stripped fields matching a blocklist (name, email, phone, and any field mapping to a user record) before writing to our logging service. I added a lint rule to the CI pipeline that flagged direct logging of user objects. I also ran a team retrospective to document which fields were sensitive so the blocklist stayed current.

*Result:* The follow-up audit found zero personal information in logs. The CI lint rule caught new potential leaks in the following quarter before they reached production.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you took ownership of something outside your usual scope.

*Situation:* Our mobile app was crashing on a specific Android model during Bluetooth pairing, and the firmware engineer who owned that layer had just left the company. Users were raising complaints and there was no one to hand the issue to.

*Task:* I was a backend engineer, but I volunteered to investigate since the crashes were blocking an upcoming release.

*Action:* I read through the Bluetooth pairing code, reproduced the crash on a test device, and traced it to a race condition where the app was writing to a device characteristic before the hardware had finished its handshake. I added a retry-with-backoff wrapper around the write call and a timeout that showed the user a helpful 'please try again' message rather than a silent crash.

*Result:* The crash rate on that device dropped to near zero, the release shipped on schedule, and I documented the Bluetooth flow so future engineers had a clear reference. My team lead cited this in my next review as an example of startup-level ownership.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For system design questions (questions 1, 2, 8): Start by clarifying scale and constraints, then walk through components in layers: client, API, data store, async workers, and monitoring. For Inito specifically, always address the device-to-cloud sync layer and how you handle offline or duplicate data. Mention trade-offs explicitly rather than presenting one answer as the only correct one.

For behavioural questions (questions 3, 7, 9, 11): Use the STAR format. Situation (one or two sentences of context), Task (what you were responsible for), Action (what you specifically did, using 'I' not 'we'), and Result (a concrete outcome with a clear before/after if possible). Keep the Situation short and spend most of your answer on Action.

For health-data and reliability questions (questions 5, 6, 12): Show that you think in layers: data in transit (TLS, certificate pinning), data at rest (encryption, field-level masking), access control (who can query what), and auditability (logs that capture access without leaking content). For reliability, talk about how you catch regressions: unit tests, integration tests, canary releases, and alerting on error rates.

For product-thinking questions (questions 4, 10): Demonstrate that you understand the user's stakes. A fertility monitor user is making health decisions based on your data. Errors are not just bugs, they affect real people. Show that empathy first, then back it up with engineering rigour.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Inito is a small health tech team shipping a product that real users depend on for significant personal decisions. Interviewers typically look for four things.

Product empathy. Can you connect a technical decision to a user outcome? Engineers who think only in terms of code and ignore what the product does for the person using it are a poor fit for a mission-driven health company.

Ownership without waiting. Candidates report that Inito values people who see a problem and move on it rather than waiting for a manager to assign it. Stories where you stepped outside your job description tend to land well.

Rigour on reliability. Because the product is health-related, interviewers probe how you think about edge cases, data accuracy, and failure modes. A shallow answer like 'I write unit tests' is not enough. Be ready to talk about your whole quality strategy, from code review to production alerting.

Startup pragmatism. Inito is not a large tech firm. Interviewers are less interested in perfectly optimal solutions and more interested in your ability to ship, learn, and iterate. Show that you can make a reasonable call under uncertainty and revisit it when you have more information.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Understand the product and stack
Download the Inito app and read every public blog post or press release about how the device works. Understand the core data flow: test strip to device to app to cloud to insight. This gives you concrete material for system design questions. Look up publicly reported stack mentions in interview forums, keeping in mind that Inito has not officially published their technology choices.

Week 2: DSA and system design practice
Focus on arrays and strings, graph traversal, and database design, since those come up most in health tech backend interviews. For system design, practise designing a time-series data store, a sync system for IoT devices, and a push notification pipeline. Practise out loud, not just on paper.

Week 3: Behavioural preparation
Write out 6-8 STAR stories from your past work. Tag each one with the theme it covers: ownership, conflict, quality, speed vs correctness, cross-functional collaboration. For each story, prepare a one-sentence headline you can deliver if an interviewer asks you to 'quickly tell me about a time when X.'

Week 4: Mock interviews and research
Do a few mock technical interviews with a peer or on a practice platform. Prepare 3-4 thoughtful questions to ask the interviewer, such as the team's biggest current technical challenge, how on-call works, and how engineering decisions get made. 07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating this like a FAANG interview. Inito values pragmatic thinking and startup ownership. Spending your entire system design answer on theoretical scale you will never reach, while ignoring data privacy or device sync edge cases, signals a mismatch with the role.

Vague behavioural answers. Saying 'we built a feature and it went well' tells an interviewer nothing. Every STAR story needs a concrete result: a metric that moved, a timeline that was met, or a problem that stopped recurring.

Ignoring the health context. Candidates who treat health data like any other data miss the point of the product. If you do not acknowledge that a bug in a fertility tracker carries a different human cost than a bug in a food delivery app, interviewers notice.

Not asking questions. A candidate who asks nothing about the role, the team, or the product signals low interest. Prepare genuine questions, not flattery.

Overcomplicating the technical answer. Inito is a startup. The right answer to most system design questions is the simplest thing that handles the stated requirements reliably. Proposing a complex distributed architecture when a well-structured monolith with a job queue would do the job is a red flag, not a green one.
