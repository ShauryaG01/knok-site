03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: A critical bug surfaces a day before release. How do you handle it?

*Situation:* At my previous company, we were one day from releasing a major product update when I found a data-loss bug that appeared only under a specific user flow.

*Task:* I needed to reproduce it reliably, assess its severity, and escalate without creating panic.

*Action:* I documented the exact reproduction steps, recorded a short screen capture, and flagged it immediately to the engineering lead and product manager. I also ran a quick sweep to check whether related flows were affected.

*Result:* The engineering team patched it within a few hours. The release went out on schedule, and the product manager later said the early escalation saved them from a serious user-trust issue.

---

Q: How do you decide what to automate versus what to keep as manual tests?

*Situation:* On a previous project, we had a large regression suite and the team was spending too much time on manual runs before every deployment.

*Task:* I was asked to identify which tests were worth automating first.

*Action:* I prioritized tests that ran frequently, had stable selectors, and covered core user flows. I left exploratory tests and one-off checks as manual. I also avoided automating tests for features that were still changing rapidly.

*Result:* The automated suite covered the highest-risk paths, and the team could release with confidence without running through a long list of manual cases each time.

---

Q: Tell me about a disagreement you had with a developer over a bug.

*Situation:* A developer marked one of my reported bugs as 'not a bug' because it was technically within spec, but it was causing real confusion for users.

*Task:* I needed to make a case for fixing it without damaging the working relationship.

*Action:* I pulled together user feedback and a short walkthrough showing the exact point of confusion. I framed it as a usability issue, not a blame issue, and suggested a small fix rather than a redesign.

*Result:* The developer agreed it was worth addressing, and we shipped the fix in the next sprint. The approach helped build better collaboration between QA and engineering on that team.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioral questions: Every 'tell me about a time' question expects a clear Situation, Task, Action, and Result. Keep the Situation and Task brief, spend most of your answer on Action (what you specifically did), and always end with a concrete Result.

Risk-based reasoning for prioritization questions: When asked how you decide what to test first or what to cut, show that you think in terms of user impact, likelihood of failure, and how hard the bug would be to catch later. Avoid saying 'I test everything', since that signals you have not faced real-world constraints.

Tool-first, then 'why' for technical questions: When asked about automation or testing tools, name the tool first, then explain why you chose it for that context. Interviewers tend to probe deeper after you name a tool, so know it well before you mention it.

Collaboration framing for conflict questions: Any question about disagreements with developers or product managers is really a question about how you influence without authority. Focus on data, user impact, and shared goals rather than 'I was right'.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Ownership of quality: Product companies want to see that you treat quality as your responsibility and not just a checklist. Show that you raise issues proactively and think about the user experience, not just test cases.

Automation mindset: Manual testing skill is expected, but candidates who can also write and maintain automation scripts, integrate tests into pipelines, and think about coverage metrics tend to stand out.

Clear communication: QA engineers sit between developers and product managers. Interviewers watch for candidates who can explain a bug clearly, push back without being combative, and adapt their communication to the audience.

Practical trade-off thinking: Real QA work involves trade-offs: what to automate, what to defer, what is a blocker versus a nice-to-have. Candidates who can articulate these decisions with a clear rationale are preferred over those who give idealistic, textbook answers.

Domain curiosity: Interviewers typically appreciate candidates who have looked into what abra's product does and who ask informed questions about the tech stack, team structure, or release cadence.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Phase 1: Technical foundation
Revise core QA concepts: test design techniques, boundary value analysis, equivalence partitioning, and defect lifecycle. Brush up on whichever automation framework you list on your resume. If you mention Selenium, be ready to write a basic test from memory. If you mention Cypress or Appium, know the setup and the common challenges.

Phase 2: Automation and tooling depth
Build or revisit a small automation project. Focus on writing clean, maintainable test code rather than a large but messy suite. Practice API testing with Postman or RestAssured. Understand how tests plug into a CI/CD pipeline at a conceptual level, even if you have not set one up end-to-end.

Phase 3: Behavioral prep and company research
Write out STAR answers for the behavioral questions listed above. Research abra's product, the industry it operates in, and any publicly available information about its tech stack. Prepare a few questions to ask the interviewer about team structure, release cadence, and how QA fits into the development cycle.

Practice out loud, not just in your head. Candidates who rehearse answers verbally tend to come across as more confident and concise in the actual interview.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Listing tools without depth: Saying 'I have used Selenium, Cypress, and Appium' without being able to explain the trade-offs between them is a common red flag. Only mention tools you can discuss in detail.

Vague STAR answers: Answers like 'I worked with the team to fix it' or 'we improved quality' do not tell the interviewer what you specifically did. Every Result in a STAR answer should be concrete, even if you cannot cite a metric. 'The release went out with no critical issues' is better than nothing.

Treating QA as purely manual: Even if you are interviewing for a mostly manual role, showing no awareness of automation or quality metrics signals limited career growth to the interviewer.

Not preparing questions to ask: Candidates who say 'no, I think you covered everything' at the end often leave a weaker impression. Prepare at least two thoughtful questions about the team or the product.

Overclaiming on tools or past impact: Interviewers probe deeply. If you say you 'built the entire automation framework', be ready to walk through the architecture, the trade-offs you made, and what you would do differently. Overclaiming and then being unable to back it up is one of the fastest ways to lose an offer.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does abra's QA interview typically have?

Candidates typically report going through a technical screening, a hands-on or case-based round, and one or more behavioral rounds. The exact structure can vary by team and role level, so it is worth asking the recruiter at the start of your process. Being prepared for multiple rounds is the safest approach.

Does abra ask live coding or scripting questions in QA interviews?

Candidates for automation-focused QA roles commonly report being asked to write test scripts or review code during a technical round. For manual QA roles, the focus is more likely on test case design and defect analysis. Either way, practice writing test cases on paper or a shared document before you go in, since writing under observation is different from working in your usual environment.

What salary can I expect as a QA Engineer at abra?

abra does not publicly disclose its salary bands. In the broader Indian market, QA Engineers earn 4-9 LPA at entry level (0-2 years), 9-17 LPA at mid-level (3-5 years), 17-30 LPA at senior level (6-9 years), and 28-45+ LPA for lead roles. For abra-specific figures, check Glassdoor or levels.fyi for self-reported compensation data.

How should I prepare if I come from a purely manual testing background?

Focus on test design, defect lifecycle, and strong STAR answers for behavioral questions. Also show some awareness of automation: understand what Selenium or Cypress does at a high level and be ready to talk about when automation makes sense versus when it does not. Interviewers at product companies increasingly expect QA candidates to have a growth mindset toward automation, even if their current role is mostly manual.

Is domain knowledge of abra's product important for the interview?

Candidates who research the product before the interview consistently report a better experience. You do not need deep domain expertise, but understanding what abra does, who its users are, and what kinds of bugs would matter most to those users helps you give more relevant answers. It also gives you better questions to ask the interviewer, which tends to leave a strong impression.
