02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These 12 questions come up repeatedly in Frontend Engineer interviews at staffing consultancies, based on candidate-reported patterns. Career Maker Consultant Bpc interviews typically follow the same themes.

  1. Walk us through a complex UI component or feature you built from scratch. Interviewers want to hear your decision process, not just the final output.
  1. How do you decide between local state, lifted state, and a global store in React? Expect a follow-up asking you to defend your choice for a specific scenario.
  1. Explain what happens in the browser after a user types a URL and presses Enter. Tests depth of understanding from DNS lookup to page rendering.
  1. How have you diagnosed and fixed performance issues in a frontend application? Be ready to name specific tools such as Lighthouse, Chrome DevTools, and React Profiler.
  1. A client says the website feels slow. Walk through your debugging approach. Common in consulting contexts because client-facing communication is part of the role.
  1. Describe a time you had to learn a new framework or library quickly. What was your approach? Assesses adaptability, which consultancies value highly.
  1. How do you handle it when a backend API contract changes mid-sprint and breaks your UI? Tests cross-team collaboration and how you manage blockers without stalling.
  1. What is your strategy for writing scalable, maintainable CSS in a large codebase? Candidates report answers referencing BEM, CSS Modules, or Tailwind tend to be well-received.
  1. Explain JavaScript closures and give an example from a real project. A classic question that still catches candidates who only know the textbook definition.
  1. How do you make sure your UI is accessible to users with disabilities? Mention ARIA roles, keyboard navigation, and colour contrast at minimum.
  1. How have you handled cross-browser compatibility issues in past projects? Name specific browsers and tools rather than saying 'I tested on multiple browsers'.
  1. How would you guide a junior developer who keeps writing duplicated, non-reusable components? Relevant for senior and lead roles; shows mentorship ability.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR method: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Aim for about two minutes per answer when spoken aloud.

---

Q: Walk us through a complex UI feature you built from scratch.

*Situation:* 'Our e-commerce client needed a product configurator where users could pick colour, size, and add-ons in real time, with a live price update on every selection.'

*Task:* 'I was the sole frontend engineer on the feature, and it had to go live in three weeks.'

*Action:* 'I modelled the configuration flow as a state machine using React Context to avoid prop-drilling five levels deep. I memoised expensive recalculations with useMemo and wrote snapshot tests for each configuration state so regressions would surface immediately.'

*Result:* 'The feature shipped on time. The team reported zero regression bugs in the first two weeks after launch, and the client noted a drop in cart abandonment, though I cannot quote an exact figure without their analytics access.'

---

Q: Describe a time you had to learn a new framework quickly.

*Situation:* 'My previous company won a project built on Vue 3. My entire background was in React.'

*Task:* 'I had one week before the first sprint started and needed to be a contributing member from day one.'

*Action:* 'I mapped Vue concepts directly to React equivalents: computed properties to useMemo, watchers to useEffect, and slots to children props. I built a small internal tool in Vue to get hands-on practice before touching client code. I also paired daily with a Vue-experienced teammate for the first three days.'

*Result:* 'I was contributing production code by the end of week two. My manager said my ramp-up was faster than expected, and I led two Vue features before the project closed.'

---

Q: How have you handled a backend API change that broke your UI mid-sprint?

*Situation:* 'Three days before a key demo, the backend team restructured a response object and several of my dashboard components started throwing errors.'

*Task:* 'I needed to fix the UI quickly without blocking the backend team or delaying the demo.'

*Action:* 'I added a thin adapter layer in our API service file that normalised both the old and new response shapes. My components did not need to change at all. I also raised the incident in the next sprint retro and proposed adopting a mock server contract so both teams could develop independently going forward.'

*Result:* 'The demo ran on schedule. The team adopted the mock server approach in the following sprint, which reduced similar blockers for the rest of the engagement.'

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For behavioural questions (tell me about a time, describe a situation): use STAR. Situation sets context fast, Task clarifies your specific role, Action is where you spend the most time with three to four concrete steps, and Result closes with a measurable or observable outcome. If you lack a hard number, describe what changed: 'the team adopted it', 'the bug did not recur'.

For technical design questions (how would you build X, how do you decide between A and B): use a Why-What-How structure. Start with the constraint or goal (Why), name the approach you would take (What), then walk through the implementation (How). This signals you think before you code.

For debugging or troubleshooting questions (something is broken, a client is unhappy): use a Diagnose-Isolate-Fix pattern. Describe how you gather information first (browser console, network tab, user reports), then narrow to the root cause, then explain the fix and how you would prevent it recurring. Interviewers want a systematic method, not guesswork.

For 'why do you want this role' questions: anchor on two things, the specific technical challenge the role offers and a concrete way your background is directly useful. For a consultancy like Career Maker Consultant Bpc, mentioning that you enjoy working across different client problems typically resonates well with the panel.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Technical depth, not just breadth. Knowing many libraries matters less than demonstrating you understand what is happening under the hood in the two or three you use daily. Expect follow-up questions that go one level deeper than your first answer.

Clear communication. Consultancies often place engineers in client-facing environments. If you can explain a technical decision in plain language, that is a strong signal you will represent the firm well on client projects.

Evidence of ownership. Interviewers respond well to candidates who say 'I decided' or 'I pushed back on that approach because' rather than 'the team did'. Even in collaborative work, be specific about your personal contribution.

Adaptability. Consulting work often involves switching stacks, teams, or domains across engagements. Candidates who can narrate a genuine story of picking something new up quickly stand out from those who have only ever worked in a single codebase.

Attitude to quality. Mentions of code reviews, testing, accessibility, and performance show that you think beyond 'it works on my machine'. You do not need to be a testing evangelist; just show you care about the next developer who touches your code.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Solidify fundamentals

Revisit JavaScript core topics: closures, the event loop, prototypal inheritance, and async patterns including Promises and async/await. Cover React specifically: reconciliation, the virtual DOM, hooks lifecycle, and common performance patterns. Do at least two short coding exercises each day to build recall speed.

Week 2: Build and review projects

Pick two or three projects from your portfolio and write out a STAR story for each one. Prepare to explain every technical decision you made and what you would do differently today. Rebuild one small feature from scratch using a framework you are less confident in, so you have a fresh learning story ready.

Week 3: Mock interviews and system thinking

Practise answering questions out loud, not just in your head. Time yourself: about two minutes per behavioural answer, five to eight minutes for a technical design question. Review the browser rendering pipeline, web performance metrics (Lighthouse categories are a useful guide), and at least one accessibility standard.

Ongoing: Stay current and active

Glance at React and MDN changelogs weekly. Being able to mention one recent update signals you are actively working in the field, not just preparing for interviews. 07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Reciting definitions without context. Saying 'a closure is a function that remembers its outer scope' and stopping there is a weak answer. Always follow a definition with a concrete example from your actual work.

Skipping the 'why' in technical answers. Interviewers do not just want to know what you did. They want to know why you chose that approach over alternatives. Build the habit of always naming at least one option you considered and rejected.

Treating soft-skill questions as a formality. Questions about teamwork, handling feedback, or explaining a past mistake are evaluated seriously. Prepare specific stories rather than generic statements like 'I am a team player'.

Overcomplicating simple questions. If asked to explain flexbox versus CSS grid, a clear two-sentence explanation with one use case each beats a five-minute monologue. Match your depth to the interviewer's follow-up pace and stop when they look satisfied.

Not asking questions at the end. Candidates who ask nothing signal low interest. Prepare two or three genuine questions about the tech stack, team structure, or the kinds of client problems the role involves.

Ignoring performance and accessibility topics. These are no longer bonus areas. Candidates who cannot speak to Lighthouse metrics or basic ARIA attributes are increasingly screened out, especially at mid and senior levels.
