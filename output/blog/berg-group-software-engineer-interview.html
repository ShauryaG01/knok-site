02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These are the questions candidates most commonly report from Berg Group Software Engineer interviews. Technical depth and clear thinking come up repeatedly as priorities.

  1. Walk me through your most complex technical project from start to finish.
  2. How do you debug a production issue when you have limited information and users are actively affected?
  3. Design a system that needs to handle a large volume of concurrent requests reliably. (System design round)
  4. How do you handle a disagreement with a senior engineer about the right technical approach?
  5. Describe a time you had to learn a new technology or framework quickly to meet a deadline.
  6. How do you approach code reviews, both when you are the reviewer and when your own code is being reviewed?
  7. Tell me about a time you identified and fixed a significant performance bottleneck in a live system.
  8. If you joined the team tomorrow, how would you ramp up and start contributing? What would your first few weeks look like?
  9. How do you maintain code quality when the team is moving fast and deadlines are tight?
  10. Describe a situation where you had to balance paying down technical debt against shipping a feature on time.
  11. How do you prioritize when multiple tasks or urgent bugs compete for your attention at the same time?
  12. Tell me about a project you owned that did not go as planned. What did you do and what did you learn?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for every behavioural question. Here are three full examples.

Q: Tell me about a time you found and fixed a significant performance bottleneck in production.

*Situation:* Our main API endpoint was responding slowly during peak hours, triggering user complaints and on-call alerts.

*Task:* I was asked to investigate and fix the issue without a scheduled maintenance window.

*Action:* I pulled application performance logs and spotted that a database query was running inside a loop, once per item, instead of in a single batch call. I rewrote the logic to batch-fetch all items at once, added an index on the most-filtered column, and deployed behind a feature flag so I could roll back instantly if needed.

*Result:* Response time for that endpoint dropped noticeably within the first hour of the fix going live. The team later added batch-fetch checks to our standard code review checklist.

---

Q: Describe a time you had to learn a new technology quickly to meet a deadline.

*Situation:* My team was asked to integrate a third-party payment gateway that none of us had worked with before. The business had already committed to a hard launch date.

*Task:* I volunteered to own the integration end to end, including the test coverage and the security review.

*Action:* I spent the first few days reading the official documentation and building a small proof-of-concept in a sandbox environment. I joined the provider's developer community, found a couple of engineers who had done similar integrations before, and got answers to the edge cases quickly. I wrote integration tests covering both success and failure paths before writing any production code.

*Result:* We shipped on time with no rollbacks. The tests I wrote caught a subtle webhook-replay vulnerability during QA that would have been a serious bug in production.

---

Q: How do you handle a disagreement with a senior engineer about the right technical approach?

*Situation:* A senior engineer wanted to build a custom in-house caching layer. I believed an existing open-source solution would meet our needs and reduce long-term maintenance overhead.

*Task:* I needed to make my case clearly without derailing the project or damaging the working relationship.

*Action:* I prepared a short written comparison covering implementation time, operational cost, and risk for both options. I shared it with the team before our next design meeting and asked for a time-boxed discussion. I made it clear I was open to being wrong, and invited the senior engineer to walk through their reasoning for going custom.

*Result:* The team agreed to use the open-source solution with one targeted customization that addressed the senior engineer's main concern. The decision was written up so future teammates could understand the trade-off.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For behavioural questions: Use STAR without over-engineering it. Keep Situation and Task brief, spend most of your time on Action (the specific steps you personally took), and make the Result concrete. Candidates who give vague closings like 'it went well' typically score lower than those who describe a specific, observable outcome.

For coding questions: Think out loud from the start. Confirm your understanding of the problem before writing a single line. Walk through a brute-force approach first, then improve it. Explain trade-offs between solutions. Candidates report that Berg Group interviewers pay close attention to how you reason, not just whether you reach the answer.

For system design questions: Use a structured approach: clarify requirements (functional and non-functional), discuss scale at a high level, sketch the main components, then go deeper into the parts the interviewer probes. Address consistency, availability, and failure modes. You are not expected to design everything perfectly. Interviewers want to see which trade-offs you reach for and why.

For 'how would you ramp up' questions: Be specific about actions such as reading documentation, pairing with a teammate, or picking a small bug to fix first, rather than offering vague intentions. This signals self-awareness and shows you have done this before.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Candidates who have interviewed at Berg Group typically report that a few qualities stand out across all rounds.

Clear ownership. Can you talk about a project as though you genuinely drove it? Interviewers push back on vague answers with questions like 'What did you specifically do?' If you cannot answer that cleanly, it signals you were a passenger rather than a driver.

Honest communication. Saying 'I do not know, but here is how I would find out' is valued more than bluffing. Candidates consistently report that interviewers respect intellectual honesty and become sceptical when answers feel constructed rather than recalled.

Technical depth where it matters. For coding rounds, strong candidates know their data structures and algorithms, write readable code, and explain their reasoning about complexity and trade-offs. For senior roles, system design fluency matters: candidates who reason well about scale, failure modes, and operational cost tend to stand out.

Collaboration and communication. Interviewers want to see that you can push back respectfully, take feedback without getting defensive, and explain technical decisions to people who are not engineers. Expect at least one question about working with others.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Understand the role. Read the Berg Group job description carefully. Map your past experience to the stated requirements and note any gaps so you can address them honestly if they come up.

Step 2: Refresh coding fundamentals. Technical rounds typically cover arrays, strings, trees, graphs, dynamic programming, and searching or sorting. Practice on platforms like LeetCode or HackerRank. Focus on mid-difficulty problems and make sure you can explain your reasoning, not just produce a working solution.

Step 3: Prepare your STAR stories. Write out several strong stories covering: a hard technical problem you solved, a conflict you navigated, a project you owned end to end, a mistake you made and what you learned, and a time you worked closely with non-technical stakeholders. Practice telling each one out loud.

Step 4: Study system design if relevant. Mid-level and senior candidates should review how to design common systems: a URL shortener, a notification service, a feed. Focus on clarifying requirements, discussing trade-offs, and explaining your reasoning step by step.

Step 5: Research Berg Group. Look at their LinkedIn page, any public engineering blog, and recent news. Understand what their products do and what engineering challenges are common in their space. Interviewers notice when a candidate has done their homework.

Step 6: Prepare genuine questions to ask. Having real questions ready about team structure, the technical stack, deployment practices, or what success looks like in the first few months signals genuine interest and maturity.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping in without clarifying the problem. Many candidates start coding or designing immediately, without confirming their understanding of the problem. Interviewers report this as one of the most common red flags. Take a moment to ask a clarifying question or restate the problem before you begin.

Vague STAR answers. Answers like 'I worked with my team and we fixed the issue' leave interviewers with nothing to evaluate. Be specific: what exactly was the issue, what did you personally do (not 'we'), and what changed as a concrete result?

Not knowing the salary range. Come prepared. Bands commonly cited for Software Engineer roles in India run from 6-12 LPA (entry level), 15-25 LPA (mid level), 28-45 LPA (senior), and 40-65+ LPA (lead or staff), based on industry surveys. Anchoring too low or refusing to give a range both create unnecessary friction.

Staying silent when stuck. In a coding or design round, silence is a bad signal. If you are stuck, say so and narrate your thinking out loud. Interviewers are evaluating your process, not just your final answer.

Not asking any questions. Saying 'No, I think I am fine' when asked if you have questions reads as low interest. Prepare at least a couple of genuine questions about the team or the work.

Rehearsing answers word for word. Candidates who recite scripted answers often sound flat and lose the thread when an interviewer interrupts or asks a follow-up. Know your stories well enough to tell them naturally, not to perform them.

How many rounds does the Berg Group Software Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates typically report a process with a recruiter or HR call, a technical screening, one or two deeper technical rounds covering coding and system design, and a final behavioural discussion. What salary can I expect as a Software Engineer at Berg Group?

Salary bands for Software Engineer roles in India, as commonly cited in industry surveys, run from 6-12 LPA (entry level, 0-2 years), 15-25 LPA (mid level, 3-5 years), 28-45 LPA (senior, 6-9 years), and 40-65+ LPA (lead or staff, 10+ years). Candidates at the junior level typically report fewer or lighter system design questions, with more focus on coding fundamentals and behavioural fit. How long does the Berg Group hiring process take end to end?

Candidates report varying timelines depending on the team and the urgency of the role. Does Berg Group allow me to choose my coding language in interviews?

Candidates typically report that Berg Group interviews let you code in the language you are most comfortable with, though the team's actual production stack may differ.
