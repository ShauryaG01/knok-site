03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for all behavioural questions. Here are three full examples.

---

Q: Describe a time your team faced a major production incident. How did you handle it?

*Situation:* A core booking integration at my previous company began returning errors for a subset of enterprise users during a peak traffic period.

*Task:* As Engineering Manager, I needed to coordinate the response, keep stakeholders informed, and ensure the team resolved the issue without burning out.

*Action:* I immediately set up a dedicated incident channel, assigned a senior engineer as incident commander, and took on the external communication role myself to protect the team from stakeholder pressure. I sent structured status updates to product and account management on a regular cadence. I also rotated engineers during the extended investigation to prevent fatigue.

*Result:* The team resolved the root cause overnight. I facilitated a structured post-mortem the following day, and we shipped two preventive fixes in the next release cycle. Account management later said our communication was a key reason client trust was maintained.

---

Q: How do you develop senior engineers into tech leads?

*Situation:* I had a senior engineer who was technically strong but avoided cross-team collaboration and rarely participated in design reviews outside their own scope.

*Task:* I wanted to help them grow toward a staff or tech-lead path without pushing them into discomfort too fast.

*Action:* After a direct career conversation, I learned they were interested in a staff engineer trajectory. We co-created a growth plan with three milestones over two quarters: co-leading one cross-team design review, mentoring a mid-level engineer, and authoring a technical RFC. I provided coaching throughout, not just feedback at the end.

*Result:* By the end of the second quarter they had led two cross-team reviews, and their RFC was adopted as the team standard for a major migration. They were promoted to tech lead at the next cycle.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you pushed back on a product requirement due to technical constraints.

*Situation:* Product wanted to launch a new reporting dashboard in one week. Our data pipeline was not built for the query patterns the feature required.

*Task:* I needed to communicate the risk clearly while keeping the business goal intact.

*Action:* I prepared a short technical brief showing the gap between pipeline capability and feature requirements, then proposed two options: a scoped v1 that worked within current constraints and could ship on time, or a full version needing an extra two weeks for a pipeline change. I brought product and design leads into a joint call to walk through the trade-offs.

*Result:* The team chose the scoped v1. It shipped on schedule, received positive customer feedback, and the full version followed in the next sprint. The product lead later said the options-framing made the conversation much easier than a flat 'no'.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the default structure for every behavioural question. Keep the Situation brief (two to three sentences), spend most of your time on Action, and always close with a measurable or observable Result.

Options Framing works well for conflict or constraint questions. Instead of explaining why something cannot be done, present two or three alternatives with clear trade-offs. This signals product-engineering partnership rather than a blocker mindset.

Situation-Behavior-Impact (SBI) is useful for feedback and people-management questions. Describe the specific situation, the behavior you observed, and the impact it had before explaining what you did about it.

The 'Why, What, How' structure suits architectural or strategic questions. Start with why the change was needed, then what you decided, then how you executed and got buy-in. This mirrors how senior stakeholders think about decisions.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on Serko's public job descriptions and candidate reports, interviewers typically look for five qualities in Engineering Manager candidates.

Product-engineering partnership. Serko is product-led. Interviewers want to see that you treat product managers as peers, not requesters, and that you can negotiate scope without creating adversarial dynamics.

Reliability mindset. Travel software has real financial consequences when it goes down. Expect questions about your incident management process, post-mortem culture, and how you balance speed with stability.

Distributed team experience. With teams across multiple time zones, Serko values managers who have concrete practices for async communication, documentation, and keeping remote engineers included.

People development depth. Questions about growing engineers are common. Interviewers look for specificity: not 'I give feedback' but 'here is how I structured a growth plan and what resulted from it'.

Scaling mindset. Serko is a growing company. They typically want managers who can identify when a process needs to change as team size increases, and who have done that before.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Study Serko's product. Spend time with the Serko website and any public case studies. Understand who their enterprise clients are and what 'travel and expense management' means operationally. This gives you context to tailor your examples to their domain.

Step 2: Map your stories to their priorities. Pick five to six strong STAR stories from your career. For each one, check whether it covers: incident management, cross-functional collaboration, engineer growth, technical trade-offs, or distributed team dynamics. These are the themes that appear most often in reports.

Step 3: Prepare your reliability and architecture examples. Serko is cloud-native. Be ready to discuss a specific reliability improvement or architectural decision your team made, including the trade-offs you considered and how you drove consensus.

Step 4: Practice out loud. Candidates consistently report that answers which feel clear in your head run long or lose structure when spoken. Practice with a peer or record yourself to check pacing and structure.

Step 5: Prepare two to three thoughtful questions. Good questions signal genuine interest. Ask about engineering culture, how product and engineering collaborate on roadmap, or what success looks like in the first six months. Avoid questions whose answers are on the company website.

Step 6: Check Glassdoor and levels.fyi for any recent interview experience reports from Serko. Even a small number of data points can reveal the specific focus areas for their current interview process.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Staying too high-level. Engineering Manager candidates often describe what their team did rather than what they specifically did. Interviewers are evaluating your judgment and decisions, not your team's output. Use 'I' where it is accurate.

Skipping the result. STAR answers without a concrete result feel incomplete. If you do not have a metric, describe a qualitative outcome: team sentiment improved, the client renewed, the feature shipped without a rollback.

Not tailoring to travel tech. Generic EM answers miss the mark at Serko. Weave in awareness of SLA-sensitive systems, enterprise clients, or distributed operations to show you understand the domain.

Framing conflict as a win-lose. Questions about pushing back or disagreeing with product are tests of collaboration, not assertiveness. Always show how you reached a decision together.

Underestimating the people-development questions. Candidates sometimes treat 'how do you grow engineers' as a soft question and give a brief answer. Serko interviewers typically probe deeply here. Prepare a detailed example with a growth plan, concrete actions, and a clear outcome.

Not asking good questions. Ending an interview with 'I think you covered everything' is a missed signal. Thoughtful questions show you are evaluating fit seriously, which matters to interviewers.
