01 Overview

Overview

Supabase is an open-source backend platform built on PostgreSQL, known for its developer-first culture, fast product iteration, and remote-first setup. With 52 open roles as of July 2026, the company is actively scaling, and Engineering Manager positions sit at the heart of that growth. Expect to lead small, high-ownership teams building infrastructure, realtime features, auth, or storage products that millions of developers rely on daily.

The interview process typically spans several rounds, candidates report, covering behavioural depth, engineering leadership philosophy, cross-functional collaboration, and occasionally a case discussion. There is rarely a formal coding round for EM roles, but interviewers will probe your technical judgment and your ability to communicate with highly skilled engineers.

Salary ranges for EM roles, based on knok jobradar data, sit around 35-60 LPA at the Manager level, 55-90 LPA at Senior Manager, and 90-150+ LPA at Director. Most EM roles in the broader market are concentrated in Bangalore, where 182 of the 975 Engineering Manager openings tracked by knok are currently listed.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How do you lead an engineering team in a remote-first, async environment?

*Situation:* I joined a team spread across Bangalore, London, and San Francisco, with no common overlap hours between all three locations.

*Task:* We needed to ship a major API redesign affecting three dependent services, with tight cross-team coordination required throughout.

*Action:* I introduced a documentation-first culture: every decision, design choice, and trade-off was written down before any synchronous discussion. I set up a daily async standup in a shared doc (not a meeting), created clear ownership maps so engineers never blocked on each other, and held one weekly video sync for relationship-building only, not decisions.

*Result:* The API shipped ahead of schedule. Post-launch, the team reported higher clarity on ownership than on any previous project, and onboarding a new engineer mid-project was significantly faster than the team had experienced before.

---

Q: How do you handle community feedback that conflicts with your roadmap?

*Situation:* Our team was several weeks into a performance refactor when a high-profile open-source contributor filed a detailed GitHub issue requesting a conflicting approach and rallied community support around it.

*Task:* I needed to respond transparently, assess the merit of the suggestion, and protect team focus without alienating our most engaged contributors.

*Action:* I responded on the issue the same day, acknowledged the contributor by name, and asked them to join a short call with our tech lead to walk through both approaches. We documented the trade-offs publicly in a GitHub Discussion. Ultimately, we incorporated one element of their suggestion into our plan and explained clearly why the rest would need to wait.

*Result:* The contributor became one of our most active reviewers during the PR phase. The community appreciated the transparency, and the public discussion thread became a go-to reference for future contributors understanding our architecture decisions.

---

Q: How do you manage engineers who know the technical domain far better than you do?

*Situation:* I was brought in to manage a Postgres extension team where two engineers had been core contributors to the PostgreSQL project for years. My own database depth was solid but not at their level.

*Task:* I needed to earn credibility quickly, create structure without getting in their way, and still make resourcing and priority decisions that required understanding their work.

*Action:* My first month was intentionally listening-heavy. I ran weekly one-on-ones and asked engineers to 'teach me' rather than 'brief me', sharing my notes back with them for correction. I focused on removing what they said slowed them down: unclear priority signals, interrupted deep-work time, and ambiguous on-call ownership. I learned enough to ask sharper questions, not to review their code, but to understand risk.

*Result:* Within a quarter, team sprint predictability improved noticeably. One senior engineer mentioned in their performance review that it was the first time they had felt their needs were understood by a manager. Both engineers remained on the team long-term.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For behavioural questions: Use a tight STAR structure. Supabase interviewers, candidates report, value specificity over polish. Name the actual product or system, state what was at stake, describe your actions in the first person, and give a concrete result.

For 'how would you handle' questions: Lead with your principle, then walk through your decision process step by step. Supabase values written clarity, so interviewers often respond well to answers structured like a well-written internal document.

For technical trade-off questions: Use a 'context, options, chosen path, outcome' structure. Be honest about what you would not know yet and how you would find out. Saying 'I would ask the team' is fine if you explain what specifically you would ask and why.

For open-source and community questions: Show that you understand the dual audience: internal engineers and external contributors. Frame decisions around transparency first, then speed, then technical correctness.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Supabase interviewers are looking for managers who are genuinely developer-empathetic. This means you have either built products for developers or used developer tools so deeply that you think in terms of friction, documentation gaps, and API ergonomics, not just delivery timelines.

Async-first thinking is a strong signal. Candidates who default to 'I would set up a meeting' for every coordination challenge do not land well. Show that you write clearly, document decisions, and build team rituals that work without synchronous presence.

Comfort with open-source dynamics matters more at Supabase than at most companies. Interviewers want to see that you can work in public, handle external feedback graciously, and help your team engage with contributors without losing roadmap focus.

Technical credibility without false authority is the balance they look for. You do not need to know PostgreSQL internals to succeed as an EM at Supabase, but you need to show that you ask good technical questions, understand risk, and trust your engineers while still being able to make judgment calls.

High ownership with low ego is the culture signal. Supabase runs small teams where every person, including the manager, is expected to do the work, not just coordinate it.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know the product deeply. Use Supabase for a real project. Set up a project, try Auth, Storage, Realtime, and Edge Functions. Read the open-source GitHub repo and look at recent issues and pull requests. This is not just background research: interviewers will notice if you have actually used the product.

Week 2: Prepare your STAR stories. Map your past experience to the themes Supabase cares about: remote collaboration, open-source community, developer-facing products, technical trade-offs, and people development. Have six to eight stories ready and practise delivering each in under three minutes.

Week 3: Study their engineering culture. Read the Supabase blog, their GitHub Discussions, and any public talks by Supabase engineering leaders. Look at how they communicate decisions publicly. Borrow their language when it fits naturally in your answers.

Before each round: Write down two or three things you genuinely want to learn about the role, the team, or the challenge they are solving. Interviewers at product-led companies respond well to candidates who ask sharp, curious questions rather than generic prompts.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Talking about process over outcomes. Supabase is results-oriented. Saying 'I introduced agile rituals' without explaining what changed as a result will not land. Always close with the concrete outcome.

Underselling async and writing skills. Many candidates assume interviews are about speaking clearly. At Supabase, your written clarity is also on display. If there is a take-home case or written exercise, treat it as seriously as any live round.

Treating open-source as an afterthought. If you have not engaged with open-source communities before, interviewers will notice. Be honest about your exposure level and explain concretely how you would learn quickly.

Overcomplicating your management philosophy. Supabase values directness. A long answer about frameworks and models reads as noise. Say what you actually do, with a real example.

Not doing the product homework. Candidates who cannot speak to Supabase's actual products or recent product changes signal that the role is not a genuine priority for them.

Leaving 'it depends' hanging. 'It depends' is fine if you immediately explain what it depends on and how you would decide. Stopping there reads as evasive, not thoughtful.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Supabase Engineering Manager interview typically have?

Candidates report a process that typically runs four to six rounds, covering behavioural interviews, a conversation with engineering leaders, and sometimes a cross-functional or case discussion. There is generally no coding round for EM roles, but expect deep technical judgment questions. The exact structure can vary by team, so it is worth asking your recruiter for the specific format upfront.

Do I need deep PostgreSQL knowledge to interview for an EM role at Supabase?

Not necessarily, but you need enough technical grounding to ask sharp questions and understand risk. Supabase builds on PostgreSQL, so familiarity with database concepts, extensions, and reliability concerns will help you speak credibly. What interviewers care more about is your ability to manage highly technical engineers and make good trade-off calls, even when your own depth is lower than the team's.

Is the Supabase EM interview remote or in person?

Supabase is a remote-first company, so candidates report that interviews are conducted entirely over video call. This also means your async communication skills, including how clearly you write and structure your thoughts, may be assessed through take-home exercises or written responses. Treat your written communication with the same care as your spoken answers.

What salary can I expect as an Engineering Manager at Supabase?

Based on knok jobradar data, Engineering Manager roles in the Indian market sit around 35-60 LPA at the Manager level, 55-90 LPA at Senior Manager, and 90-150+ LPA at Director. Supabase is a well-funded international product company, so compensation for strong candidates tends to be on the higher end of these bands. Verify current numbers on Glassdoor or levels.fyi for the most up-to-date benchmarks.

How important is open-source experience for this role?

It is more important at Supabase than at most companies. Supabase's product is open-source, their community is active on GitHub, and their engineering culture values public, transparent communication. You do not need to be a long-time open-source contributor, but you should be able to discuss how you would engage with contributors, handle public feedback on your team's work, and help engineers see community interaction as part of the job.

What is the best way to stand out in a Supabase EM interview?

Actually use the product before your interview. Candidates who have built something real with Supabase, even a small side project, demonstrate genuine interest and give themselves concrete examples to draw on. Pair this with clear, specific answers about your experience managing remote teams, developer-facing products, or open-source communities, and you will stand out from candidates who only prepared generic engineering manager responses.
