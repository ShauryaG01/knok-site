02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions are drawn from publicly discussed interview experiences and the nature of the Platform Engineer role at a healthtech company like Twin Health. Your panel will likely not ask all of them, but preparing for each will cover most of what you will face.

  1. Walk us through how you would design a real-time data ingestion pipeline for continuous glucose monitors and wearables at scale.
  2. How do you approach infrastructure-as-code? Which tools have you used in production and what trade-offs did you encounter?
  3. Twin Health processes sensitive health data. How have you handled data security and compliance (such as HIPAA or similar frameworks) in a previous role?
  4. Describe your experience with Kubernetes. How did you manage cluster scaling and reliability in a production environment?
  5. How would you design a multi-tenant architecture where each patient's data is isolated but the platform stays operationally efficient?
  6. Tell us about a time a production incident happened on your watch. What was your incident response process?
  7. We work with IoT device data that can be noisy or have gaps. How have you handled data quality issues in a pipeline you owned?
  8. How do you manage secrets and credentials in a cloud environment? Walk us through your approach.
  9. Describe how you would set up observability (metrics, logs, traces) for a microservices platform handling health data.
  10. How do you decide between a managed cloud service and a self-hosted solution for a given infrastructure component?
  11. Twin Health is growing fast. How have you scaled infrastructure to keep pace with rapid user growth without breaking reliability?
  12. How do you collaborate with data scientists and ML engineers to ship models to production reliably?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use these as templates. Swap in your own projects and outcomes.

Q: Walk us through how you would design a real-time data ingestion pipeline for wearables at scale.

*Situation:* At my previous company we integrated data from fitness trackers for a corporate wellness product with thousands of active devices.

*Task:* I was responsible for designing a pipeline that could ingest, validate, and store device events with low latency and handle burst traffic during morning and evening usage peaks.

*Action:* I set up an event streaming layer using Kafka, partitioned by device ID to preserve ordering. A consumer service normalised raw payloads, flagged anomalies, and wrote validated records to a time-series database. I used Kubernetes horizontal pod autoscaling tied to Kafka consumer lag so the system scaled during peaks without manual intervention. I also added a dead-letter queue for malformed events so nothing was silently dropped.

*Result:* The pipeline handled bursts reliably and the engineering team could trace any dropped event within minutes. Latency stayed within our agreed target even at peak load.

---

Q: Describe a time you led an incident response for a production outage.

*Situation:* A database connection pool on one of our core services was exhausted during a promotional campaign, taking the product down for a significant period.

*Task:* As the on-call platform engineer that night, I needed to restore service quickly, find the root cause, and prevent recurrence.

*Action:* I immediately rolled back a recent deployment that had changed connection pool settings, which restored service. While the team monitored, I dug into slow-query logs and found a new feature was running unbounded queries under high load. I worked with the backend team to add query limits and reviewed pool sizing across all services. I wrote a postmortem and added a runbook to the on-call wiki.

*Result:* No repeat of the issue in the months that followed. The postmortem format I introduced became the team standard.

---

Q: How have you handled sensitive health or user data in a cloud environment?

*Situation:* At a healthtech startup I joined, the platform stored lab results and prescription data but had no formal data classification policy.

*Task:* I was asked to lead an audit and implement controls before an upcoming compliance review.

*Action:* I inventoried all data stores, tagged them by sensitivity level, and applied encryption at rest and in transit for anything containing personal health information. I moved secrets to a dedicated vault, restricted IAM roles to least privilege, and set up automated scanning to catch unencrypted storage buckets. I also ran a short training session for the backend team on the new policies.

*Result:* We passed the compliance review without any critical findings, and the changes meaningfully reduced our attack surface.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For system design questions: Start by clarifying the scale (how many devices or users, expected throughput, latency requirements). Then describe your architecture in layers: ingestion, processing, storage, and serving. Call out trade-offs explicitly. At Twin Health, weave in data security and compliance considerations because health data has regulatory implications that a general SaaS product does not.

For behavioural questions (STAR): Use Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation brief (one or two sentences). Spend most of your answer on Action, because that is what the interviewer is evaluating. End with a concrete Result, ideally something measurable, or a clear description of the impact on the team or product.

For technical deep-dives: Think out loud. Interviewers at product-led companies like Twin Health want to see your reasoning, not just the final answer. If you are unsure, say 'I would typically approach this by...' and walk through your thinking. Asking clarifying questions is a positive signal, not a weakness.

For 'why Twin Health' questions: Connect your background to their specific mission (chronic disease reversal, continuous wearable data, AI-driven personalisation). Generic answers about 'innovation' or 'growth' do not land as well as a specific reference to their product or a technical problem you find genuinely interesting.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Twin Health interviewers typically look for four things in a Platform Engineer candidate.

Reliability mindset: They operate a health platform where downtime or data loss has real patient impact. Candidates who speak naturally about SLOs, on-call hygiene, incident retrospectives, and error budgets stand out from those who treat reliability as an afterthought.

Data pipeline depth: The core product depends on continuous data streams from wearables and IoT sensors. Strong hands-on experience with event streaming, time-series data, and handling noisy or missing sensor readings is directly relevant to what the team works on every day.

Security and compliance instincts: Health data is regulated. Candidates who bring up encryption, access controls, and audit logging without being prompted signal that they understand the stakes. You do not need a compliance certification, but you should be able to discuss these topics fluently.

Cross-functional collaboration: Platform Engineers at Twin Health work closely with data scientists, ML engineers, and product teams. Candidates who can describe how they have bridged infra and product work, or helped non-infra colleagues ship safely, tend to do well in the final rounds.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Company and domain context
Read everything public about Twin Health's product and their 'Whole Body Digital Twin' approach. Understand how they use continuous monitoring data and the basics of health data handling, including HIPAA-relevant concepts, even if you have not worked in healthtech before. This context will colour your answers across all rounds.

Week 2: System design practice
Practise designing real-time data pipelines end to end. Focus on IoT ingestion, event streaming (Kafka or similar), time-series storage, and observability. Draw out your designs and practise explaining them out loud so you can spot gaps in your reasoning before the interview.

Week 3: Infrastructure and cloud depth
Revisit your primary cloud platform (AWS, GCP, or Azure). Practise explaining Kubernetes cluster management, IaC using Terraform or Pulumi, secrets management, and autoscaling decisions. Prepare specific stories from your own work where you made a trade-off call and can explain why you chose it.

Week 4: Behavioural stories and mock interviews
Write out 6-8 STAR stories covering incidents, scaling challenges, cross-team collaboration, and navigating ambiguity. Do at least two mock interviews with a peer or mentor. Practise staying calm and thinking out loud under pressure, which is a skill that improves with repetition.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping the 'health data is different' angle: Candidates with strong general infra experience sometimes answer as if this were a standard SaaS product. Twin Health's data has patient safety and compliance dimensions. Mention them proactively rather than waiting to be asked.

Over-indexing on tools, not problems: Saying 'I used Terraform' is less compelling than explaining what problem it solved and what trade-off you accepted. Interviewers want to evaluate your judgment, not your tool familiarity.

Vague STAR answers: 'We improved performance' is weak. A specific outcome with context ('we eliminated the primary source of user complaints that quarter by cutting query time significantly') is far more credible. Use concrete outcomes wherever you can.

Skipping clarifying questions in system design: Jumping straight to an architecture without asking about scale, constraints, or existing systems signals poor engineering judgment. Always clarify first, even if you think the answer is obvious.

Not preparing questions to ask: Candidates who ask nothing come across as unengaged. Prepare two or three thoughtful questions about the team's current infrastructure challenges, on-call culture, or how the platform roadmap connects to the clinical product.

Underselling reliability experience: If you have on-call experience, runbook authorship, or postmortem leadership, highlight it explicitly. These are high-trust signals for a healthtech platform role where reliability has direct patient impact.

Frequently asked

How many interview rounds does Twin Health typically have for a Platform Engineer?

Candidates report typically 3-5 rounds. These commonly include an initial recruiter screen, one or two technical rounds covering infrastructure and system design, a coding round, and a final culture or leadership conversation. Round structure can vary by team and hiring manager, so it is worth asking your recruiter for the specific format when you get the call.

What cloud platform does Twin Health use?

Based on publicly available job descriptions and engineering content, Twin Health is generally understood to operate primarily on AWS. That said, candidates report the interviews test cloud-agnostic principles rather than AWS-specific commands. Demonstrating strong fundamentals on your primary cloud platform is more valuable than memorising any single provider's service catalogue.

Is there a coding round, and what should I expect?

Candidates report that coding rounds for Platform Engineer roles at Twin Health tend to focus on scripting, automation, and data processing tasks rather than pure algorithmic puzzles. Python is commonly used. Practise writing clean scripts for tasks like parsing logs, processing streaming data, or automating infrastructure operations, and prioritise readable, maintainable code over clever one-liners.

Do I need prior healthtech experience to apply?

Not necessarily. Candidates with strong infrastructure and data pipeline backgrounds from other industries (fintech, edtech, e-commerce) do advance through the process. However, you should be able to speak credibly about data security, compliance thinking, and why health data requires extra care. Prepare a brief explanation of how you would approach HIPAA-adjacent concerns even if you have not worked in the sector before.
