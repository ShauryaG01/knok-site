01 Overview

Overview

Team8 is a cybersecurity-focused venture creation platform that builds and invests in enterprise security companies. A Security Engineer here works across research, product, and operations, helping portfolio companies design secure systems and respond to threats. As of July 2026, Team8 has 81 open roles across its teams, making it one of the more active cybersecurity employers in the market right now.

For context on the broader hiring picture, there are 628 Security Engineer openings tracked nationally as of the same date. Bangalore leads with 69 positions, followed by Delhi and Pune (12 each), Hyderabad (10), Mumbai (7), and Chennai (6).

Interviews at Team8 are typically technical and scenario-driven. Candidates report multiple rounds covering threat modeling, hands-on security problem-solving, and discussions about how security fits into fast-moving product organizations. Expect questions that go beyond textbook definitions and test how you reason under uncertainty.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates report that Team8 Security Engineer interviews focus heavily on practical thinking and real-world judgment. Here are the questions that come up most often:

  1. Walk me through how you would threat-model a new SaaS application before it goes to production.
  2. A portfolio company asks you to review their cloud infrastructure for misconfigurations. What is your process?
  3. How do you prioritize which vulnerabilities to fix first when the engineering team has limited bandwidth?
  4. Describe a time you had to explain a complex security risk to a non-technical stakeholder and get buy-in to fix it.
  5. What is your approach to incident response when the scope of a breach is still unclear?
  6. How would you design a secrets management strategy for a startup that is scaling quickly?
  7. A developer pushes credentials to a public repository. Walk me through your immediate response.
  8. How do you keep up with the evolving threat landscape, and how do you translate new intelligence into action?
  9. What is the difference between a vulnerability assessment and a penetration test, and when would you recommend each?
  10. How would you set up a security monitoring pipeline for a company that has no existing logging infrastructure?
  11. Describe your experience with identity and access management. How do you balance security controls with developer productivity?
  12. If you discovered a critical zero-day in a third-party library used across multiple portfolio companies, what would you do?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) to structure your answers. Here are three examples tailored to the kinds of scenarios Team8 typically explores.

Q: Walk me through how you would threat-model a new SaaS application.

*Situation:* At my previous company, we were launching a B2B analytics platform that would store sensitive customer data. The engineering team had already started building and had no formal security review in place.

*Task:* I was brought in mid-development to run a threat model before the beta launch.

*Action:* I started by mapping out all data flows, trust boundaries, and entry points. I interviewed the product and backend teams to understand what data was stored, who could access it, and how authentication worked. I then listed threats by category, covering tampering, information disclosure, denial of service, and elevation of privilege, and scored each by likelihood and impact. I flagged the top issues in a document with recommended fixes and effort estimates.

*Result:* The team fixed three critical issues before beta. One of them was an insecure direct object reference in the API that would have exposed customer records to any authenticated user. The structured process also gave the CTO a repeatable template for future features.

---

Q: A developer pushes credentials to a public repository. Walk me through your immediate response.

*Situation:* This actually happened at a startup I worked with. A junior developer accidentally committed an API key for a production payment gateway to a public GitHub repo.

*Task:* I needed to contain the exposure, assess the damage, and put controls in place to prevent a repeat.

*Action:* First, I immediately revoked the exposed key and issued a new one, which moved the risk from active to contained. Then I checked the repository's commit history and secret scanning alerts to see how long the key had been public and whether the repo had been cloned. I pulled access logs from the payment gateway to check for unauthorized activity. I documented the timeline and notified relevant stakeholders within the hour. Finally, I worked with the team to add pre-commit hooks and a secrets scanning step to the CI pipeline.

*Result:* No unauthorized transactions were found. The new controls caught two more accidental credential commits in the following month before they ever reached the repository.

---

Q: How do you prioritize vulnerabilities when engineering bandwidth is limited?

*Situation:* At a cloud security startup, our quarterly scan produced a large number of findings across several services. The engineering team had capacity for roughly twenty items that sprint.

*Task:* I needed a defensible, clear framework for deciding what got fixed first.

*Action:* I scored each finding on exploitability (is there a known exploit in the wild?), exposure (is the affected system internet-facing?), and business impact (what data or functionality is at risk?). I grouped the findings into critical, high, medium, and low buckets. I then met with the engineering lead to align on the top items and drafted a one-page brief for the product manager explaining the business rationale. I also flagged a few low-severity findings that were trivially easy to fix, so the team could knock them out for momentum.

*Result:* The team cleared all priority items within the sprint. The scoring method became the standard process for future scans.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For threat-modeling questions: Use a structured decomposition. Start with assets (what are we protecting?), then actors (who might attack?), then attack surfaces (where are the entry points?), then threats by category. Show that you know the difference between a theoretical threat and a realistic one given the business context.

For incident response questions: Lead with containment before investigation. Interviewers want to see that your first instinct is to stop the bleeding, not to understand every detail before acting. Then walk through investigation, eradication, recovery, and lessons learned in that order.

For prioritization questions: Tie your answer to business impact, not just technical severity. A medium-severity vulnerability on a customer-facing payment system is more urgent than a high-severity finding on an internal dev tool with limited access. Explain your reasoning out loud.

For stakeholder communication questions: Use the 'so what' test. After every technical statement, ask yourself what it means for the business. Translate technical jargon into plain language: instead of naming an exploit type, say what an attacker could actually do and who would be affected. Practice this translation until it is natural.

For 'how do you stay current' questions: Be specific. Name actual sources you read, communities you participate in, or labs you practice on. Generic answers do not stand out. Mention a recent technique or vulnerability you found interesting and what you learned from it.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Team8 interviewers are typically looking for several things beyond technical skills.

Practical judgment over theoretical knowledge. They build real security products and work with portfolio companies that face real threats. They want to see that you can make a decision with incomplete information rather than waiting for certainty.

Clear communication across teams. Security engineers at Team8 often work with product managers, developers, and founders who are not security specialists. Candidates who can explain risk in business terms, not just technical ones, consistently stand out.

Ownership and follow-through. Team8 is a venture environment, which means pace is fast and resources are lean. They want engineers who will see a problem through from discovery to resolution, not ones who file a report and move on.

Genuine curiosity about the threat landscape. Candidates report that interviewers respond well to people who have a real interest in how attackers think, who practice on lab environments, or who contribute to security communities. It signals that you grow on your own, not just when prompted by a manager.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused two-to-three week plan that covers the areas Team8 commonly tests.

Week one: Foundations and threat modeling. Revisit core concepts in network security, application security, and cloud security. Practice walking through a threat model out loud for a sample application, without notes, until you can do it smoothly. Review how common attack patterns work at a technical level, including injection attacks, broken authentication, and cloud misconfigurations.

Week two: Hands-on practice. Spend time in a lab environment. Platforms like Hack The Box and TryHackMe have scenarios that match the kind of applied thinking Team8 tests. Focus on cloud environments since Team8's portfolio is heavily cloud-native. Practice writing up findings clearly, because documentation and communication are part of the job.

Week three: Interview polish and company research. Read about Team8's portfolio companies and the security challenges each one faces. Think about how your past experience maps to their use cases. Practice your STAR answers out loud, ideally with someone who can give honest feedback. Prepare three to five questions to ask the interviewer that show you have done your homework.

On the day: Candidates report that thinking out loud helps. Team8 interviewers want to follow your reasoning, not just hear your conclusion. If you are unsure, say so and explain how you would find out.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Giving textbook answers to practical questions. Saying 'I would follow the incident response lifecycle' without walking through what you would actually do in that specific scenario is a common miss. Make your answers concrete and specific to the situation.

Skipping the business context. Security engineers who only speak in technical terms lose their audience. Every answer should connect back to what the vulnerability means for customers, revenue, or operations.

Over-claiming tool expertise. If you list a tool on your resume, expect a deep question about it. Only claim what you can demonstrate confidently.

Not asking clarifying questions. In a threat-modeling or scenario question, jumping to an answer without first asking about scope, threat actors, or business context is a red flag. Interviewers want to see you gather information before you act.

Ignoring the human side of security. Team8 works across multiple companies and teams. Candidates who treat security as purely a technical problem and ignore the organizational and communication challenges tend not to advance.

Arriving without questions. Coming to a final round with no questions for the interviewer signals low interest. Prepare specific, thoughtful questions about the team, the portfolio companies, and the security challenges they are actively working on.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Team8 Security Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report a process that typically includes an initial screening call, one or two technical rounds, and a final discussion with a senior team member or hiring manager. The exact structure can vary by team and role level. It is worth asking your recruiter upfront what to expect so you can prepare the right way.

Does Team8 give a take-home assignment for Security Engineer roles?

Some candidates report receiving a take-home exercise, often a short threat model or a written security review of a sample architecture. Not all candidates get one, and the format can change. Treat it as a real-world task, document your thinking clearly, and explain the trade-offs in your recommendations.

What salary can I expect for a Security Engineer at Team8 in India?

Team8 does not publicly list salary bands. Publicly reported ranges for Security Engineer roles in India vary widely depending on level, city, and specialization. Glassdoor and levels.fyi are good starting points for benchmarking. Bangalore typically commands a premium based on commonly cited market data for cybersecurity roles.

Is prior startup experience required to join Team8?

Startup experience is not listed as a hard requirement, but Team8's model of building and investing in early-stage security companies means the environment is fast-paced and resource-lean. Candidates who can show adaptability, a bias toward action, and comfort with ambiguity tend to be viewed favorably, regardless of whether that experience came from a startup or a larger company.

How important is cloud security knowledge for this role?

Very important, based on Team8's portfolio focus. Candidates report that cloud misconfigurations, identity and access management, and securing cloud-native architectures come up frequently in interviews. Hands-on experience with at least one major cloud provider is a strong advantage. If you have gaps here, a few weeks of focused lab practice before your interview can help.

What certifications are helpful when applying to Team8?

No specific certifications are required. Candidates report that practical skills and the ability to demonstrate them in an interview matter more than credentials alone. That said, security certifications that are commonly cited in the industry can help your resume pass initial screening, especially if your experience is light in a particular area. Always be ready to back up any certification with real examples from your work.
