01 Overview

Overview

Divisions Maintenance Group (DMG) is a US-based facilities maintenance company that coordinates maintenance services across a large portfolio of residential and commercial properties. An Engineering Manager at DMG typically leads teams building field-ops platforms, technician scheduling tools, vendor coordination systems, and internal data pipelines that keep maintenance workflows running. The work is operationally critical, so interviewers weight reliability thinking and cross-team collaboration heavily.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time when a system failure directly impacted live operations. How did you lead your team through it and what changed afterward?

*Situation:* At my previous company, our field technician dispatch system went down during peak morning hours. Technicians were not receiving job assignments and coordinators had reverted to manual phone calls.

*Task:* As Engineering Manager, I needed to restore the system as quickly as possible while keeping operations, customer success, and leadership informed throughout.

*Action:* I opened a dedicated incident channel, assigned one engineer solely to communicate status updates to stakeholders at regular intervals, and had the rest of the team split into two tracks: one diagnosing the root cause and one preparing a rollback. I kept the conversation focused on signals and next actions, not blame.

*Result:* We restored service within the same working day. The incident led us to formalize our runbook and add automated alerting for the affected service. Our response on comparable incidents improved significantly in the months that followed.

---

Q: Tell me about a build-versus-buy decision you made for a core operational tool. How did you frame the evaluation and what did you decide?

*Situation:* Our team needed a vendor onboarding and credentialing workflow. We were tracking certificates and insurance documents manually, which was creating compliance risk and slowing vendor activation.

*Task:* My director asked me to evaluate whether to build internally or integrate a third-party tool and then own the delivery.

*Action:* I ran a structured comparison across our top options, scoring each on time to value, total cost of ownership, data ownership, maintenance burden, and fit with our existing systems. I documented my assumptions clearly and presented the trade-offs to leadership with a specific recommendation rather than just a list of options.

*Result:* We went with a vendor integration for the core workflow and built a thin internal layer for proprietary data. Vendor onboarding time dropped noticeably and the compliance team reported fewer exceptions in the next audit cycle.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you pushed back on leadership on scope or timeline. How did you frame it and what was the outcome?

*Situation:* Leadership wanted a new contractor payment feature shipped quickly to support an upcoming campaign. My team had just finished a major migration and available bandwidth was genuinely limited.

*Task:* I needed to either find a way to meet the deadline or make an honest case for an alternative, without appearing uncooperative.

*Action:* I prepared a capacity breakdown showing current sprint commitments and the estimated effort for the payment feature. I brought the key scenarios to leadership: a full build on the original schedule with quality risks clearly listed, or a scoped-down version delivered on time with the remaining work phased into the next cycle. I framed it as a trade-off decision for them to make, not a complaint.

*Result:* Leadership chose the scoped version. The feature launched on time and the follow-on work was sequenced into the next quarter. The conversation also improved my working relationship with the VP because the transparency gave them something concrete to act on.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioral questions (the primary format at DMG)

Most DMG EM questions are behavioral. Use Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep the Situation to one or two sentences, spend the most time on Action (what you specifically did, not what the team did), and close with a concrete Result tied to business or team impact.

Context, Constraints, Decision, Outcome for systems questions

When asked about architecture, tooling, or reliability trade-offs, use this framing: what the system did and at what scale (Context), what limited your options (Constraints), what you chose and why (Decision), and what happened (Outcome). This shows engineering judgment without turning the conversation into a whiteboard session.

Competing Interests, Information Gathered, Trade-off Made, Who Decided for stakeholder questions

When asked how you handle prioritisation or conflict, structure your answer around what the competing interests were, how you gathered information, what trade-off you made, and who owned the final call. DMG interviewers care that you can hold a clear point of view while staying genuinely collaborative.

Make the pronoun clear in every answer

For whichever framework you use, 'I decided,' 'I drove,' 'I recommended' lands differently than 'we explored.' Be generous in crediting your team but make your personal contribution unambiguous.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

DMG engineering supports physical operations that run every day, so interviewers are looking for a specific profile.

Operational reliability mindset. Can you build and run systems that hold up during peak load? Do you think about SLAs, incident response, and runbooks without being prompted? Candidates who talk only about feature shipping without discussing reliability tend to score lower.

Real people leadership experience. DMG wants managers who have had hard conversations: underperformance situations, scope pushback, team restructuring. They are less interested in theory and more in what you have actually done and how it went.

Cross-functional fluency. Engineering at DMG works alongside operations, field teams, finance, and vendors. Interviewers want to see that you can communicate in business terms, not just engineering terms, and that you have built those relationships in past roles.

Business impact orientation. Strong candidates connect engineering decisions to outcomes the business cares about: faster technician dispatch, fewer compliance exceptions, lower vendor onboarding friction. Purely technical framing without business context tends to fall flat.

Calm under pressure. Facilities maintenance is time-sensitive. Interviewers listen for how you have behaved when systems failed or timelines compressed, not just your approach when everything went according to plan.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Research DMG's operational domain.
DMG works in facilities maintenance. Before your interview, understand the core workflow: a property manager raises a maintenance request, a work order is created, a technician is dispatched, work is completed and verified. Your engineering stories should connect to some part of this flow, even if your background is in a different industry.

Step 2: Build a bank of STAR stories.
Prepare real examples covering these themes: a major incident or system failure you managed, a stakeholder conflict you navigated, a team performance situation you handled, a technical architecture or tooling decision, a build-versus-buy or vendor evaluation, and a moment when you pushed back on leadership or scope. Having these ready means you can answer most EM questions without scrambling.

Step 3: Refresh your reliability and operations vocabulary.
Be ready to discuss SLAs, on-call culture, incident response, and deployment practices with real examples. You do not need to memorise metrics, but you should be able to describe what 'good' looked like in your past teams and why it mattered to the business.

Step 4: Prepare questions for each interviewer.
For the hiring manager: ask about the team's biggest engineering challenge in the current roadmap. For senior stakeholders: ask how engineering success is measured beyond sprint velocity. Specific, thoughtful questions show genuine interest in how DMG operates.

Step 5: Practice your answers out loud.
EM interviews at DMG are conversational. Your answers will land better if you have spoken them at least once before the real thing. Practice with a peer or record yourself and listen for whether your Result is specific enough to be convincing.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Talking about the team when you should talk about yourself.
Interviewers want to know what you did. Replace 'we implemented' with 'I decided' or 'I drove' where that is accurate. Be generous in crediting your team but make your own contribution clear.

Staying too technical for an EM role.
Going deep on code architecture without connecting it to team impact or business outcome signals you are still operating as an individual contributor. Mention the technical detail briefly, then pivot to how it affected your team's output, the end user, or the business.

Giving process descriptions instead of real examples.
Saying 'I like to have regular 1:1s and set clear goals' is a process description, not an example. Every question should be answered with a real story from a real job. If you do not have a direct example, say so and give the closest relevant story instead.

Underselling your impact.
Indian candidates in particular often stay modest about their contributions. 'Things improved' is not enough. Say what improved, for whom, and why it mattered to the business or the team.

Not preparing questions to ask.
Leaving no time for your questions, or asking generic ones, signals low engagement. Prepare at least two specific questions based on what you have learned about DMG's operations model.
