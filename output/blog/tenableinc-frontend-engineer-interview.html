01 Overview

Overview

Tenable is a cybersecurity company best known for Nessus and its cloud platform Tenable.io, which helps security teams discover, prioritise, and fix vulnerabilities across IT and cloud infrastructure. Frontend Engineers here build dashboards, scan result viewers, and reporting interfaces that enterprise security analysts rely on daily. The work is data-heavy: large tables, real-time charts, and multi-filter search panels are routine, so interviewers care as much about rendering performance and accessibility as they do about clean component architecture.

Tenable currently has 58 open roles across its career listings (knok jobradar snapshot, July 2026). For broader context, knok's data shows 405 Frontend Engineer openings in India at that time, with Bangalore leading at 102 roles, Delhi at 36, and Pune at 11.

The interview process typically spans three to four rounds: a recruiter screen, a technical coding exercise, one or two engineering panels covering JavaScript depth and system design, and a final conversation with the hiring manager. Candidates report that Tenable interviewers pay close attention to how you reason through a problem, not just the answer you reach.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions appear frequently in candidate reports for Tenable Frontend Engineer roles. Prepare a concrete example or explanation for each one.

  1. Walk me through how you would build a real-time vulnerability dashboard that updates live without overwhelming the browser.
  2. Tenable's products display large tables of security findings. How do you render thousands of rows efficiently in React?
  3. Which data visualisation libraries have you used in production (D3.js, Recharts, Chart.js), and what guided your choice?
  4. How do you manage global state in a large React codebase? Walk through a past decision and the tradeoffs you weighed.
  5. Security products must be usable by everyone. How do you approach WCAG accessibility compliance in your day-to-day work?
  6. Describe a time you diagnosed and fixed a slow page or component. Which tools did you reach for?
  7. How would you design a shared component library that multiple product teams can consume without creating conflicts?
  8. Tenable handles sensitive security data. What front-end practices (CSP headers, XSS prevention, input sanitisation) have you applied in past projects?
  9. How have you worked with REST or GraphQL APIs that return large, deeply nested data structures?
  10. What is your front-end testing strategy? How do you test a complex interactive UI?
  11. Tell me about a disagreement with a designer or product manager over a UI decision. How did you handle it?
  12. How do you keep up with the JavaScript ecosystem? What have you adopted recently and why?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How do you render thousands of rows efficiently in React?

*Situation:* At my previous company we built an audit-log viewer that could return tens of thousands of rows from a backend query.

*Task:* The initial implementation mounted all rows at once, which froze the browser for several seconds on load and made the page unusable on older machines.

*Action:* I introduced windowed rendering using react-window, so only the rows visible in the viewport plus a small buffer were ever in the DOM. I also memoised row components with React.memo and moved sort and filter logic to a web worker to keep the main thread free.

*Result:* Initial render time dropped dramatically, confirmed with the Chrome DevTools Performance tab. Users could scroll through the full dataset without any jank, and the pattern became our team's standard for all large list components.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you improved the accessibility of a product.

*Situation:* Our security dashboard used colour alone to convey severity (red, orange, yellow, green), and our compliance team flagged it as unusable for colour-blind users.

*Task:* I owned the fix as part of a quarterly accessibility sprint with a two-week deadline.

*Action:* I added pattern fills and icon overlays alongside the colour coding, updated all interactive elements to have visible focus rings, and integrated axe-core into our CI pipeline to catch regressions automatically. I also arranged a usability session with a screen-reader user from our UX research panel.

*Result:* We cleared all critical and serious axe-core violations and the compliance team signed off. The usability session surfaced a few additional issues we fixed before shipping, and axe-core now runs on every pull request.

---

Q: Describe a disagreement with a product manager over a UI decision.

*Situation:* A product manager wanted to add several new filter options to an already crowded toolbar ahead of an upcoming sales demo.

*Task:* I had to push back without blocking a release tied to a customer commitment.

*Action:* I put together a quick visual comparison: the existing toolbar at a standard laptop viewport (already hitting breakpoint) versus a collapsible filter panel that kept all the same functionality. I shared it in Slack alongside a note citing support tickets from users who had complained the toolbar was 'too busy'. I proposed shipping the collapsible panel instead, which needed a couple of extra days.

*Result:* The PM agreed after seeing the comparison. The collapsible panel shipped with the demo build, and post-launch session recordings confirmed users were opening the filter panel regularly, validating the approach.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the most reliable structure for behavioural questions. Keep Situation and Task brief, two to three sentences combined, so the bulk of your answer covers Action and Result.

For technical design questions, use a three-step approach: first, clarify requirements and constraints; second, walk through your solution with tradeoffs explained; third, describe how you would validate or test it. Tenable interviewers typically want to hear you think out loud, so narrate your reasoning rather than jumping straight to a solution.

For debugging questions, describe the tools you reached for (Chrome DevTools, React DevTools, Lighthouse, axe-core), the hypothesis you formed, how you confirmed it, and what you changed. Specific tool names and a clear thought process matter more than a generic statement about 'debugging skills'.

For 'tell me about a time' questions, prepare at least one story from each of these areas: a performance win, an accessibility or quality improvement, a cross-team collaboration, and a moment you disagreed with someone and handled it professionally. Tenable's customers are enterprise security teams, so stories that demonstrate care for correctness and reliability land especially well.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Tenable's frontend work is closer to enterprise tooling than consumer apps, and interviewers typically reflect that. Here is what candidates report being evaluated on.

Deep JavaScript knowledge. Expect questions on closures, the event loop, prototypal inheritance, and how React's reconciler works under the hood. Knowing how to use React is not enough. Interviewers want to understand your mental model of why it behaves the way it does.

Performance awareness. Because Tenable's interfaces handle large security datasets, candidates who can discuss virtualisation, memoisation, bundle splitting, and lazy loading stand out clearly from those who cannot.

Security-minded thinking. This is a cybersecurity company. Mentioning XSS prevention, Content Security Policy, or safe handling of user-supplied data in your answers signals cultural alignment, not just technical competence.

Collaboration and communication. Multiple rounds focus on how you work with designers, backend engineers, and product managers. Tenable uses cross-functional squads, so stories showing you navigated disagreements or unblocked teammates carry real weight.

Testing discipline. Candidates report that Tenable teams maintain meaningful test coverage, and reviewers ask about your philosophy. Be ready to discuss the tradeoffs between unit, integration, and end-to-end tests and when you choose each.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Core JavaScript and React depth
Revise closures, the event loop, promises, async/await, and prototypal inheritance. Then go deeper into React: reconciliation, the fibre architecture, when to use useMemo vs. useCallback, and common useEffect pitfalls. Practise explaining these concepts out loud, not just reading about them.

Week 2: Data-heavy UI patterns
Build or review a project that uses windowed list rendering (react-window or react-virtual) and at least one charting library. If you have not used D3.js before, work through a few guided examples so you can describe the mental model in an interview. Optimise a sample page using Lighthouse and Chrome DevTools.

Week 3: Accessibility and security basics
Run axe-core on a project you own and fix the violations you find. Read through the WCAG 2.1 Level AA success criteria at a high level. For security, review OWASP's front-end guidance on XSS and Content Security Policy so you can discuss these topics naturally in conversation.

Week 4: System design and behavioural prep
Practise designing a dashboard component system on paper or in a whiteboard tool. Prepare four to five STAR stories covering performance, quality, collaboration, and conflict resolution. Do at least two mock interviews with a peer or on a practice platform.

Ongoing: Tenable.io has a free trial. Spend an hour exploring it to understand the kind of interface you would be building. This context makes your interview answers noticeably more specific and relevant to the role.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping the 'why' in technical answers. Saying 'I used Redux' is far less convincing than saying 'I chose Redux because multiple teams were writing to shared state and we needed predictable devtools traces.' Tenable interviewers probe the reasoning behind decisions, so prepare your justifications.

Treating accessibility as an afterthought. Candidates who mention accessibility only when directly asked signal that it is not part of their natural workflow. Weave it into your performance and design answers proactively.

Generic STAR stories. A story about 'a React app at a startup' with no measurable outcome is forgettable. Tie your stories to concrete results: load time improvements, defect counts, or user satisfaction changes.

Ignoring the security context. Forgetting that Tenable is a cybersecurity company and giving answers with no mention of data safety or secure coding practices is a missed opportunity at every technical round.

Rushing to code in live exercises. Candidates report that interviewers value the clarifying questions you ask before writing a single line. Take a couple of minutes to align on requirements and edge cases before opening your editor.

Underselling testing. If your answer to 'how would you test this?' is 'I would write some unit tests,' that is likely not enough. Be specific about what you test, what you mock, and what you leave for integration or end-to-end coverage.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Tenable Frontend Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates typically report three to four rounds: a recruiter screen, a technical coding exercise, one or two engineering panels, and a final conversation with the hiring manager. The exact structure can vary by team and level, so ask your recruiter for the full process at the start. Some senior candidates report a system design discussion folded into the engineering panel rather than as a separate round.

Is the coding exercise take-home or live?

Candidates report both formats at Tenable, and the choice sometimes depends on the hiring team. Take-home exercises typically involve building a small feature or fixing a bug in a provided codebase, with a communicated time limit. Live coding sessions are usually in a shared editor and focus on practical problem-solving rather than algorithm puzzles. Confirm the format with your recruiter early so you can prepare accordingly.

What salary can I expect as a Frontend Engineer at Tenable in India?

Tenable does not publish India-specific pay bands publicly, so exact figures are hard to verify independently. knok's jobradar data for the broader Indian market shows mid-level roles (3-5 years) in the 12-22 LPA range and senior roles (6-9 years) in the 24-40 LPA range. For Tenable specifically, check Glassdoor or levels.fyi for publicly reported figures, and use those as a benchmark when negotiating your offer.

Does Tenable offer remote or hybrid work for India-based Frontend Engineers?

Tenable has offered hybrid and remote options for some India-based roles historically, though this varies by team and changes over time. knok's jobradar snapshot from July 2026 shows 58 open Tenable roles but does not break them down by location type for this data set. Check each individual listing for the work-location policy and confirm with the recruiter before your first interview.

Do I need cybersecurity knowledge to interview as a Frontend Engineer at Tenable?

You do not need deep knowledge of penetration testing or network security. However, understanding what Tenable's products do at a high level (vulnerability management, exposure tracking) and being familiar with front-end security concepts like XSS prevention and Content Security Policy will help you give more relevant answers. Spending an hour exploring Tenable.io before your interview is genuinely worth the time.
