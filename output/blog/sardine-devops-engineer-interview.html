01 Overview

Overview

Sardine is a fintech company building real-time fraud detection and compliance infrastructure for banks, payment companies, and crypto platforms. Their systems process high volumes of financial transaction signals continuously, which means their DevOps team operates at the intersection of speed, security, and strict regulatory requirements.

As of the knok jobradar snapshot from July 2026, sardine has 35 open roles, with DevOps Engineer among the positions they are actively filling. Across the broader market, there are 811 DevOps Engineer openings tracked in India, with Bangalore leading at 187 roles, followed by Delhi (40), Pune (37), Hyderabad (28), Chennai (13), and Mumbai (11).

Candidates typically report a process of four to five rounds: a recruiter screen, a take-home or async technical assessment, a system design discussion, a hands-on infrastructure round, and a final culture or team fit conversation. Sardine is a remote-first company, so all rounds are typically conducted over video. Expect questions that probe your instincts around security hardening, infrastructure reliability, and the ability to work autonomously in a lean team.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through how you would design a CI/CD pipeline for a fraud detection microservice that requires zero-downtime deployments.

*Situation:* At my previous company, we ran a real-time risk scoring service where any deployment gap would drop payment authorisations for downstream clients.

*Task:* I was responsible for redesigning the deployment pipeline to eliminate downtime and add a clear audit trail for every release, a compliance requirement from our enterprise customers.

*Action:* I moved from rolling restarts to blue-green deployments on Kubernetes, using Argo Rollouts to automate traffic shifting. I added automated smoke tests that ran against the new version before any production traffic was routed to it. Each deployment step was logged to a centralised audit store with commit SHA, deployer identity, and approval metadata captured via our GitOps workflow in Argo CD. I also added automated rollback triggers based on error rate thresholds in Prometheus.

*Result:* We achieved zero failed deployments causing customer-visible downtime over the following six months. Audit reports that previously required manual effort were generated automatically, satisfying our compliance team and cutting review prep time significantly.

---

Q: A deployment you pushed caused a spike in false positives in the fraud engine. Walk us through your incident response.

*Situation:* We had just deployed a config change to our feature flag service that inadvertently altered the threshold for a fraud scoring rule. Within minutes, our on-call alerting fired on a sharp rise in declined transactions.

*Task:* I was the on-call engineer and needed to contain the impact, communicate clearly with stakeholders, and identify root cause quickly.

*Action:* I immediately rolled back the deployment using our GitOps tooling. I posted live incident updates in the engineering channel every ten minutes so the support and product teams could communicate with affected clients. Once the rollback stabilised metrics, I pulled the diff between the last known-good deploy and the faulty one, identified the config key that had changed, and wrote a post-mortem with a corrective action to add validation tests for any config touching scoring thresholds.

*Result:* The incident was contained within minutes of detection. The post-mortem action item shipped the following sprint, and we had no recurrence of that class of issue in the next quarter.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you reduced infrastructure costs without sacrificing reliability.

*Situation:* Our Kubernetes cluster ran at a low average utilisation rate during off-peak hours because we had provisioned for peak load at all times.

*Task:* I was asked to reduce monthly cloud spend without touching SLAs or introducing complexity the team could not maintain.

*Action:* I implemented Kubernetes Cluster Autoscaler alongside KEDA so that non-critical batch workloads scaled down to near zero overnight. I also moved long-running, interruption-tolerant jobs to spot or preemptible instances, with automatic fallback to on-demand when spot capacity was unavailable. I set up Kubecost to give the team per-namespace cost visibility so engineers could see the impact of their own resource requests.

*Result:* Industry surveys commonly cite meaningful savings for similar autoscaling implementations, and our results fell within that range. More importantly, we had zero SLA breaches during the three months after the change, and cost-awareness became a routine part of pull request reviews.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For system design questions (pipeline architecture, disaster recovery, observability), open by clarifying the constraints you need: SLA requirements, data sensitivity, team size, and compliance scope. Then walk through your design layer by layer, covering compute, networking, data flow, security controls, and observability. Sardine interviewers typically care more about your reasoning and trade-off awareness than about landing on a single correct architecture.

For incident and debugging questions, follow a structured narrative: how you detected the problem, your first containment action, how you communicated during the incident, how you found root cause, and what you changed to prevent recurrence. This maps directly to the STAR format and shows operational maturity.

For behavioural questions about process, team dynamics, or compliance, use STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) and add a fifth beat: what you would do differently now. This signals self-reflection and maturity, qualities sardine's lean team environment values highly.

For 'how do you balance X and Y' questions (velocity vs. compliance, cost vs. reliability), avoid picking a side. Describe the specific levers you use: feature flags, progressive rollouts, automated policy checks in CI, and clear escalation paths. Ground your answer in a concrete example where you navigated that tension successfully.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Sardine's engineering culture is reported to value ownership, directness, and pragmatism. In a DevOps interview, candidates report that interviewers probe for the following qualities.

Security-first thinking. Because sardine handles sensitive financial data, they want engineers who treat security as a default, not an afterthought. Mention IAM least-privilege, secret rotation, network policies, and audit logging naturally as part of your technical answers, not as an add-on at the end.

Reliability instincts under pressure. Real-time fraud detection cannot afford extended downtime. Interviewers look for candidates who have designed for failure, built rollback mechanisms into pipelines, or run chaos experiments to validate their system assumptions.

Comfort with lean, autonomous work. Sardine is a growth-stage fintech with a relatively small engineering team. They typically want engineers who can scope their own work, write their own runbooks, and make reasonable decisions without waiting for sign-off on every step.

Clear communication during incidents. Fraud detection is a customer-facing, revenue-critical system. Candidates who can narrate their incident response clearly and treat post-mortems as learning tools rather than blame exercises stand out.

Compliance and audit awareness. You do not need to be a compliance expert, but familiarity with concepts like immutable audit logs, change management workflows, and access reviews signals that you understand the regulatory context sardine operates in.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Core technical revision

Refresh your Kubernetes fundamentals: pod scheduling, resource limits, network policies, RBAC, and Helm chart structure. Review your CI/CD tool of choice (GitHub Actions, GitLab CI, or Argo CD) and be ready to draw out a complete pipeline end to end. Practice explaining blue-green and canary deployment strategies with their specific trade-offs.

Week 2: Security and compliance deep dive

Read up on secrets management tools such as HashiCorp Vault or AWS Secrets Manager. Review how you have implemented audit logging and access controls in past roles. Prepare a short story about a time security requirements influenced an infrastructure decision you made.

Week 3: System design practice

Practice designing two systems from scratch: a high-throughput event ingestion pipeline and a multi-region disaster recovery setup for a financial service. Focus on articulating trade-offs rather than memorising diagrams. Read any available sardine engineering blog posts or public talks to understand their stack preferences.

Week 4: Behavioural and mock interviews

Write out four to six STAR stories covering incidents, cost optimisation, cross-team collaboration, and a time you pushed back on a bad technical decision. Do at least two mock interviews with a peer who can ask follow-up questions. Aim to deliver each answer in under three minutes.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating security as a separate topic. Many candidates answer infrastructure questions and only add 'and of course we would add security controls' at the end. At sardine, security is part of the architecture from the start. Weave in IAM policies, secret management, and audit logging as natural components of every design you describe.

Vague incident stories. Saying 'I led the response and we fixed it quickly' is not enough. Interviewers want the timeline, the specific tools you used, how you communicated, and what the post-mortem action item was.

Over-engineering system design answers. Candidates sometimes propose architectures that would require a large team to maintain. Sardine's team is lean, so show that you can make pragmatic choices and recognise when a simpler solution is the right one.

Not asking about the stack. Failing to ask what cloud provider, IaC tools, or monitoring stack sardine uses signals low curiosity. Ask early in the process so you can tailor your examples to their actual environment.

Ignoring the compliance angle. DevOps in fintech is different from DevOps at a consumer SaaS startup. Candidates who never mention audit trails, change management, or regulatory context can appear underprepared for sardine's domain.

Claiming sole credit without context. In small-team environments, interviewers notice when every answer is 'I did X' with no mention of collaboration, trade-offs, or peer input. Balance personal ownership with team context.
