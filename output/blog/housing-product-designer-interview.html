01 Overview

Overview

Housing.com is one of India's leading real estate platforms, connecting buyers, renters, and sellers across the country. With 188 open Product Designer roles as of July 2026, it is among the most active hirers in this space right now. Across all companies, knok's jobradar tracked 393 Product Designer openings nationally, with Bangalore (62 roles) and Delhi (33 roles) seeing the highest demand.

Designers at Housing work across property search, listing pages, map-based discovery, home loan journeys, and tools for real estate agents. The work touches some of the highest-stakes decisions Indian users make: buying a home, taking a loan, relocating for work. That context shapes interviews here. Interviewers probe not just your craft, but your empathy for a user who is anxious, often less digitally comfortable, and making a once-in-a-decade financial choice.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up frequently in Housing.com Product Designer interviews, based on what candidates typically report.

  1. Walk us through a project where you simplified a complex user flow. What was the problem and how did you solve it?
  2. Housing's users include first-time home buyers, seasoned investors, and NRIs. How do you design for such a wide range of intent and digital literacy?
  3. How would you improve the property search experience on Housing.com? Walk us through your thinking step by step.
  4. Tell us about a time you used data (analytics, heatmaps, user interviews) to challenge or change a design decision you had already made.
  5. How do you approach designing for users in tier-2 and tier-3 cities who may be on slow connections or older Android phones?
  6. Describe a situation where a stakeholder pushed back hard on your design. How did you handle the disagreement?
  7. Real estate is a high-stakes, high-anxiety category. How does that shape the way you write copy, structure flows, or choose visual hierarchy?
  8. How do you work with PMs and engineers to decide what goes into a release? Walk us through your typical process.
  9. Tell us about a design you shipped that did not perform as expected. What did you learn and what did you do next?
  10. How would you design the map view for property search to help users quickly narrow down options without feeling overwhelmed?
  11. What metrics would you define to measure whether a redesigned property listing page is working?
  12. How do you balance visual polish with usability when the two pull in opposite directions?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for all behavioural questions. Here are three examples built around the real estate and housing context.

Q: Walk us through a project where you simplified a complex user flow.

*Situation:* At my previous company, we had a loan application flow with a high drop-off rate. Internal tracking showed the biggest exit happened midway, where the screen mixed employment detail fields with document uploads all at once.

*Task:* I was asked to redesign the flow to reduce abandonment without removing any required fields the compliance team needed.

*Action:* I ran five moderated user sessions and found people felt overwhelmed because they did not know what documents to have ready before they started. I broke the flow into three distinct phases: eligibility check, document upload, and review and submit. I added a 'what you will need' checklist on the landing screen and a visible progress bar throughout. I worked with engineering to auto-fill fields from data the user had already entered at registration.

*Result:* After launch, the team tracked a clear improvement in completion rates in the first month. The PM shared the numbers in a retrospective, noting that support tickets about form confusion also dropped.

---

Q: Describe a situation where a stakeholder pushed back hard on your design.

*Situation:* I was redesigning a property listing card for a proptech startup. I proposed removing the price-per-sqft figure from the card header to reduce clutter, based on user interviews showing most buyers did not understand the metric.

*Task:* The sales head strongly disagreed. Agents had specifically asked for that number to be prominent, and the sales team saw it as non-negotiable.

*Action:* Rather than arguing from opinion, I set up a quick test with five buyers and five agents. The data showed buyers consistently ignored the figure, while agents found it essential. I proposed a split design: the buyer-facing listing card would tuck the figure into a 'details' section, while the agent portal kept it front and centre. I presented both flows side by side with the research clips.

*Result:* The stakeholder agreed to the split design. It shipped in the next sprint. Agent satisfaction scores in our quarterly survey held steady, and buyer task-completion times improved.

---

Q: Tell us about a design you shipped that did not perform as expected.

*Situation:* I redesigned a property shortlist feature, replacing a simple 'save' button with a comparison tool that let users pin up to four properties side by side.

*Task:* The goal was to help users make faster decisions by comparing specs, price, and location in one view.

*Action:* We shipped the feature to a portion of users. Analytics showed engagement with the comparison view was very low. I ran follow-up interviews with five users who had tried saving properties and found the feature was buried two taps deep, and most users did not know it existed.

*Result:* I redesigned the entry point to be visible directly on the listing card and added a tooltip on first use. Engagement improved in the next measurement period. The lesson: discoverability is part of the design, not a marketing problem.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For 'How would you improve X' questions: Start by stating what you know about the current experience and the users it serves. Name the specific problem you are solving and why it matters. Propose two or three solutions at different effort levels: a quick win, a medium-effort change, and a larger redesign. End with what you would measure to know if it worked. Avoid jumping to visual solutions before you have defined the problem.

For 'How do you work with PMs and engineers' questions: Describe your role at each stage. Discovery: user research and problem framing. Definition: aligning on scope and success metrics. Design: iterating with PM input and getting early engineering feedback on feasibility. Delivery: handoff, QA support, and post-launch tracking. Be specific about the tools or rituals you use, like weekly design reviews or async Figma comments.

For metrics and success questions: Use a simple structure. What is the user trying to do (goal)? What would success look like for them, such as task completion or reduced time on task (leading indicators)? What would success look like for the business, such as listings viewed per session or loan applications started (lagging indicators)? Avoid vanity metrics like raw page views in isolation.

For 'Tell me about a time' questions: Use strict STAR format. Keep Situation and Task brief. Spend most of your time on Action, specifically your personal decisions and the reasoning behind them. Make sure the Result is concrete, even if you cannot share exact numbers.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Empathy for a high-stakes user. Housing interviewers consistently look for designers who understand that buying or renting property in India is an emotional and often stressful process. They want to see that you design to reduce anxiety, not just to improve visual clarity. Candidates who talk about user emotions alongside user flows tend to stand out.

Systems thinking. Housing's product is not one screen. It is a journey across search, shortlist, site visit scheduling, loan applications, and paperwork. Interviewers want to see that you understand how one part of the funnel affects another, and that your design decisions account for what comes before and after the screen you are working on.

Comfort with data. Candidates report that Housing interviewers often ask how analytics or research shaped a specific decision. They are not looking for data scientists. They want designers who can read a funnel drop-off report, form a hypothesis, and design a test to validate it.

Collaboration without authority. Product designers at Housing typically work with PMs, engineers, and content writers who all have strong opinions. Interviewers want evidence that you can align stakeholders who disagree, without letting the design get diluted to the point of uselessness.

Clarity of communication. Your portfolio walkthrough and your interview answers should both be easy to follow. Interviewers often note that designers who explain their reasoning in plain language tend to be more effective day-to-day collaborators.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week one: Know the product. Use Housing.com every day for a week. Go through property search, save listings, try the map view, and start a home loan enquiry. Take notes on what feels confusing or friction-filled. This gives you specific, honest observations to bring into your interview instead of generic UX critiques.

Week two: Build your portfolio story. Pick two or three projects that show range: one research-heavy project, one where you navigated a stakeholder conflict, and one where you measured impact after launch. For each, prepare a five-minute walkthrough using STAR structure.

Week three: Practice out loud. Run through the 12 questions in the 'Most Asked Questions' section of this guide. Record yourself and play it back. The goal is to sound clear and direct, not rehearsed. Ask a friend or colleague to throw follow-up questions at you so you get comfortable going deeper.

Before the interview: Prepare your 'improve Housing.com' answer. Pick one specific feature (the search filters, the listing page, or the saved homes flow) and prepare a structured critique with two or three redesign ideas and the metrics you would watch.

Typically, Housing's process includes a portfolio review, a design challenge or case study, and one or more rounds of behavioural interviews. Candidates report the process runs across three to four conversations, though this varies by team and seniority. 07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Talking about tools instead of thinking. Saying you used Figma, ran a heuristic audit, or did competitive analysis is not the same as explaining why you made specific choices. Interviewers care about your reasoning, not your software stack.

Generic answers to a domain-specific question. Answering 'How would you improve Housing.com?' with generic UX principles (reduce clicks, add whitespace, improve onboarding) without any reference to real estate users, property anxiety, or the competitive landscape tells the interviewer you have not done your homework.

Skipping the problem definition. Jumping straight to a new UI or flow without first stating who has the problem, why it matters, and how you know it exists. This is the single most common mistake in product design interviews.

Weak or vague metrics. Saying you would measure 'user satisfaction' or 'engagement' without being specific. Be precise: time to shortlist, listings contacted per session, or return visits within two days of first search.

Not asking clarifying questions. Candidates who treat 'How would you improve X?' as a solo presentation miss the point. Interviewers expect you to ask: Who is the target user? What does the business care about most right now? What constraints exist? Asking good questions is itself a design competency being assessed.

Portfolio with no outcomes. Showing beautiful screens without explaining what changed in user behaviour or business results after you shipped. Even qualitative outcomes (the PM deciding to expand the feature to all users, for example) are better than silence on results.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Housing.com Product Designer interview typically have?

Candidates typically report three to four conversations for Product Designer roles. This usually includes a portfolio walkthrough, a design exercise or case study, and one or more rounds focused on collaboration and behavioural questions. The exact number varies by team and by the seniority of the role you are applying for.

Do I need real estate or proptech experience to get a Product Designer role at Housing?

You do not need deep domain expertise, but you do need to show genuine curiosity about the space. Interviewers want to see that you have used the product, thought about user anxieties specific to property search, and have a clear point of view on what could be improved. Candidates who walk in without having used Housing.com tend to give generic answers that do not land well.

What should my portfolio include for a Housing.com application?

Include at least one project that shows your end-to-end process: research, ideation, iteration, and a measured outcome. If you have experience with search, discovery, or high-stakes transactional flows (finance, healthcare, logistics), lead with that project. Three well-explained case studies are far stronger than ten projects with no context on your decisions.

What salary can I expect as a Product Designer at Housing.com?

Based on knok jobradar data as of July 2026, entry-level designers (0-2 years) typically see ranges of 6-12 LPA, mid-level (3-5 years) 14-24 LPA, senior (6-9 years) 26-40 LPA, and lead or principal designers 36-55+ LPA. Actual offers depend on your experience, the specific team, and how you negotiate. For additional benchmarks, Glassdoor and levels.fyi have user-reported salary figures for this role.

Is the Housing.com design challenge a take-home exercise or a live session?

Candidates report both formats depending on the team and role level. Some teams give a take-home case study with a few days to complete it, while others run a live Figma or whiteboarding session. It is completely fine to ask your recruiter which format to expect so you can prepare accordingly.

How do I give a strong answer to 'How would you improve Housing.com?'

Pick one specific feature, not the whole product. State the user problem first and back it with an observation from your own experience using Housing.com. Propose two or three solutions at different effort levels, and name what you would measure to confirm your change worked. Specific, structured thinking beats broad ideas in every product design interview.
