03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a system you owned end to end, from requirements to deployment.

*Situation:* Our product detail page had a stock availability check that polled the database on every page load, causing slow response times during peak traffic.

*Task:* I was asked to redesign this so the page could handle peak load without degrading the user experience.

*Action:* I mapped the current flow, identified the database as the bottleneck, and proposed an event-driven cache layer. I wrote the design document, got sign-off from the tech lead, built the service across sprints, wrote integration tests, and coordinated the deployment with the ops team.

*Result:* Page load time for that component dropped noticeably and we had zero incidents at the next major sale event. Another team later reused the same pattern for a similar problem.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you pushed back on a product requirement.

*Situation:* A product manager wanted to store user payment method details directly in our application database to speed up checkout.

*Task:* I needed to flag the compliance and security risk without blocking the overall goal of improving checkout speed.

*Action:* I put together a short document explaining why storing raw card data conflicts with PCI-DSS guidelines and the legal exposure it creates. I came to the meeting with an alternative ready: a tokenisation service that achieves the same checkout speed with none of the risk.

*Result:* The PM appreciated having a solution alongside the concern. We shipped the tokenisation approach and the compliance team later highlighted it as a best practice for the organisation.

---

Q: How would you approach migrating a high-traffic feature from a monolith to microservices?

*Situation:* At a previous company, the order management module inside a monolith was creating deployment bottlenecks for the entire engineering org.

*Task:* I was the lead engineer tasked with extracting it without downtime.

*Action:* I used the strangler fig pattern: built the new order service in parallel, added a routing layer in front of the monolith, and gradually shifted traffic while monitoring error rates and latency. I kept the monolith code in place until we had several weeks of clean production data confirming stability.

*Result:* The migration completed without a single customer-facing incident. Deployment frequency for the order team increased from once a week to multiple times per day.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For coding questions: Before writing a single line, restate the problem in your own words and ask about edge cases (empty input, duplicates, very large inputs). Walk through the brute-force approach first, then optimise. State the time and space complexity of your final solution.

For system design questions: Use a four-step flow. First, clarify the scale and constraints (read-heavy or write-heavy? consistency or availability the priority?). Second, sketch the high-level components. Third, go deep on whichever component the interviewer focuses on. Fourth, discuss trade-offs: what you chose and what you gave up. This signals structured thinking rather than pattern-matching.

For behavioural questions: Use STAR without announcing the framework. Open with one sentence of context (Situation), one sentence on what you personally owned (Task), a few sentences on concrete steps you took (Action), and close with a measurable or observable outcome (Result). Focus on your individual contribution, not the team effort.

For 'why fairdealmarket' questions: Connect your background to the marketplace domain. If you have experience with search, payments, logistics, or seller tooling, name it directly. Show that you understand who their users are and why that makes the engineering problems genuinely interesting.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Product thinking: fairdealmarket serves two user groups: buyers and sellers. Interviewers want to see that you think about who is affected by a technical decision, not just whether the code works. Mention user impact, even briefly, when discussing your system design choices.

Ownership: Candidates report that interviewers follow up to check whether you personally drove something or just contributed to it. Use 'I' statements in behavioural answers and be specific about the decisions that were yours.

Clarity under ambiguity: Marketplace systems involve real trade-offs (speed vs. consistency, seller experience vs. buyer experience). Interviewers watch for whether you ask good clarifying questions before diving in and whether you explain your reasoning as you go.

Scale awareness: Even for features that seem small, think about what happens under high load. Show that you reach for caching, queues, database indexes, and failure-mode planning as natural tools, not just textbook concepts you can name.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

First: Build your story bank. List several projects from your experience. For each, prepare a STAR answer covering what you built, a technical challenge you faced, a conflict or trade-off, and a measurable result. These stories will cover most behavioural questions across rounds.

Second: Sharpen data structures and algorithms. Focus on arrays, strings, trees, graphs, and dynamic programming. Work through problems in increasing order of difficulty. Do not just read solutions: type them out and explain your approach aloud as you go, the same way you will need to in the interview.

Third: Study system design for marketplace problems. Practice designing systems like a product catalogue with search, an order management system, a notification service, and a rate-limiting layer. For each, work through requirements, components, data model, and scaling approach.

Fourth: Research fairdealmarket specifically. Read their engineering blog or any public technical talks if available. Understand the product from a user perspective: what does a buyer see, what does a seller manage, and where does software make or break that experience?

Fifth: Practice out loud. Do mock interviews with a friend or record yourself answering questions. Hearing your own answers helps you catch when you are rambling or skipping the Result step in STAR stories.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping clarification in system design. Jumping straight into a solution without asking about scale, users, or constraints signals that you code before you think. Spend the opening minutes asking questions; it changes the quality of everything that follows.

Weak or missing Results in STAR answers. 'It went well' is not a Result. Mention what changed: latency dropped, incidents reduced, the team shipped faster. If you do not have hard numbers, describe the observable improvement clearly.

Copying a generic architecture without adapting it. Interviewers at marketplace companies notice when a candidate reuses a standard microservices diagram without connecting it to the specific problem. Name the marketplace-specific constraints: catalogue size, traffic spikes during sales, payment reliability.

Asking no questions at the end. Candidates who ask nothing signal low interest or poor preparation. Prepare a few genuine questions about the team's tech stack, how they handle incidents, or what the first quarter looks like for a new hire.

Underselling individual contribution. In behavioural rounds, 'we did this' answers make it impossible for the interviewer to assess you. Clarify your specific role within any team effort, even if the work was highly collaborative.

How many rounds does the fairdealmarket Software Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically includes a recruiter or HR screening call, followed by technical rounds covering coding and system design, and a final behavioural or culture-fit round. The exact structure can vary by team and the level of the role. Ask your recruiter at the start what the process looks like for your specific opening.

What programming language should I use in the coding round?

Most candidates report that fairdealmarket interviewers allow you to choose your preferred language. Pick the one you are most fluent in for data structures work, commonly Python, Java, or C++. What matters more than the language is that you can explain your logic clearly and handle edge cases confidently. Confirm the preference with your interviewer at the start of the round.

Is system design asked for entry-level Software Engineer roles?

Typically, system design is weighted more heavily for mid-level and senior roles. For entry-level positions (0-2 years experience), interviewers usually focus on coding and computer science fundamentals, with design questions at a simpler scope if asked at all. That said, knowing how databases, caches, and queues work will help you at any level.
