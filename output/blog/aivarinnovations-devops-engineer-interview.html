02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates report that Aivari Innovations DevOps interviews focus on practical experience and problem-solving rather than theory alone. These are the questions that come up most often:

  1. Walk us through a CI/CD pipeline you built or owned. What tools did you pick and why?
  2. How do you handle a deployment that starts failing halfway through a production release?
  3. How would you set up monitoring and alerting for a microservices system? Which tools would you use?
  4. Describe your hands-on experience with Kubernetes. How have you handled cluster scaling and node failures?
  5. How do you approach Infrastructure as Code? Walk through a recent project using Terraform, Pulumi, or Ansible.
  6. Tell us about a time you reduced deployment time or improved release frequency. What exactly did you change?
  7. How do you manage secrets and credentials securely across development, staging, and production environments?
  8. What does a good Dockerfile look like for a production workload? What do engineers typically get wrong?
  9. How would you design a disaster recovery plan for a critical service with a tight recovery time objective?
  10. Describe a situation where you had to debug a production issue urgently alongside developers. What was your process?
  11. How do you detect and prevent configuration drift in your infrastructure?
  12. What is your experience with cloud providers? If you had to migrate a workload from one cloud to another, where would you start?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through a CI/CD pipeline you built or owned.

*Situation:* Our team was deploying a Node.js API to production manually, using shared credentials and no automated tests in the release flow. Deployments were slow and rollbacks were painful.

*Task:* I was asked to automate the release process and make it safe enough for the team to ship multiple times a day.

*Action:* I set up a GitHub Actions pipeline with three stages: lint and unit tests on every pull request, a Docker image build and push to a container registry on merge to main, and a rolling deployment to our cloud-managed container service. I added Slack notifications for failures and a one-click rollback step using the previous image tag. I also documented the pipeline so the team could maintain it without me.

*Result:* The team went from one weekly release to shipping multiple times a day. Rollbacks, when needed, completed in minutes rather than requiring a manual hotfix. The process became something developers trusted rather than feared.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you had to debug a production issue urgently alongside developers.

*Situation:* A payment service started timing out for a subset of users on a Friday evening. The on-call developer had no immediate visibility into what had changed since the morning.

*Task:* I needed to identify the root cause quickly and restore service, ideally without a full rollback.

*Action:* I pulled the recent deployment logs and checked our monitoring dashboards for anomalies around the incident start time. I found a spike in database connection pool exhaustion. I traced it to a new query introduced in that morning's release. I increased the connection pool limit as a short-term fix, then worked with the developer to add a query timeout and a missing index as a permanent solution.

*Result:* The service recovered quickly and users stopped seeing timeouts. We added connection pool metrics to our alerting setup so the same issue would page on-call the moment it started, not after users noticed.

---

Q: Describe your experience with Kubernetes and how you have handled cluster scaling.

*Situation:* We ran a data processing platform on a self-managed Kubernetes cluster. During peak hours, jobs queued up and latency spiked because we had fixed node counts and no autoscaling in place.

*Task:* I was responsible for making the cluster respond automatically to load without over-provisioning during off-peak hours.

*Action:* I configured the Cluster Autoscaler with separate node pools for CPU-heavy and memory-heavy workloads. I also set Horizontal Pod Autoscaler policies on our processing deployments based on custom Prometheus metrics rather than CPU alone, since our workload was queue-depth-driven. I validated the setup with a load test before enabling it in production.

*Result:* The cluster scaled up during peak periods and back down overnight. Candidates on the team reported infrastructure costs dropped noticeably, and job queue wait times fell. The team stopped getting late-night alerts about processing backlogs.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for experience and behavioural questions. Structure your answer as Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task brief (two or three sentences combined). Spend most of your time on Action, specifically what you did rather than what the team did. Always close with a concrete Result. If you do not have a metric, describe the clear before-and-after.

Structured walkthrough for technical design questions. Start by clarifying requirements and constraints before diving in. Then describe your approach layer by layer: compute, networking, data, monitoring, and failure handling. Call out trade-offs as you go. Interviewers typically want to see your thinking process, not just the final architecture on a whiteboard.

Five-whys framing for incident and debugging questions. Walk the interviewer through how you approached the problem: what you observed first, what you ruled out, and how you narrowed down the root cause. This shows systematic thinking, which is the core skill for any DevOps role.

Compare-and-decide structure for tool-choice questions. Name the options you considered, explain the trade-off (cost, complexity, team familiarity, ecosystem fit), and state what you picked and why. Avoid saying one tool is simply 'better'. Interviewers at product companies want engineers who make context-driven decisions, not ones who have a single favourite stack.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Hands-on depth, not surface familiarity. You should be able to explain not just what a tool does but how you used it, what went wrong, and how you fixed it. Saying 'I have used Kubernetes' is not enough. Saying 'I managed a cluster on a managed service, hit an issue with pod scheduling under memory pressure, and resolved it by adjusting resource requests and limits' tells the interviewer you have actually done the work.

Ownership and accountability. DevOps roles at product companies require engineers who treat reliability as their own problem. Interviewers typically listen for language like 'I set up' and 'I fixed' rather than 'the team handled it'. Talk about your specific contribution even when describing team projects.

Developer empathy. Aivari Innovations, like most product companies, wants DevOps engineers who make developers' lives easier. Expect questions or follow-up probes about how you have improved developer workflows, reduced toil, or made deployments safer and faster for the engineering team.

Security awareness. Secrets management, IAM policies, least-privilege access, and vulnerability scanning in CI pipelines are topics candidates report being tested on. You do not need to be a security specialist, but you should know the basics and be able to explain what goes wrong when they are ignored.

Communication under pressure. Incident response questions are partly a communication test. Can you explain a complex failure clearly to a non-technical stakeholder? Can you stay calm and methodical when things break in production? Practise telling incident stories in a clear, structured way.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week one: core technical revision.
Review the tools you use most: Docker, Kubernetes, your preferred CI/CD platform, and one cloud provider in depth. For each, write down one real project where you used it and one specific problem you solved. This gives you raw material for STAR answers during the interview.

Week two: system design practice.
Practise designing two or three common DevOps systems out loud. Good starting points: a CI/CD pipeline for a microservices app, a monitoring and alerting stack, and a multi-environment infrastructure setup using IaC. Use a whiteboard or text document, and practise talking through your decisions rather than just sketching diagrams.

Week three: behavioural preparation.
Map your past experiences to common themes: a time you improved a process, a difficult incident you resolved, a disagreement with a developer, and a time you automated something that saved the team hours. Write down the STAR structure for each story and practise delivering them in two to three minutes.

Before the interview.
Read the Aivari Innovations job description carefully. Note the specific tools and platforms they mention, and make sure you can speak to your experience with each one. Candidates report that interviewers often base technical questions directly on the job description.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Talking about tools instead of problems. Listing every tool in your stack without explaining what problem it solved is the most common mistake candidates make. Interviewers want to see judgment, not a resume recitation.

Being vague about your personal contribution. Saying 'we built a pipeline' does not tell the interviewer what you did. Always be specific about your role within team work, even when the outcome was a shared effort.

Skipping the result in STAR answers. Candidates often describe the action in detail and then trail off. Always close with what happened: the service recovered, the deployment time dropped, the team stopped getting false alerts. If you do not have a metric, describe the qualitative change clearly.

Treating security as someone else's job. If you say things like 'the security team handled secrets management', that signals a gap for interviewers at product companies. Show that you understand why security matters in infrastructure work and that you have taken practical steps to address it.

Not asking clarifying questions in design rounds. Jumping straight into an answer without asking about scale, constraints, or existing systems makes you look like someone who builds first and thinks second. A brief 'can I ask a few questions before I start' signals strong engineering instincts.

Underselling Linux and scripting skills. Many candidates focus on cloud and Kubernetes and forget to highlight solid Bash or Python scripting, Linux troubleshooting, and process management. These remain core DevOps skills and Aivari Innovations interviewers typically test for them.

DevOps interviews at most product companies do not include algorithmic coding rounds of the LeetCode type. Candidates report that any coding component is typically practical: writing a Bash or Python script to automate a task, debugging a broken Dockerfile, or fixing a Kubernetes manifest. How should I prepare for the system design part of the interview?

Practise designing complete systems out loud, not just sketching diagrams in your head. Common DevOps design questions include: design a CI/CD pipeline for a microservices application, design an observability and alerting stack, and design a zero-downtime deployment strategy. For each, cover the tools you would use, how you would handle failures, and what trade-offs you are making. Interviewers typically want to see structured thinking and awareness of real-world constraints rather than a textbook-perfect answer.
