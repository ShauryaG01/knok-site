02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up frequently in Hexaware BA interviews, based on candidate reports and the nature of Hexaware's client-facing delivery work.

  1. Walk me through how you gather requirements when stakeholders are not aligned with each other.
  2. How do you handle scope creep when a client starts asking for features that were not in the original plan?
  3. What BA tools and documentation have you used, for example JIRA, Confluence, BRD, or FRD?
  4. How do you prioritise requirements when the business wants more than the team can deliver in one sprint?
  5. Tell me about a process improvement you identified and the steps you took to roll it out.
  6. What is the difference between functional and non-functional requirements? Give a real example from your work.
  7. How do you ensure requirements are complete, unambiguous, and testable before handing them to the development team?
  8. Describe a time the technical team pushed back on a requirement and how you resolved it.
  9. How do you use data to validate a business assumption or spot a problem early?
  10. Walk me through how you handle a stakeholder who keeps changing their mind during a project.
  11. How do you write user stories and acceptance criteria that a development team can actually act on?
  12. A project is falling behind and the client is asking for daily updates. How do you manage that situation?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk me through how you gather requirements when stakeholders are not aligned.

*Situation:* At my previous company, two department heads had conflicting ideas about what a new customer portal should do. The sales team wanted self-service order placement, while the operations team wanted the focus on delivery tracking.

*Task:* My job was to capture requirements that served both teams without letting the disagreement stall the project.

*Action:* I set up a joint workshop where both stakeholders listed their priorities. I applied MoSCoW prioritisation to separate must-have features from nice-to-haves. I then mapped overlapping needs, like real-time order visibility, which both teams agreed on once we broke it down item by item. I circulated a draft BRD to both parties for sign-off before any development began.

*Result:* Both stakeholders signed off within a week. The portal launched with core features serving both departments, and we deferred lower-priority items to a later release, keeping the project on schedule.

---

Q: How do you handle scope creep in the middle of a project?

*Situation:* Partway through an IT integration project, the client started requesting new reporting dashboards that were not in the original scope.

*Task:* I had to manage the client's expectations while protecting the delivery timeline.

*Action:* I documented each new request formally through our change request process. I presented the client with a clear impact analysis showing how each addition would shift the timeline. I then worked with the project manager to separate requests that were genuinely critical from those that could go into a second phase.

*Result:* The client agreed to defer three of the five new requests. The two critical ones went through the formal change request process with an adjusted timeline. The original scope delivered on time, and the deferred items were picked up in the next release cycle.

---

Q: How do you use data to validate a business decision?

*Situation:* The operations team believed a particular customer segment was unprofitable and wanted to stop serving them entirely.

*Task:* I was asked to validate this assumption before the business made a major strategy change.

*Action:* I pulled transaction data from the CRM and finance system, segmented it by customer type, and built a simple profit and loss view per segment. I found that while the segment had lower average order values, their repeat purchase rate was among the highest. I presented the findings in a one-page summary with a recommendation to review pricing rather than exit the segment.

*Result:* Leadership revised their decision. Instead of exiting the segment, they adjusted the pricing model, and the segment's contribution margin improved noticeably within two quarters. The team told me the exercise changed how they approached similar decisions going forward.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions. Every 'tell me about a time' question follows Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task brief (two to three sentences combined) and spend most of your answer on Action and Result. Hexaware interviewers want concrete steps, not general statements about what a BA 'typically does'.

MoSCoW for prioritisation questions. When asked how you prioritise requirements, name MoSCoW (Must Have, Should Have, Could Have, Won't Have this release). Show you can apply it practically by referencing a real situation where it helped resolve a stakeholder disagreement.

Five Whys for root cause questions. For questions about process analysis or problem-solving, walk through how you ask 'why' repeatedly until you reach the actual root cause. This signals analytical depth without needing complex tools or jargon.

Impact before solution for case studies. When given a live business problem, first state the impact (who is affected, what is at risk), then propose a structured approach to investigate further before jumping to solutions. Interviewers want to see that you diagnose before you prescribe.

Communication ladder for stakeholder questions. Frame stakeholder answers across three levels: what you said, how you said it (format and channel), and what you did to confirm the message landed correctly. This shows you think about communication deliberately rather than reactively.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Hexaware BA interviewers are typically looking for five things, shaped by the company's client-facing, services-based delivery model.

Clear communication under pressure. BAs at Hexaware work between the client and the delivery team. Interviewers want to see that you can simplify complex requirements into plain language and stay steady when stakeholders are unclear or difficult to deal with.

Hands-on documentation skills. Expect to be asked about BRDs, FRDs, user stories, and acceptance criteria. Knowing what each document is matters less than being able to describe when and why you choose one format over another.

Structured thinking. Interviewers often give a vague business scenario to see how you break it down. They are not always looking for the 'right answer'. They want to see how you approach ambiguity and what questions you ask before reaching a conclusion.

Agile familiarity. Hexaware runs many projects in agile or hybrid delivery modes. Understanding sprint planning, backlog grooming, and the BA's role in a scrum team is expected at most levels of experience.

Client and stakeholder management experience. Because Hexaware is a services company, the BA often manages client expectations directly. Stories that show you have handled difficult stakeholders, changing requirements, or tight delivery windows carry more weight than purely internal project examples.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Build your story bank. List every significant project you have worked on. For each, prepare a STAR answer covering requirements gathering, a conflict you resolved, a process improvement, and a time something went wrong. Aim for six to eight solid, specific stories.

Week 2: Sharpen domain and tool knowledge. Review core BA concepts: types of requirements, traceability matrix, gap analysis, use cases versus user stories, and UAT sign-off. Also review any domain area Hexaware is active in that relates to your background, for example banking, healthcare, or logistics.

Week 3: Practice case studies out loud. Ask a friend or colleague to give you a vague business problem and work through your approach verbally. Focus on asking the right clarifying questions before proposing anything. Record yourself if possible to catch filler words or rushed answers.

Before the interview. Research Hexaware's recent projects and technology focus areas. Candidates report that interviewers respond well when you connect your experience to Hexaware's work in automation or digital transformation. Prepare two or three thoughtful questions to ask the interviewer about the team, the client engagement, and what success looks like in the first few months on the job.

Tools to review. JIRA, Confluence, Excel for data work, and basic SQL if the role involves any data analysis. You do not need expert-level depth, but you should be able to describe how you used each in a real project context.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Vague answers with no outcomes. Saying 'I improved the process and everyone was happy' is weak. Even a rough, observable result (the client approved the BRD on the first review, the delivery cycle shortened by a few days) makes your story credible. Close every STAR answer with something that changed.

Describing what a BA does instead of what you did. When asked 'how do you gather requirements', do not recite the theory. Describe a specific situation, the steps you personally took, and what happened as a result.

Ignoring the client-service angle. Hexaware is an IT services company. Candidates who only discuss internal team dynamics and not client management or delivery accountability miss what the role actually requires.

Asking no questions at the end. Not asking the interviewer anything signals low interest in the role. Prepare genuine questions about the project type, team structure, and what a successful BA looks like on their team.

Over-preparing for tools, under-preparing for scenarios. Knowing JIRA deeply is useful, but being able to walk through a real business problem is what separates candidates at interview stage. Balance both in your preparation.

Not translating your experience to a services context. If your background is in-house product or a bank's internal team, reframe your stories in terms of client, delivery, scope, and timelines. Hexaware interviewers think in those terms.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Hexaware BA interview typically have?

Candidates report that the process typically includes three to four rounds: a recruiter screening call, one or two domain or competency discussions with a hiring manager or senior BA, and a final HR round. Some roles include a case study as a separate step or as part of the domain round. The exact structure varies by seniority and team, so it is worth asking the recruiter to clarify the process upfront.

Does Hexaware give a case study in the BA interview?

Many candidates report receiving a short business scenario during the interview, either as a written case or a verbal problem posed by the interviewer. You are typically asked to structure your approach and walk through your thinking out loud rather than produce a finished document. The best preparation is to practice breaking down vague problems by asking clarifying questions before proposing any solution.

What salary can I expect for a BA role at Hexaware?

Hexaware does not publicly publish salary bands for BA roles. Platforms like Glassdoor and levels.fyi list publicly reported salary ranges for IT services BA positions in India, and these vary by city, experience level, and specialisation. Research current figures on those platforms before your HR discussion and come prepared with a range based on your experience and the market data you find.

Are there technical or coding questions in the Hexaware BA interview?

The Hexaware BA interview is not a coding round. Candidates report questions on SQL basics, Excel, and data interpretation if the role involves data work, but these are practical rather than algorithmic. The primary focus is on business analysis skills, stakeholder communication, and process thinking. Review any tools listed in the job description and be ready to describe how you used them in a real project.

How long does the Hexaware hiring process take from application to offer?

Candidates report the process from first contact to offer typically spans two to four weeks, though this can vary by team and how urgently the position needs to be filled. Following up with your recruiter after each round is a good practice. Applying through a referral or reaching out directly tends to get faster responses than submitting through a job portal without prior contact.

How can I stand out in a Hexaware BA interview?

The strongest candidates connect their past experience directly to Hexaware's work in IT services and digital transformation. Prepare specific STAR stories involving client-facing situations, cross-team coordination, and delivery under pressure. Showing familiarity with agile delivery and arriving with thoughtful questions about the team and the project type signals genuine interest and serious preparation.
