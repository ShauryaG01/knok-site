02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates interviewing for Software Engineer roles at product companies like Lucio commonly report questions across three themes: coding fundamentals, system design, and behavioural.

Coding and Data Structures

  1. Given a large stream of integers, return the top K most frequent elements in real time. Walk through your data structure choices.
  2. Design a rate limiter that works across multiple servers. What algorithm would you use, and why?
  3. You have a binary tree. Write a function to find the lowest common ancestor of two given nodes, without using parent pointers.
  4. Implement a thread-safe LRU cache. Explain where locks are necessary and where they can be avoided.
  5. Given a directed graph representing task dependencies, detect a cycle and return the cycle if one exists.

System Design

  1. Design a notification service that sends emails, SMS, and push notifications at scale. How do you handle failures and retries?
  2. You need to build a search-as-you-type feature for a large product catalogue. Walk through the architecture from the frontend to the database.
  3. How would you design a distributed job scheduler that guarantees at-least-once execution with idempotency?

Behavioural

  1. Tell me about a time you disagreed with a technical decision made by your team. What did you do?
  2. Describe a situation where you had to deliver a project with incomplete requirements. How did you handle ambiguity?
  3. Give an example of a production incident you owned end to end. What did you learn?
  4. How do you decide when to take on technical debt versus refactoring right away?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for every behavioural answer. Keep each answer to two to three minutes when spoken aloud.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you owned a production incident end to end.

*Situation:* Our payments service started throwing errors for a subset of users on a Friday evening. Transaction failure rates climbed and the on-call alert fired.

*Task:* I was the engineer on call. My responsibility was to identify the root cause, restore service, and communicate status to stakeholders.

*Action:* I checked recent deployments and spotted a config change pushed that day which set a connection pool limit too low. I rolled back the config, verified metrics normalised, wrote a postmortem, and added a test to catch similar misconfigurations before they reach production.

*Result:* Service was fully restored within twenty minutes of the alert firing. The postmortem was shared with the wider engineering team and the misconfiguration test was merged the following week.

---

Q: Describe a time you improved system performance under pressure.

*Situation:* Two weeks before a major product launch, load tests showed our search API response times degrading sharply under peak traffic.

*Task:* I was asked to investigate and fix the bottleneck without delaying the launch.

*Action:* I profiled the API and found a database query running without an index on a frequently filtered column. I added the index in a migration, tested it on a staging environment with production-scale data, and deployed it. I also added caching for the most common queries.

*Result:* Response times under peak load dropped substantially. The launch went ahead on schedule with no performance incidents.

---

Q: Give an example of handling ambiguous requirements.

*Situation:* A product manager asked me to 'make the dashboard faster' with no specific metrics or timeline.

*Task:* I needed to turn a vague request into a scoped, deliverable improvement.

*Action:* I set up user session recordings and spoke to a few internal users to understand which parts felt slow. I then proposed a concrete goal and broke the work into two phases: quick wins (lazy loading widgets) and a longer refactor of the data-fetching layer.

*Result:* The quick wins shipped within one sprint. Users reported the dashboard felt noticeably faster, and the product manager approved the second phase with a clear brief.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for Behavioural Questions

Every behavioural answer should follow the Situation, Task, Action, Result structure. Spend the least time on Situation and Task (set the context quickly), and the most time on Action. Be specific about what you personally did, not what 'we' did. Always end with a concrete Result.

Clarify Before You Code

For coding questions, spend the first two to three minutes asking clarifying questions: input constraints, edge cases, expected output format, and whether optimising for time or space matters more. Interviewers score you on problem decomposition, not just the final solution.

Think Aloud on System Design

Start system design answers by restating requirements and estimating rough scale (read/write ratio, data volume, latency needs). Then move through components: client, API layer, service logic, storage, and any async workers. Call out trade-offs explicitly. Interviewers want to see how you reason under ambiguity, not just whether you know the right answer.

The 'Why' Framework for Technical Decisions

When asked why you chose a particular technology or approach, structure your answer as: what the requirement was, what options you considered, why you chose this one, and what you would do differently with hindsight. This shows engineering maturity rather than just pattern-matching.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates report from Software Engineer interviews at product companies of similar profile:

Ownership and follow-through. Interviewers want to see that you take a problem from first principles to delivery, including handling the messy middle (blocked dependencies, shifting requirements, production fires). Candidates who say 'we did X' without explaining their personal contribution tend to score lower.

Clear communication under uncertainty. You will not always know the answer. Interviewers are watching how you think out loud, ask for clarification, and make reasonable assumptions rather than freezing or guessing silently.

Depth over breadth in technical knowledge. It is better to know one area very well (say, distributed systems or database internals) and be honest about gaps in others, than to give shallow answers across the board. Interviewers can work with depth; they cannot train for it.

Pragmatism over perfection. When asked to design a system, acknowledge trade-offs. A candidate who proposes a perfectly scalable but unrealistic architecture scores lower than one who starts simple, identifies bottlenecks, and scales incrementally.

Curiosity and a learning mindset. Mention times you learned from a mistake or a colleague. Companies building software in fast-moving spaces need engineers who update their mental models quickly.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Two to four weeks before the interview

  1. Revise core data structures: arrays, hash maps, trees, graphs, heaps, and tries. Focus on time and space complexity for every operation.
  2. Solve a solid set of medium-difficulty coding problems covering sliding window, binary search, dynamic programming, and graph traversal. Use a timed approach so you practise under pressure.
  3. Study two to three system design case studies relevant to Lucio's likely product space. Practise drawing diagrams and talking through trade-offs out loud.

One week before

  1. Do two to three mock interviews with a peer or using an online platform. Record yourself if possible and review how clearly you communicate.
  2. Prepare five to six STAR stories from your own experience covering: a production incident, a technical disagreement, a time you improved performance, a time you handled ambiguous requirements, and a time you mentored or were mentored.
  3. Research Lucio: check their engineering blog if one exists, look at LinkedIn for recent product announcements, and note any technologies mentioned in the job description.

Day before

  1. Do one light coding problem to stay sharp, not a marathon session. Confirm the interview format, time zone, and platform with your recruiter.
  2. Prepare two to three questions to ask your interviewer. Good ones: 'What does the on-call rotation look like for this team?' and 'What would success in this role look like after six months?'

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping straight into code. Candidates who start coding without clarifying the problem often solve the wrong question. Spend the first few minutes aligning with the interviewer on constraints and expected output.

Saying 'we' instead of 'I'. Behavioural questions are about your contribution. 'We built a pipeline' tells the interviewer nothing about you. Be specific about what you personally did.

Designing for infinite scale from the start. In system design, starting with a massively distributed architecture before establishing requirements signals poor judgement. Start simple, identify where it breaks, then scale the right components.

Not asking for help when stuck. Sitting in silence for several minutes helps no one. If you are stuck, say so, explain what you have tried, and ask whether you can get a small hint. Most interviewers see this as a positive signal.

Memorising answers instead of principles. Interviewers can tell when an answer is rehearsed word for word. Know the underlying principle so you can adapt when they change a detail of the scenario.

Forgetting to mention the outcome. Every STAR answer needs a Result. 'I fixed the bug' is incomplete. 'I fixed the bug and monitoring confirmed the error rate returned to baseline' is a complete answer.

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Lucio Software Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates typically report three to five rounds. These commonly include at least one coding round focused on data structures and algorithms, a system design round for mid-level and above candidates, and one or two behavioural rounds. The exact structure can vary by team and level, so confirm with your recruiter after the screening call.

Is there a take-home assignment in the Lucio interview process?

Some candidates report a short take-home coding task before or instead of a live coding round, though this is not universal. Typically, take-home tasks at product companies involve building a small feature or debugging a provided codebase. Ask your recruiter what to expect so you can prepare accordingly.

How should I prepare for the system design round?

Start by practising how to clarify requirements and estimate rough scale before drawing any architecture. Focus on common patterns like read-heavy vs write-heavy systems, caching strategies, and asynchronous processing with message queues. Interviewers at product companies want to see your reasoning process and how you call out trade-offs, not just a correct final design. Two to three weeks of deliberate practice with mock interviews makes a clear difference.

Keep the Situation and Task brief and spend most of your time on Action, being specific about what you personally did rather than what your team did. Always close with a concrete Result.
