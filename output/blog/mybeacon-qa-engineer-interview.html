03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time you caught a critical bug late in the release cycle.

*Situation:* We were days before a major product release at my previous company. The dev team had signed off on testing and we were in the final smoke-test phase.

*Task:* I was running an end-to-end check on the payment flow and noticed that certain discount codes were applying twice, effectively giving users double the discount.

*Action:* I immediately documented the issue with screenshots and reproduction steps, filed it as a P0 bug, and pulled the developer and product manager into a call. I also checked whether the issue existed in the previous release to confirm it was a regression and not pre-existing behaviour.

*Result:* The team patched the bug and pushed a hotfix the same day. The release went ahead with a short delay. The product manager noted it would have caused significant revenue loss if it had gone live undetected.

---

Q: How do you decide which test cases to automate?

*Situation:* At my previous job, we had a large backlog of manual test cases and a lengthy regression cycle. The team wanted to reduce the effort but had limited bandwidth for automation work.

*Task:* I was asked to define and lead the automation strategy for the QA team.

*Action:* I prioritised test cases based on three criteria: how often the flow ran in production, how critical it was to core business functions, and how stable the underlying UI or API was. High-frequency, stable, critical flows went into automation first. Complex UI edge cases with frequent layout changes stayed manual.

*Result:* We automated the highest-priority test cases and brought the regression cycle down meaningfully. The team could run a full regression check within each sprint rather than blocking releases for days.

---

Q: How do you handle a disagreement with a developer over whether something is a bug?

*Situation:* I had reported an issue where a form accepted negative values for a quantity field. The developer argued it was 'by design' since no specific validation was mentioned in the requirements.

*Task:* I needed to resolve the disagreement without creating conflict and without letting a genuine defect slip through.

*Action:* I went back to the product manager and pulled up the original user story, which described the field as accepting 'valid item quantities.' I framed my argument around user intent rather than making it personal, and showed how the behaviour could result in a broken order in the backend system.

*Result:* The product manager agreed it was a defect and validation was added before the release. After that, the team began writing explicit acceptance criteria around edge cases in every new user story.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For behavioural questions (anything starting with 'Tell me about a time' or 'Describe a situation'), use the STAR structure: Situation (brief context), Task (what you were responsible for), Action (what you specifically did, using 'I' not 'we'), and Result (a concrete outcome). Keep Situation and Task short and spend most of your time on Action and Result.

For technical questions about your process or approach, use a What-Why-How structure. Start with what you do, explain why that approach works, then walk through how you apply it step by step. For example, if asked about test prioritisation: 'I prioritise by risk and frequency. High-risk, frequently-used flows break the most users if they fail, so automation effort goes there first. My process is to map each test case to a business flow, rate its failure impact, and check how often it runs in a sprint.'

For conflict-related questions, candidates who stand out typically follow three steps: acknowledge the other person's point of view, bring in evidence such as requirements, user stories, or acceptance criteria, and escalate to a third party only when genuinely needed. Avoid framing these answers as 'I was right, they were wrong.' Interviewers are specifically watching for how you collaborate under pressure.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

QA interviews at product companies and mid-size tech firms like Mybeacon typically test for a mix of technical skill and professional maturity.

Attention to detail without being pedantic. Interviewers want to see that you catch real, impactful problems rather than logging cosmetic issues. Be ready to talk about how you triage and prioritise bugs by severity and business impact.

Practical automation experience. Knowing a framework is not enough. Candidates who can explain their test architecture decisions, such as why they chose Page Object Model or how they handle flaky tests, stand out from those who simply list tools on a resume.

Clear communication across teams. QA sits between engineering and product. Interviewers look for people who can write a clear, reproducible bug report, push back on requirements respectfully, and flag risks to timelines without creating panic.

Involvement from the start of the SDLC. Most modern teams expect QA Engineers to review requirements and ask questions before development begins. Show that you check user stories for testability and think about edge cases early in the sprint, not just at the end.

Ownership mindset. Candidates report that Mybeacon values people who treat quality as a shared team responsibility. Phrases like 'I collaborate with developers to write better acceptance criteria' land better than 'my job is to find what developers miss.'

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Strengthen your fundamentals.
Revisit core concepts such as the software testing life cycle, test case design techniques (boundary value analysis, equivalence partitioning, decision tables), and the differences between functional, regression, integration, and exploratory testing. Practise writing test plans for apps you use every day to build the habit of thinking in test scenarios.

Week 2: Sharpen your automation skills.
If Mybeacon's job description mentions a specific framework such as Selenium, Cypress, Playwright, Appium, or REST-Assured, do a few focused hands-on exercises with it. Revisit or build a small project using Page Object Model and connect it to a CI tool like GitHub Actions or Jenkins. Be ready to walk through your code and explain your design decisions in the interview.

Week 3: Practise your stories out loud.
Write out several work situations using the STAR format: a critical bug you found, a process improvement you drove, a conflict you resolved, a deadline you managed under pressure. Practise saying these aloud rather than just writing them, and aim for two to three minutes per story so you do not ramble or cut off too early.

Before the interview:
Research Mybeacon's product and note any publicly available information about their tech stack or engineering culture. Prepare two or three thoughtful questions for your interviewer, such as how QA integrates with development sprints or what the biggest quality challenges are for their current product. Asking good questions signals genuine interest and QA curiosity.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Listing tools instead of explaining your thinking. Saying 'I know Selenium, Jira, and Postman' does not differentiate you. Interviewers already expect tool familiarity. What they want to hear is how you decided when to use which tool and what trade-offs you made in past projects.

Overclaiming coverage. Saying 'I make sure every edge case is tested' sounds like overconfidence. Experienced interviewers know complete coverage is not realistic. Be honest about how you prioritise and what you consciously deprioritise under time pressure.

Using 'we' throughout your answers. Candidates often say 'we reduced the regression cycle' or 'we caught the bug.' Interviewers want to know your specific contribution. Say 'I identified the bottleneck' or 'I proposed the change' to make your impact clear.

Jumping to answers without clarifying the question. When given a scenario such as 'How would you test a login page?', diving straight in without asking about platform, user base, or constraints signals shallow thinking. A quick 'Can I ask a few questions first?' shows exactly the kind of instinct a good QA Engineer should have.

Ignoring non-functional testing. Many candidates focus entirely on functional testing. If you have any experience with performance, security, or accessibility testing, bring it up. It sets you apart from candidates who only test happy paths.

Being negative about past employers or teammates. If asked about a difficult project or colleague, stay factual and focus on what you learned. Venting about a former employer raises immediate red flags about how you will fit into a new team.
