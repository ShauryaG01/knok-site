02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Based on candidate reports and the nature of staffing-firm QA roles, these questions come up most often in Strategic Employment QA interviews:

  1. Walk me through your testing process from requirements review to sign-off.
  2. What types of testing have you worked on: functional, regression, smoke, UAT, performance?
  3. Which test management tools have you used (JIRA, TestRail, Zephyr, Quality Center)?
  4. How do you decide what to automate versus what to test manually?
  5. Describe a serious bug you found late in the release cycle. How did you handle the pressure?
  6. Have you written automation scripts? If yes, which frameworks: Selenium, Cypress, Playwright, or Appium?
  7. How do you approach API testing? Have you used Postman or REST Assured?
  8. What is your process for writing test cases from a vague or incomplete requirement?
  9. How do you prioritise test execution when the release window is tight?
  10. Describe a time you disagreed with a developer about whether something was a bug. What did you do?
  11. How comfortable are you working directly with a client to clarify acceptance criteria?
  12. Where do you see your QA career going over the next two to three years?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a serious bug you found late in the release cycle. How did you handle the pressure?

*Situation:* At my previous company, we were two days from go-live for a payment integration module. The dev team had already signed off on UAT.

*Task:* I was doing a final regression pass when I noticed that refund transactions above a certain amount were silently failing, with no error message shown to the user.

*Action:* I immediately documented the bug with exact reproduction steps, attached network logs, and marked it critical in JIRA. I contacted the dev lead directly rather than waiting for the daily stand-up. I also ran a quick impact check to confirm which user flows were affected, so the team could make an informed call on whether to delay or patch.

*Result:* The team pushed a targeted fix within a few hours. Go-live shifted by one day, but we shipped with zero payment-related defects in production. The product manager said catching it pre-launch saved a costly rollback.

---

Q: How do you decide what to automate versus what to keep as manual tests?

*Situation:* On a previous project, the team wanted to automate everything, but we had short sprints and limited automation bandwidth.

*Task:* I was asked to build an automation strategy that gave the most coverage for the effort invested.

*Action:* I mapped all test cases against three criteria: how often the test runs, how stable the feature is, and how complex the test data setup is. Regression tests that ran every sprint with a stable UI went into the automation suite first. Exploratory tests, edge cases with complex data, and UI flows that changed frequently stayed manual. I documented the reasoning so the team could revisit it each quarter.

*Result:* We reduced regression cycle time considerably without losing coverage on high-risk areas. The approach was adopted as the team standard for future projects.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you disagreed with a developer about whether something was a bug.

*Situation:* A developer marked one of my critical bugs as 'works as designed' without consulting me or the product owner.

*Task:* I needed to get the right people aligned quickly without creating friction that would slow the release.

*Action:* I went back to the original requirements document and found the acceptance criterion the behaviour violated. I put together a short write-up: the requirement, the expected behaviour, the actual behaviour, and a screen recording. I shared it with both the developer and the product owner in the same message so everyone had the same information at once.

*Result:* The product owner confirmed it was a bug. The developer fixed it without any conflict, and we kept a good working relationship for the rest of the project. The product owner also asked me to add the clarification to the requirements wiki as a reference for the team.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

The STAR method is your primary structure for any behavioural question. State the Situation briefly, clarify your Task specifically, describe your Action in concrete steps (focus on what *you* did, not the team), then close with a measurable or clearly visible Result. Keep the whole answer to two to three minutes.

For technical questions, use the CAR structure: Concept (explain what you understand), Application (how you have used it in practice), and Relevance (why it matters for the role). This keeps answers grounded and stops you from going too abstract or textbook-heavy.

For 'how would you approach' questions, think out loud using a three-step frame: clarify the inputs (requirements, constraints, scope), describe your process step by step, then explain how you would validate the output. Staffing firm interviewers want to see that you can ramp up quickly in a new client environment without hand-holding.

For salary questions, know your current CTC and your target range before the call. Current knok job radar data puts mid-level QA roles at 9-17 LPA across major cities. Come in with a specific number rather than a vague range, because staffing firms negotiate on your behalf with the client and need something concrete to work with.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Strategic Employment places QA professionals with multiple clients, so they evaluate you on two things at once: whether you are technically solid, and whether they can confidently present you to a client contact.

Technical soundness. They want to see you understand the full testing lifecycle, can write clear test cases, and have real hands-on experience with at least one automation framework and one test management tool. Being vague about tools listed on your own resume is the fastest way to lose credibility in the technical round.

Communication clarity. Because you may work directly with a client's product or development team, interviewers listen carefully for how clearly you explain technical issues. Practise describing a bug or a test plan the way you would to someone who is not a tester.

Ownership mindset. Candidates who say 'we found the bug' or 'our team built the suite' consistently lose points compared to those who say 'I found' and 'I designed.' Interviewers are not assessing your team. They are assessing you.

Adaptability. Staffing-firm QA roles often require fast ramp-up on a new client's domain. Show you have done this before: joined a project mid-cycle, picked up an unfamiliar codebase, or learned a new tool under time pressure.

Professionalism around defects. Good QA engineers treat bugs as information, not as accusations against developers. Interviewers notice if you speak about developers negatively. Show that you raise issues constructively and work toward resolution.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1, part 1: Strengthen your fundamentals. Review the STLC and SDLC end to end. Write out a clear definition and one real example for each: smoke testing, sanity testing, regression, integration testing, UAT, and exploratory testing. If you cannot explain each in two sentences without hesitation, spend extra time here before moving on.

Week 1, part 2: Tools audit. List every tool on your resume. For each one, prepare a specific example of how you used it and what outcome it produced. For automation frameworks, practise writing a basic test case structure in a shared doc or on paper, since some interviewers ask you to do this live during the technical round.

Week 2, part 1: Build your STAR stories. Write out four to five stories covering: a critical bug you caught, a process you improved, a conflict you resolved professionally, a tight deadline you managed, and a time you learned something new quickly. Staffing firm interviews often touch all of these. Practise saying each one out loud, not just writing them down.

Week 2, part 2: API basics. If you have not done API testing, spend a few hours with Postman running requests against any public API. Being able to say 'I have validated API responses using Postman' is much stronger than saying nothing at all.

Day before: Match your resume to the job description. Read the specific JD you applied to. Identify the tools and responsibilities listed and pair each to a story you have prepared. If the JD mentions Agile or Scrum, be ready to talk about how you work within sprints specifically.

Day of: Logistics check. Confirm the interview format. If it is online, test your connection and camera. Keep your resume open during the call. Have one genuine question ready about the client team or the type of projects you would work on.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Listing tools you cannot speak to in depth. Writing Selenium, JIRA, TestRail, and Postman on your resume and then struggling to give a concrete example for each is the most common reason candidates fail the technical round. Only list tools you can discuss with a real example ready.

Over-crediting the team. Saying 'we found the bug' or 'our team built the automation framework' removes you from your own story. Use 'I' when describing your specific contribution. The interviewer is evaluating your capability, not your team's.

Skipping the Result. Many candidates give a strong Situation and Action but trail off before stating the outcome. Always close the loop: what happened, what improved, what you learned. Even a small visible result is better than none.

Being vague about your testing process. Answers like 'I test to make sure things work' raise immediate red flags. Walk through your actual process: requirements review, test plan, test case design, execution, defect logging, and sign-off.

Speaking negatively about developers or previous employers. Staffing firms place you with their clients and will not risk putting someone who creates conflict into a client relationship. Keep all references to past colleagues constructive.

Not asking any questions. Candidates who ask nothing signal low interest or poor preparation. Have at least one genuine question ready about the role, the client environment, or what success looks like in the first few months.
