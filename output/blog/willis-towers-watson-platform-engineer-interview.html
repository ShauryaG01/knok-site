03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How do you design a CI/CD pipeline for a large enterprise application?

*Situation:* At my previous employer, our monolithic application was being split into microservices, but each team was running its own ad-hoc deployment scripts with no consistency across environments.

*Task:* I was asked to design a unified CI/CD platform that all product teams could adopt without losing their own flexibility.

*Action:* I built a centralised pipeline using reusable templates in GitLab CI, added automated security scanning and code quality gates, and set up environment-specific approval workflows for production deployments. I ran onboarding sessions so teams could migrate at their own pace.

*Result:* Teams reported faster release cycles and fewer rollback events within the first quarter. The pipeline became the default for all new services launched after that point.

---

Q: How would you handle a production outage affecting multiple teams using your shared platform?

*Situation:* Our Kubernetes ingress controller failed, taking down services used by several product teams at once.

*Task:* I was the on-call platform engineer responsible for restoring service and managing communication across all affected teams.

*Action:* I immediately posted a status update in the incident channel, began rolling back the ingress controller to the last stable version, and opened a parallel thread for root cause analysis. I kept all affected team leads updated at regular intervals until the issue was resolved.

*Result:* Service was restored quickly. We followed up with a blameless post-mortem and introduced a canary rollout policy for all shared platform components going forward.

---

Q: Describe a time you improved developer experience significantly.

*Situation:* Developers at my company were losing a meaningful portion of each sprint to local environment setup and slow builds.

*Task:* I was tasked with reducing this friction as part of a platform engineering initiative.

*Action:* I introduced a standardised dev container setup, integrated a remote build cache, and wrote documentation that walked teams through the new workflow. I ran a short onboarding session for all engineering squads.

*Result:* New joiner setup time dropped noticeably. Developers reported shorter build waits, and team satisfaction scores in our quarterly survey improved the following cycle.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural and scenario questions: Structure every story as Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep the Situation brief (one or two sentences). Spend the most time on the Action, describing exactly what you did. Make the Result observable, even if you do not have precise metrics.

Constraints-first for system design: When asked to design a platform or architecture, lead with your constraints (scale, compliance requirements, team size, existing tooling). Then describe your approach and walk through trade-offs. WTW values regulated-industry thinking, so weave in audit trails, access controls, and security posture as part of the design rather than adding them as afterthoughts.

The 'platform as a product' lens: Frame your platform work around the developer as an internal customer. Talk about reducing toil, measuring adoption, and treating the platform team as a service provider to other engineering squads. This framing resonates strongly with WTW interviewers, who care about impact across teams, not just technical output.

Handling gaps honestly: If a question covers a tool you have not used, say so clearly and bridge to something adjacent you have worked with. Candidates report that WTW interviewers appreciate intellectual honesty over bluffing.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates report, WTW Platform Engineer interviewers look for the following signals:

Enterprise-grade reliability thinking. Can you build platforms that hold up under regulated-industry scrutiny? Security, compliance, and audit trails should feel natural to your answers, not like extra requirements bolted on at the end.

Shared ownership mindset. Platform engineers at WTW serve other engineering teams as internal customers. Interviewers want to see that you think about reducing toil for developers and improving shared tooling, not just solving problems for your own squad.

Hands-on cloud-native depth. Kubernetes, Terraform, CI/CD pipelines, observability stacks. You should be able to go deep on at least two of these and speak to real trade-offs you have encountered in production environments.

Communication under pressure. Incident response questions are common. Interviewers want to see structured thinking and proactive stakeholder communication, especially when multiple teams are affected.

Business awareness. WTW is not a pure-play tech company. Candidates who understand that their platform work supports insurance broking and risk advisory products, and who connect technical decisions to business outcomes, tend to stand out in the final rounds.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Build technical depth
Review Kubernetes, Terraform, and your primary CI/CD tool carefully. Be ready to explain architecture decisions and trade-offs, not just commands. Practice drawing infrastructure diagrams and talking through them out loud as if explaining to a panel.

Week 2: Add business context
Read about WTW's core areas: insurance broking, risk advisory, and human capital management. Understand why compliance and data security are non-negotiable in this context. Adjust at least one of your STAR stories to reflect a regulated or high-stakes environment where audit and access control mattered.

Week 3: Sharpen your stories
Prepare STAR answers for at least these three scenarios: a platform or infrastructure migration, a production incident you managed, and a developer experience improvement. Practise each story until it fits cleanly within a few minutes without rambling or losing the result.

Before each round: Re-read the job description. Note any specific tools or platforms mentioned and prepare to discuss your hands-on experience with them. Prepare a few thoughtful questions about the team's current platform stack and what the roadmap looks like for the next year.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

  1. Using generic DevOps language without enterprise context. Saying 'I set up a pipeline' without mentioning compliance approvals or auditability misses what WTW specifically cares about. Always connect your technical choices to the regulated environment.
  1. Ignoring the platform-as-a-product mindset. Candidates who talk only about infrastructure without mentioning internal developer experience leave points on the table. WTW wants platform engineers who think like product managers for their internal users.
  1. Skipping observability in system design answers. Any platform design that does not address logging, metrics, and alerting reads as incomplete in a WTW context. Build it into your design from the start.
  1. Not asking clarifying questions in design rounds. Jumping to a solution without scoping constraints signals that you do not think carefully about real-world complexity. Pause, ask questions, and establish scope before answering.
  1. Underestimating the HR and culture discussion. WTW places genuine weight on collaboration and communication skills. The final round is not a formality, and candidates who treat it as one often lose offers they otherwise deserved.
  1. Vague results in STAR answers. 'Things improved' is not a result. Even without hard numbers, describe what changed in observable terms: faster deployments, fewer incidents, positive feedback from dependent teams.
