01 Overview

Overview

Razorpay is one of India's largest fintech companies, powering payment infrastructure for millions of merchants ranging from kirana stores to large enterprises.   1. Razorpay's payment infrastructure must stay available around the clock. Tell me about a system you designed or managed for very high availability.
  1. How have you made a build-versus-buy decision for a critical infrastructure component? Walk me through your reasoning and the trade-offs you weighed.
  1. Imagine Razorpay's payment gateway goes down during peak merchant traffic. How do you run the incident response as the Engineering Manager on call?
  1. Razorpay serves both large enterprise clients and small merchants. How do you help your team prioritize competing demands across these very different customer segments?
  1. Tell me about a time you helped an engineer grow from one level to the next. What specifically did you do, and what was the outcome?
  1. How do you manage technical debt in a fast-moving fintech company where shipping speed also matters?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a time you managed a major production incident that affected end customers.

*Situation:* A critical payment processing service at my previous company began throwing timeout errors during peak weekend traffic, directly affecting merchant settlements.

*Task:* As the Engineering Manager, I needed to coordinate the response, keep stakeholders clearly informed, and help my team find and fix the root cause without making things worse under pressure.

*Action:* I immediately set up a virtual war room and assigned a dedicated incident commander from my team so I could focus entirely on stakeholder communication. I sent rolling status updates to business leadership at regular intervals throughout the outage. The team traced the issue to database connection pool exhaustion caused by a recent deployment. We rolled back the change and added monitoring alerts to catch this pattern earlier in future deployments.

*Result:* Service was restored within a few hours. The post-mortem identified three systemic gaps in our alerting setup and we closed all three within the next sprint. The transparency during the incident built lasting trust with the business side. The main thing I would do differently is establish a pre-agreed stakeholder communication template so we lose less time formatting updates during a crisis.

---

Q: How do you manage technical debt while keeping up with delivery commitments?

*Situation:* At my previous company, a core payments module had significant legacy code that slowed down every new feature we tried to add. Product was pushing for new features each sprint with no slack in the schedule.

*Task:* I needed to make a credible business case for investing in debt reduction without halting feature delivery or burning out the team.

*Action:* I quantified the debt in terms the business could understand: developer-hours spent navigating old code per sprint, and the projected delay impact on upcoming features in the roadmap. I proposed a structured split between feature work and tech debt, presented the case clearly, and got buy-in from the product leader. I then reorganised the team so one sub-group led debt work while another kept feature delivery moving in parallel.

*Result:* Over two quarters, our average feature cycle time dropped noticeably and the team reported less fatigue from context-switching. The business saw the payoff and we have maintained a structured debt budget in every planning cycle since. What I learned is that debt conversations need to be framed in business impact language from the start, not engineering language.

---

Q: Describe a time you had to manage an underperforming engineer.

*Situation:* One of my senior engineers was consistently missing deadlines and showing visible disengagement. The rest of the team had started to notice and it was affecting morale.

*Task:* I had to address this directly, treat the engineer with respect, and give a genuine chance to recover before considering further action.

*Action:* I started with a private, honest conversation to understand what was actually going on. The engineer was dealing with personal difficulties and also felt unchallenged by the assigned work. Together we put together a structured plan: clearer short-term goals, a project that matched their strengths, and regular weekly check-ins. I also set explicit expectations about what improvement needed to look like and by when.

*Result:* Over three months, the engineer regained momentum and delivered a key component on schedule. The experience reinforced to me that underperformance usually has a root cause worth investigating carefully before moving to a formal process. I now do short 'energy check-ins' with every direct report monthly so these situations surface earlier.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For behavioral questions, STAR is your structure. Open with the Situation and Task in two or three sentences so the interviewer understands the stakes. Spend the most time on Action: what YOU specifically decided and did, not what the team did collectively. Close with a concrete Result, then add one sentence on what you learned or would do differently. Razorpay interviewers specifically probe for that reflection step.

For system design questions, start with constraints. Before proposing anything, ask about scale, uptime requirements, data sensitivity, and regulatory considerations. Razorpay interviewers typically want you to reason through trade-offs out loud rather than jump to a single answer. Make your assumptions explicit and revisit them openly if the interviewer adjusts the scope mid-discussion.

For people and process questions, lead with outcomes. State the outcome you were trying to achieve, then describe your specific approach. Avoid abstract management principles. Concrete examples with specific results land better than general philosophy, and Razorpay interviewers will probe until they get specifics anyway, so start there.

For disagreement or conflict questions, show backbone and judgment. Demonstrate that you can hold a position backed by data, engage respectfully with the other view, and either update your thinking with new information or escalate cleanly when consensus is not possible. Razorpay values directness and first-principles thinking over harmony for its own sake.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Candidates who have interviewed at Razorpay typically report that interviewers look for a consistent set of signals across rounds.

Deep technical credibility. You do not need to write code in the interview, but you should be able to go deep on distributed systems, reliability engineering, and API design. Razorpay's products handle real money, and interviewers want confidence that you can spot risks your team might miss and hold credible conversations with staff engineers on your team.

Genuine ownership mindset. Generic answers about 'working with the team' are not enough. Interviewers want to hear what YOU decided, what YOU escalated, and what you would have done differently. The company has a strong first-principles culture and rewards directness and candour over polished storytelling.

Structured, clear communication. Fintech is complex and interviewers value managers who can translate technical detail for business stakeholders and business priorities for engineers. How clearly you organise your answers in the interview itself is direct evidence of this skill.

People focus with honest edges. Razorpay wants managers who genuinely invest in their people but are also willing to have hard conversations about performance and expectations. Overly diplomatic answers that avoid all mention of conflict, failure, or difficult decisions tend to score lower.

Regulatory and compliance awareness. Razorpay operates under RBI guidelines and handles sensitive financial data. Showing that you factor compliance, security, and data handling into engineering decisions gives you a meaningful edge over candidates who treat these concerns as someone else's problem.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know the product and the company.
Use Razorpay's payment gateway, explore the merchant dashboard if you can get access, and read their public engineering blog. Understand their core lines: payment gateway, payment links, payroll, and banking products. Be ready to reference at least one specific technical challenge or product decision in your conversations.

Week 2: Build your story bank.
List eight to ten situations from your career covering: incident management, a technical architecture decision, growing an engineer, handling underperformance, cross-functional collaboration, and a real disagreement you navigated. Structure each one in STAR format before you start interviews so you are not constructing stories under pressure.

Week 3: Practice out loud.
Record yourself answering three behavioral questions. Check that your answers are specific, that you say 'I' not 'we' when describing your own decisions, and that you land on a concrete result with a learning. Run one mock system design discussion focused on payment flows or high-throughput, low-latency systems.

Week 4: Research, calibrate, and prep your questions.
Read Glassdoor and levels.fyi reviews for Razorpay Engineering Manager interviews to calibrate the depth and tone interviewers expect. Prepare four or five thoughtful questions to ask your interviewers, focused on team structure, technical roadmap, and how EM success is actually measured at Razorpay.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Staying too abstract. The most common feedback candidates share is that Razorpay interviewers pushed back hard when answers stayed at the level of process or framework. Ground every answer in a real, specific situation with genuine context and outcomes.

Saying 'we' when you mean 'I'. Interviewers are evaluating you specifically. Even in collaborative work, be clear about what you personally decided, recommended, or drove. Teams do work together, but your individual judgment is what is being assessed.

Underestimating technical depth. Some candidates assume an EM role means lighter technical scrutiny. Razorpay typically expects you to speak credibly about system design, reliability, and engineering trade-offs. Candidates who say 'I leave the technical details to my team' often do not progress past early rounds.

Not knowing Razorpay's products. Generic fintech answers read as a lack of preparation. At least one or two of your examples or questions should reference something specific to how Razorpay operates or a challenge in their domain.

Skipping the learning close. Razorpay values a growth mindset. Ending a STAR story with only the result and no reflection on what you learned or would do differently misses an opportunity to show maturity and self-awareness, both qualities they look for at the EM level.

Asking weak questions at the end. Candidates who ask only about salary or ask nothing at all leave a noticeably weaker impression. Prepare genuine questions about engineering challenges, roadmap priorities, or how the team defines and measures impact.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Razorpay Engineering Manager interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically runs across four to six rounds. These commonly include an initial recruiter screen, one or two technical or system design discussions, and multiple behavioral rounds focused on leadership and people management. Some candidates also report a final conversation with a senior leader or a panel before the offer stage, though round structure varies by team.

What salary can I expect as an Engineering Manager at Razorpay?

Based on knok's job radar data, Manager-level roles sit in the 35-60 LPA range, Senior Manager roles in the 55-90 LPA range, and Director-level positions at 90-150+ LPA. For self-reported offer numbers from actual Razorpay employees, check Glassdoor and levels.fyi, which track compensation in more granular detail and reflect recent offers more closely than any single aggregated source.

Does the Razorpay EM interview include a coding round?

Candidates typically report that traditional coding rounds are not a standard part of the Razorpay EM interview. However, expect deep technical discussions on system design, reliability, and architecture trade-offs. The technical bar is high enough that you need to be comfortable going into real depth on engineering decisions and constraints even without writing any code.

How long does the full Razorpay EM interview process take from application to offer?

Candidates report the process typically takes a few weeks from initial recruiter contact to offer, though this varies by team urgency and role level. Applying through an internal referral generally accelerates the timeline noticeably. Following up with the recruiter after each completed round is well-received and helps prevent your process from stalling between stages.

What questions should I ask the interviewer at the end of the rounds?

Strong questions are specific and show genuine curiosity: ask about the team's biggest technical challenge over the next six months, how engineering roadmap decisions are made, or what a successful first ninety days looks like for this role. Avoid generic questions that can be answered by reading the company website. Thoughtful questions also give you real signal on whether Razorpay is the right environment for your own goals.

Is it realistic to apply for an EM role at Razorpay as a senior engineer moving into management for the first time?

Razorpay does hire first-time managers, but candidates report the bar is set for people who have some form of leadership experience, even if informal. If you have led a project team, mentored junior engineers, or functioned as a tech lead with cross-team responsibility, frame that experience clearly in your resume and in every behavioral answer. With 42 Engineering Manager roles open, there is active hiring across levels, so it is worth applying if you have real leadership experience to discuss.
