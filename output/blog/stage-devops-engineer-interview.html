01 Overview

Overview

Stage currently has 159 open DevOps Engineer roles, making it one of the more active tech hirers in the market right now. Across all companies, knok jobradar tracked 811 active DevOps Engineer openings as of July 2026, with Bangalore leading at 187 roles.

Stage's interview process for DevOps roles typically includes a screening conversation, one or more technical rounds covering infrastructure, CI/CD, and cloud platforms, and a final discussion with a hiring manager. Candidates report that the process is collaborative rather than combative, but expectations are high for hands-on, end-to-end ownership experience.

If you are preparing for a Stage DevOps role, expect questions that go beyond tool knowledge. Interviewers want to see how you think about reliability, developer experience, and owning systems through their full lifecycle.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

  1. Walk us through a CI/CD pipeline you built from scratch. What tools did you choose and why?
  1. How do you handle secrets management in a Kubernetes environment? What are the risks of doing it wrong?
  1. Describe a production incident you owned. How did you detect it, respond, and prevent recurrence?
  1. What is your approach to infrastructure as code? How do you manage Terraform state and team collaboration on shared modules?
  1. How would you design a blue-green or canary deployment strategy for a high-traffic microservice?
  1. A developer says deployments are too slow. How do you diagnose and fix this?
  1. How do you monitor and alert for a distributed system? What is your philosophy on alert fatigue?
  1. Explain how you would secure a Kubernetes cluster. Walk us through your approach.
  1. We are moving a legacy monolith to containers. What are the first three things you would do?
  1. How do you manage infrastructure drift? What tools or processes do you rely on?
  1. Tell us about a time you had to convince developers to adopt a new DevOps practice. What was the resistance and how did you handle it?
  1. How do you approach cost optimisation on cloud infrastructure without sacrificing reliability?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a production incident you owned.

*Situation:* At my previous company, our payment service went down late at night, affecting all checkout flows.

*Task:* I was on-call and had to identify the cause, restore service, and keep the business team informed throughout.

*Action:* I checked our Grafana dashboards first and saw database connection pool exhaustion. I traced this to a recent deployment with a misconfigured connection limit. I rolled back using our Helm chart, verified metrics returned to baseline, and posted real-time status updates in our team Slack channel.

*Result:* Service was restored well within our SLA. In the post-mortem I added a connection pool metric to our pre-deployment checklist and set up a dedicated alert for this pattern. We have not seen a repeat since.

---

Q: Walk us through a CI/CD pipeline you built from scratch.

*Situation:* Our team was doing manual deployments using SSH and bash scripts. The process was slow, error-prone, and only a couple of people understood it.

*Task:* I was asked to build a proper CI/CD pipeline that any engineer could use safely.

*Action:* I set up GitHub Actions for CI, with stages for linting, unit tests, integration tests, and Docker image builds. For CD, I used ArgoCD with a GitOps model so merging to main automatically synced to staging, and a manual approval gate pushed to production. I wrote Terraform modules for the underlying infrastructure so everything was versioned and reviewable.

*Result:* Deployment time dropped significantly and the team could release without involving me. We started catching regressions earlier because every pull request ran the full test suite.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you convinced developers to adopt a new DevOps practice.

*Situation:* Developers on my team were resistant to writing Dockerfiles and owning their container configs. They saw it as 'ops work.'

*Task:* I needed to move to a model where each team owned their service's container definition, as we were scaling fast and I could not be a bottleneck.

*Action:* Instead of mandating the change, I created base images and a template Dockerfile for each tech stack we used. I ran a short internal demo showing how the template was faster than waiting for me. I also paired with a few developers on their first pull requests to make the process feel safe.

*Result:* Within a few weeks, all services had developer-owned Dockerfiles. On-call handoffs became easier because developers understood their own containers, and this became standard practice in our team onboarding.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For system design questions: Start with requirements (scale, SLAs, team size) before jumping to tools. Interviewers want to see that you ask 'what problem are we solving' before picking Kubernetes or Terraform.

For 'tell me about a time' questions: Use STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result). Keep the Situation brief, spend most time on Action (what you specifically did), and always end with a measurable or observable Result.

For tool-specific questions: Lead with the problem the tool solves, not just its name. 'We used Vault because we had secrets hardcoded in environment variables and needed rotation' is stronger than 'I have experience with Vault.'

For debugging or incident questions: Show your methodology: observe (metrics/logs), hypothesise, test, fix, verify, then prevent recurrence. Interviewers are checking whether you are systematic under pressure, not just lucky.

For 'how would you' hypotheticals: Think out loud. State your assumptions, propose a first approach, then mention the trade-offs you would weigh. You are being evaluated on reasoning, not just the final answer.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Stage interviewers, like most product-company DevOps panels, are looking for engineers who treat reliability as a product feature, not a background task.

Ownership mindset: Can you point to systems you built and maintained end-to-end? Candidates who describe the full lifecycle (including incidents and improvements) come across far stronger than those who handed things off after the initial setup.

Developer empathy: DevOps at a product company means your internal customers are developers. Interviewers want to see that you have thought about developer experience, deployment confidence, and reducing toil for engineering teams.

First-principles thinking: You will be asked to design something or debug a hypothetical. Interviewers are not impressed by tool name-dropping. They want to see you reason from constraints to a solution.

Communication under pressure: Incident and on-call questions are partly a communication test. Can you write a clear status update? Can you explain a technical failure to a non-technical stakeholder? Practise this explicitly before your interview.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Core technical revision
Revise Kubernetes internals (scheduling, RBAC, networking), Terraform state management, and at least one major cloud provider (AWS or GCP). Review your past projects and prepare concrete examples for each topic area.

Week 2: System design and hands-on practice
Practise designing CI/CD pipelines and cloud architectures out loud, as if explaining to an interviewer. If your work projects are under NDA, set up a small personal project on GitHub to demonstrate current skills.

Week 3: Behavioural prep and mock interviews
Write STAR answers for several scenarios: an incident you owned, a process you improved, a disagreement with a developer, a cost-saving initiative, and a time you mentored someone. Do at least one mock interview with a peer or mentor.

Ongoing: Follow Stage's engineering blog or LinkedIn page if they publish technical content. Candidates report that showing awareness of the company's scale and product context makes a noticeable difference in interviews.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Listing tools instead of problems solved: Saying 'I know Jenkins, GitLab CI, ArgoCD, Terraform, Ansible' without context tells the interviewer nothing. Always anchor tools to the problem they solved and the outcome they produced.

Skipping the Result in STAR answers: Many candidates describe the Situation and Action in detail but forget to say what happened. Describe the outcome clearly, whether it was a restored service, a faster pipeline, or a more confident engineering team.

Being vague about incidents: Interviewers probe incidents deeply. If you say 'I handled an outage,' be ready to explain the root cause, your exact steps, the timeline, and what you changed afterward. Vague answers suggest you did not actually own the incident.

Over-engineering system design answers: Candidates often design for far beyond the stated scale without checking assumptions first. State your assumptions, then design to match the requirements. A focused design is better than an unnecessarily complex one.

Not asking clarifying questions: Jumping straight into an answer without asking about constraints, team size, or existing stack is a missed signal. Interviewers expect and appreciate clarifying questions before you dive in.
