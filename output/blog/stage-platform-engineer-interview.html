01 Overview

Overview

Stage is a tech company with 159 open roles listed as of mid-2026, making it one of the more active hirers in India right now. Platform Engineers at Stage typically own the internal developer platform, CI/CD pipelines, cloud infrastructure automation, and the reliability tooling that product squads rely on every day.

Candidates report the interview process typically covers system design, hands-on infrastructure knowledge, scripting ability, and how you work across teams. This guide walks you through the questions most commonly asked, how to structure strong answers, and a preparation plan for the weeks ahead.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

System design and infrastructure

  1. Walk us through how you would design a self-service developer platform from scratch for a fast-growing product company.
  2. How would you approach capacity planning for a new microservice being onboarded to the platform?
  3. Stage deploys frequently across teams. How would you design a CI/CD pipeline that balances speed with safety?
  4. Describe the trade-offs between running a self-managed Kubernetes cluster versus a managed service like EKS or GKE.

Hands-on platform work

  1. Tell us about a production Infrastructure as Code setup you have owned. What tool did you use and what problems did it solve?
  2. How do you handle secrets management and access control in a cloud environment used by multiple teams?
  3. Describe a migration you have led, for example moving workloads to a new cloud provider or upgrading a container runtime.
  4. How would you improve observability when logs, metrics, and traces are fragmented across different tools?

Reliability and incidents

  1. How do you handle on-call for platform-level outages? Walk us through your incident response process step by step.
  2. How do you define and track platform SLOs? What would you measure to confirm the platform is healthy?

Collaboration and influence

  1. Stage's product teams move fast. How do you balance developer velocity with platform stability?
  2. Tell us about a time you had to push back on a product team's infrastructure request and how you managed it.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How have you improved CI/CD pipeline reliability at a previous employer?

*Situation:* At my previous company, the CI/CD pipeline was a shared Jenkins setup used by multiple squads. Flaky tests and long queue times were causing delayed releases several times a week.

*Task:* I was asked to diagnose the instability and propose a fix that worked for all teams without forcing them to change their existing workflows.

*Action:* I instrumented the pipeline to capture failure rates per stage and found that a large share of failures came from a shared test database being hit concurrently by multiple jobs. I introduced per-job isolated test containers using Docker Compose, moved flaky integration tests to a nightly run, and migrated the team to GitHub Actions with self-hosted runners to remove queue contention.

*Result:* Similar changes are commonly cited to cut average pipeline wait times significantly and bring 'pipeline broken' incidents close to zero over the following quarter. My team told me developers were shipping with noticeably more confidence.

---

Q: Describe a production Infrastructure as Code setup you have owned.

*Situation:* At my previous role, cloud resources were managed through a mix of manual console changes and partially documented scripts. Provisioning a new environment took several days and was prone to configuration drift.

*Task:* I was responsible for migrating our infrastructure to a fully reproducible IaC setup.

*Action:* I chose Terraform, imported existing resources into state, and wrote reusable modules for VPCs, IAM roles, and EKS clusters. I set up a GitOps review process so all infrastructure changes went through pull requests with a mandatory plan review step. I also added automated drift detection via a scheduled Terraform plan run in CI.

*Result:* New environment provisioning dropped from several days to under an hour. The team could see exactly what changed and why, which made compliance reviews straightforward and reduced configuration surprises during incidents.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you had to push back on a product team's infrastructure request.

*Situation:* A product team requested direct production database access for a new analytics pipeline, citing urgency around a business deadline.

*Task:* My job was to support their timeline while making sure we did not create a security and reliability risk for the production system.

*Action:* I scheduled a call with their tech lead, explained the risk of ad-hoc queries hitting a live OLTP database, and proposed a read replica with automated data export to a data warehouse they could query freely. I walked them through why this approach would actually be faster for their analytical workload.

*Result:* They adopted the read replica approach. The analytics pipeline launched on their original deadline and the production database saw no additional load.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions

Most behavioural questions at Stage-type interviews want a real story, not a hypothetical. Use the STAR structure: Situation (one or two sentences setting context), Task (what you were specifically responsible for), Action (what you personally did, using 'I' not 'we'), Result (what changed as a consequence). The Action and Result sections should take up most of your answer.

CAR for system design

For infrastructure and design questions, use Context, Approach, Reasoning. Open by clarifying constraints (scale, team size, cost sensitivity, existing stack). Walk through your design step by step. Close with the trade-offs you consciously made and why you chose one path over another.

The 'so what' close

Many candidates describe what they did but forget to say what changed as a result. Every answer should end with an outcome the interviewer can evaluate. If you have no hard metric, a qualitative result works: 'engineers stopped filing tickets about broken deploys' is a real outcome.

Structuring pushback answers

Stage values engineers who collaborate under pressure. For questions about disagreement or pushback, show that you listened first, proposed an alternative rather than a flat refusal, and kept the other team's goal in mind. The structure: understand their need, explain the risk clearly, offer a path forward.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Platform engineering interviews at product companies like Stage typically probe three things.

Ownership mindset. Interviewers want to see that you treat the platform as a product, with developer experience as a key metric alongside uptime. Candidates who talk only about tools without mentioning the teams who use them tend to score lower.

Depth plus breadth. Platform Engineers sit at the intersection of networking, security, containers, CI/CD, and cloud. You do not need to be an expert in every area, but you should speak confidently about at least two or three and know enough about the others to collaborate effectively.

Clear communication under pressure. Because platform teams serve other engineers, interviewers pay close attention to how you explain trade-offs. Practise explaining a complex infrastructure decision as if you were talking to a developer who does not have your background.

Bias toward action. Companies at Stage's growth stage typically want engineers who move forward under uncertainty rather than waiting for perfect information. Show that you have made judgment calls with incomplete data and improved from there.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Two weeks before the interview

Review the core areas Platform Engineers are tested on: Kubernetes, Terraform or Pulumi, CI/CD pipelines (GitHub Actions, Jenkins, or GitLab CI), observability stacks (Prometheus, Grafana, OpenTelemetry), and at least one major cloud platform. For each area, have a story from your own experience ready to tell in detail.

One week before

Prepare STAR answers for at least five behavioural questions: a project you led end-to-end, a time you improved reliability, a conflict with another team, a failure and what you learned from it, and a time you drove a technical decision. Write these out and practise saying them aloud.

For system design, practise talking through a CI/CD redesign and an internal developer platform design. Aim to cover requirements, high-level design, and trade-offs in a focused block so you stay structured under interview conditions.

Two to three days before

Look up what Stage builds and who their customers are. Candidates report that interviewers appreciate when you connect your answers to the company's context, for example mentioning how your approach would fit their scale or deployment model. Prepare two or three questions to ask the interviewer: how do you measure platform team success here, what does the on-call rotation look like, and how do product teams raise platform requests are all solid starting points.

Day before

Run through your STAR answers one final time. Get your coding environment ready in case a hands-on exercise comes up. Rest well before the interview day.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Talking about the team instead of yourself. Interviewers want to know what you did. Answers that say 'we migrated to Kubernetes' without explaining your specific contribution are hard to evaluate. Use 'I' when describing your actions, even within a team effort.

Skipping the reasoning. Saying you used Terraform is fine. Saying you chose Terraform over Pulumi because your team had existing HCL expertise and you needed to minimize ramp-up time is much stronger. Platform Engineering interviewers expect reasoning, not just tool name-dropping.

Over-engineering the design answer. A common mistake is proposing a system far more complex than the problem requires. Start with the simplest approach that works, state your assumptions, and layer in complexity only when the interviewer pushes you. Knowing when NOT to add a component is a positive signal.

No outcome in behavioural answers. Describing a situation without a result makes it hard for interviewers to judge impact. A qualitative outcome like 'the deployment process became self-service for all squads' is far better than trailing off after describing your actions.

Not asking questions. Platform Engineering is a deeply collaborative role. Candidates who ask nothing at the end signal low curiosity about the team and the problem space. Prepare at least two specific questions before you go in.

Ignoring developer experience. Platform Engineers who focus only on infrastructure reliability without mentioning how their work helped the developers using it often miss a key signal that product-company interviewers look for.

How many rounds does the Stage Platform Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically includes a recruiter screening, a technical round covering infrastructure and system design, and one or more rounds focused on past experience and cross-team collaboration. The exact structure varies by team and role level. It is worth asking your recruiter what to expect when you receive the invite.

Does Stage ask coding questions in the Platform Engineer interview?

Candidates report that Platform Engineer interviews at companies like Stage tend to focus more on system design, infrastructure knowledge, and behavioural questions than on algorithmic coding. You may be asked to write or review scripts, Terraform configurations, or Kubernetes manifests, so hands-on practice with these is worthwhile. Confirm the exact format with your recruiter before the round.

What cloud platform experience does Stage look for?

Familiarity with at least one major cloud provider (AWS, GCP, or Azure) is typically expected, based on what candidates at similar companies report. Depth in Kubernetes, CI/CD tooling, and Infrastructure as Code tends to matter more than the specific cloud vendor. Check the job description for any explicit requirements before your interview.
