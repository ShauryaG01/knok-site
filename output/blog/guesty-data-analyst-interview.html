02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions reflect topics that candidates have reported encountering in Guesty Data Analyst interviews. The exact mix varies by interviewer and round.

  1. Walk us through a project where you turned raw data into a business decision. What was your process from data pull to final recommendation?
  2. Write a SQL query to find the top 5 properties by total booking revenue for a chosen date range, handling ties correctly.
  3. How would you design a dashboard for a property manager to track occupancy rate, average daily rate, and cancellation trends?
  4. Guesty connects to platforms like Airbnb and Booking.com. How would you identify and handle duplicate or conflicting reservation records from multiple sources?
  5. Describe a time when your analysis directly influenced a product or operational decision.
  6. What metrics would you define to assess the overall health of a short-term rental portfolio on a platform like Guesty?
  7. You are given a dataset of guest reviews across many listings. How would you extract and track sentiment trends over time?
  8. How do you prioritise competing analytics requests from different stakeholders when you have limited bandwidth?
  9. Tell us about a time you caught a data error that others had already acted on. What did you do and what was the outcome?
  10. When do you choose Python over SQL for an analysis task, and can you give a concrete example?
  11. Guesty has expanded into new markets. How would you measure whether a new market is performing as expected after launch?
  12. How do you communicate a complex finding to a non-technical audience, such as a property manager or a sales leader?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use these as structural templates. Adapt the specifics to your own experience before your interview.

Q: Describe a time your analysis directly influenced a business decision.

*Situation:* At my previous company, the operations team noticed customer churn had started rising but could not pinpoint the cause.
*Task:* I was asked to investigate usage data and surface any patterns that correlated with customers cancelling their subscriptions.
*Action:* I queried the product event logs in SQL, segmented customers by their onboarding completion status, and built a funnel visualisation in Tableau. I found that customers who had not completed a key setup step within their first week were significantly more likely to churn. I presented the finding with a clear before-and-after comparison of the two groups.
*Result:* The customer success team added a targeted nudge for customers who missed that setup step. Churn in that segment dropped noticeably over the following two months, which the team confirmed at their next quarterly review.

---

Q: How do you handle missing or inconsistent data in a dataset?

*Situation:* I was building a weekly revenue report for a hospitality client, and the source data had booking records with missing check-out dates.
*Task:* I needed to decide whether to exclude those records, impute values, or flag them separately, without distorting the revenue figures.
*Action:* I first checked whether the missing values were random or concentrated in a specific channel. Once I confirmed they came from one integration that had a known sync delay, I wrote a query to separate confirmed and pending records. I surfaced both figures in the dashboard with clear labels, so stakeholders could see the confirmed revenue and the estimated upper bound separately.
*Result:* The team appreciated the transparency. That approach became the standard template for all revenue reports pulling from multiple booking channels.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you had to prioritise competing analytics requests.

*Situation:* In a previous role, I received three data requests in the same week from the product, sales, and operations teams.
*Task:* I had to decide which to tackle first given limited bandwidth, without leaving any team blocked for too long.
*Action:* I mapped each request against two questions: how many people would be blocked without this data, and was there a decision deadline attached? I shared my priority order with all three stakeholders before starting, so no one was caught off guard. I also found that two of the three requests needed the same underlying dataset, which let me build one query and deliver outputs for both simultaneously.
*Result:* All three reports were delivered within the week. Stakeholders appreciated being kept in the loop on sequencing, and the approach reduced back-and-forth messages noticeably.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For SQL and technical questions: Restate the problem in plain business terms first to show you understand the context. Walk through your query logic step by step, mentioning edge cases like ties, nulls, or duplicates before writing the final query. Interviewers want to see structured thinking, not just correct syntax.

For 'how would you build X' questions: Use a simple three-part structure. First, define the key metrics and who will use the output. Second, describe your data sources and any known quality issues. Third, explain how you would display or deliver the insight and how you would keep it updated over time.

For behavioural questions: Use STAR: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep the Situation brief (two sentences at most). Spend most of your time on the Action, since that is what interviewers are evaluating. End with a concrete Result, even if it is qualitative rather than a specific figure.

For prioritisation questions: Show that you weigh business impact and decision deadlines, not just effort or personal preference. Mention that you communicate your priority order to stakeholders before starting, so expectations are managed upfront.

For ambiguity and data quality questions: Show comfort with incomplete information. State the assumptions you would make, explain how you would flag them in your output, and describe how your conclusion would change if the assumptions turned out to be wrong.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Candidates who have spoken to Guesty interviewers typically describe the process as practical and grounded in real scenarios rather than abstract puzzles.

Domain curiosity: Guesty operates in short-term rentals and property management. Interviewers respond well to candidates who understand concepts like occupancy rate, average daily rate, channel mix, and cancellation policy, even without prior hospitality experience. A little homework on the domain goes a long way.

SQL fluency without prompting: You will likely be asked to write queries, not just describe them. Practice window functions, CTEs, and aggregation on real datasets before the interview so you can move through a query confidently.

Communication over complexity: Guesty's analytics work crosses into product, sales, and operations. Interviewers want analysts who can explain a finding to a non-technical stakeholder clearly and concisely, not just produce technically correct outputs.

Ownership mindset: Candidates report that interviewers often ask follow-up questions like 'what did you do next?' or 'how did you make sure the finding was actually used?' Show that you follow an analysis through to action, not just to a report or slide deck.

Comfort with messy data: Real booking data from multiple channels is noisy. A clear framework for auditing, cleaning, and labelling data problems signals maturity to the interviewer.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Learn Guesty's product context. Spend time on Guesty's website and any publicly available documentation or blog posts. Understand how property managers use the platform, what data it generates (bookings, guests, revenue, reviews), and which channel integrations matter. This context shapes every interview answer.

Step 2: Sharpen your SQL. Practice writing queries on hospitality-flavoured datasets. Focus on window functions, CTEs, subqueries, and handling nulls and duplicate rows. Practice sites like Mode Analytics or StrataScratch have relevant exercises. Aim to write queries without looking up syntax.

Step 3: Review your dashboarding tools. Guesty roles typically expect experience with tools like Tableau, Looker, or Power BI. Be ready to describe a dashboard you built, the metrics it tracked, and how you iterated on it based on feedback.

Step 4: Prepare three to five STAR stories. Cover experiences that include: finding a business insight in data, handling a data quality problem, working with a non-technical stakeholder, and prioritising under pressure. Practise saying them aloud, not just reading them off a page.

Step 5: Do a timed practice run. Set a timer and answer a SQL question or a case question aloud as if you are in the interview. Hearing yourself think through a problem helps catch gaps before the real conversation.

Step 6: Prepare genuine questions for your interviewers. Ask about the data stack, how analysts collaborate with product managers, or what a strong first few months looks like in the role. This signals real engagement and helps you assess fit. 07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping business context in SQL answers. Candidates who jump straight to writing a query without restating the business problem signal that they treat data work as purely technical. Always anchor your query to the outcome it serves.

Inventing data in STAR answers. Interviewers at product companies often dig into the specifics of your examples. If you embellish outcomes, follow-up questions will expose it quickly. Use real examples and be honest about the limits of what you measured.

Treating messy data as an exception. Some candidates describe ideal scenarios where data is clean and complete. Experienced analysts know that real-world data, especially from multi-channel integrations, always has gaps. Show that you have a process for this, not just a reaction.

Leading with method instead of insight. A common pitfall is explaining your analytical approach to a stakeholder who only cares about the conclusion. Practise leading with the 'so what' and offering the methodology as backup for those who want to dig deeper.

Not asking questions at the end. Candidates who say they have no questions miss a clear chance to show genuine curiosity about the team and the role. Prepare at least two questions before you go in.

Over-engineering simple problems. If asked for a quick metric or a straightforward query, give a clean, simple answer first. You can mention extensions or edge cases afterwards. Starting with the most complex version often signals poor judgement about scope.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does Guesty typically have for a Data Analyst role?

Candidates typically report two to three rounds: a recruiter screening, a technical round covering SQL and analytical thinking, and a final round with a hiring manager or team lead. Some candidates report an additional take-home case study, though this varies by team and seniority level. The process is not fixed, so treat each stage as an opportunity to show both technical depth and business communication skills.

What SQL level does Guesty expect from a Data Analyst candidate?

Candidates report that Guesty expects solid, working SQL rather than just basic SELECT statements. You should be comfortable with JOINs, GROUP BY, window functions like RANK and LAG, CTEs, and handling nulls or duplicate rows. Questions are typically framed around real business scenarios such as booking data or revenue aggregation, not abstract algorithmic puzzles.

Does Guesty ask Python questions in Data Analyst interviews?

Candidates report that Python comes up occasionally, particularly for data manipulation or scripting tasks. SQL tends to be the primary technical focus at Guesty. It is worth preparing one clear Python example, such as cleaning a dataset or computing a metric with pandas, but you are unlikely to be expected to write production-level code during the interview itself.

What salary can I expect as a Data Analyst at Guesty?

Guesty does not publish salary ranges publicly. Based on the broader Data Analyst market in India tracked by knok jobradar, entry-level roles (0-2 years) typically offer 5-10 LPA and mid-level roles (3-5 years) offer 10-18 LPA. For a Guesty-specific figure, check Glassdoor or levels.fyi, where employees sometimes submit their own compensation data.

How should I prepare for a take-home case study?

If you receive a take-home assignment, structure your answer clearly: lead with the key insight, support it with the data, and close with a concrete recommendation. Since Guesty operates in hospitality tech, framing your analysis in terms of occupancy, revenue, or channel performance will resonate more than a generic approach. Candidates report that presentation clarity matters as much as analytical depth.

Is prior hospitality or prop-tech experience required for a Guesty Data Analyst role?

Candidates report that prior experience in hospitality or property management is not a hard requirement, but familiarity with the domain helps noticeably in interviews. Understanding concepts like average daily rate, occupancy rate, and multi-channel booking management is something you can build quickly by reading about short-term rental operations before your interview. Interviewers tend to notice when a candidate has done that homework.
