01 Overview

Overview

Toast is a US-based restaurant technology company whose core products include a cloud POS system, payment processing, payroll, digital ordering, and marketing tools used by hundreds of thousands of restaurants. As of July 2026, knok's jobradar shows Toast has 375 open roles, signalling strong hiring activity across the company. PM roles at Toast sit at the crossroads of hospitality, fintech, and SaaS. Interviewers typically probe three areas: your ability to empathise deeply with restaurant operators (a non-technical, time-pressured customer), your comfort navigating roadmaps that span both hardware and software, and your ability to drive measurable outcomes in a two-sided marketplace serving both operators and diners.

Candidates report the process typically includes a recruiter screen, a hiring manager conversation, a product exercise (design or strategy), and a cross-functional panel. The exact sequence and number of rounds varies by team and level. Preparation that combines product thinking, domain knowledge about restaurants and payments, and crisp behavioural storytelling tends to perform best.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

The questions below are drawn from candidate reports and reflect what Toast interviewers commonly explore across PM roles.

  1. Toast serves everyone from single-location food trucks to large multi-unit chains. How do you decide which customer segment to prioritise when building a new feature?
  2. Walk me through a product you owned end-to-end. What was your north-star metric and how did you know you succeeded?
  3. A restaurant operator calls support saying the POS is down during a busy dinner service. Walk us through how you would respond as a PM.
  4. How would you improve Toast's online ordering product for independent restaurants?
  5. Describe a time you had to push back on a senior stakeholder or an engineering team. What happened and what was the outcome?
  6. Toast is a two-sided marketplace serving both restaurant operators and diners. Tell me about a time you balanced competing needs from two different user groups.
  7. How do you decide when to build a capability in-house versus buying or partnering?
  8. How would you size the market opportunity for adding an AI-powered menu optimisation tool to Toast's SMB restaurant segment?
  9. Tell me about a product you owned that underperformed. What did you learn from it?
  10. Toast collects rich transactional data from millions of restaurant orders. How would you use that data to build a new feature or product for operators?
  11. How does your approach to roadmap planning change when the team ships both hardware and software?
  12. What would success look like for you in your first few months at Toast, and how would you measure it?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a product you owned that underperformed. What did you learn?

*Situation:* At a previous company, I owned a new onboarding checklist feature meant to help small business customers activate faster after signup. Several weeks after launch, our activation rate was well below the target we had committed to.

*Task:* I was the PM accountable for that activation metric and needed to diagnose the root cause and recover before the quarter ended.

*Action:* I pulled the error logs with our on-call engineer, ran a cohort analysis to identify where users were dropping off, and scheduled several moderated sessions with recently churned sign-ups. The drop-off traced to a single address-verification step that was timing out on slow mobile connections. I worked with engineering to add a fallback manual-entry path and rewrote the copy on that screen to set clearer expectations about the wait.

*Result:* Activation recovered within two sprint cycles and ended the quarter above our original target. We also added a mobile-timeout check to our standard launch checklist so the same blind spot could not repeat.

---

Q: Toast is a two-sided marketplace. Tell me about a time you balanced competing needs from two different user groups.

*Situation:* At a previous company, our supply-side partners requested a bulk discount tool to drive more order volume. At the same time, demand-side users were already reporting that pricing on the platform felt confusing and cluttered.

*Task:* I had to decide whether to ship the discount tool as requested, redesign it, or delay it, without hurting conversion on the demand side.

*Action:* I built a metrics tree starting from the top-line business goal (GMV growth) and traced it down to the leading indicators each side controlled. For supply, the key lever was average order value. For demand, it was conversion rate on the product detail page. I ran a short survey with demand-side users to understand how they processed promotional pricing, then proposed a simplified 'price badge' design that communicated discounts clearly without cluttering the listing. I aligned both teams on a phased A/B test comparing the simplified badge against a full discount-tool build.

*Result:* Demand-side conversion held steady through the test and supply-side average order value improved. The data gave leadership a clear basis for the final decision, removing extended rounds of opinion-driven debate from the calendar.

---

Q: Describe a time you had to push back on a senior stakeholder.

*Situation:* A VP of Sales at my previous company wanted the product team to build a fully custom reporting module for a single large enterprise client, framing it as critical to securing a multi-year renewal.

*Task:* I had to weigh a high-visibility commercial ask against our roadmap commitments and the risk of building something that could not scale.

*Action:* I pulled usage data for our existing reporting features across all enterprise clients and found the requested customisation overlapped substantially with a generalised export tool already on our roadmap. I wrote a short brief comparing the cost of a one-off build versus accelerating the generalised tool by one sprint. I presented this to the VP and the account team with a counter-proposal: we could deliver a subset of the required reports within two weeks using existing infrastructure, with the full generalised tool available the following quarter.

*Result:* The VP agreed to the phased approach. The client signed the renewal on the strength of the near-term deliverable, and the generalised export tool shipped on schedule and was later adopted by several other enterprise clients.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For behavioural questions, use STAR: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task to a couple of sentences each and load most of your word count into Action and Result. Use 'I' rather than 'we' in the Action portion so the interviewer can see your personal contribution clearly.

For product design questions, a clean four-step structure works well: (1) clarify the goal and any constraints, (2) identify the target user and their key jobs-to-be-done, (3) generate and prioritise solutions against effort and impact, (4) define success metrics and flag risks. Avoid jumping to a solution before establishing user context.

For market-sizing or strategy questions, start from the business goal, build a simple metrics tree down to the leading indicators you control, state your assumptions explicitly, and show how you would test them with a small experiment. Saying 'I would validate this by...' signals product maturity.

For incident or crisis questions, structure your answer around three phases: immediate customer-impact containment, root-cause diagnosis, and long-term fix with monitoring. Toast's POS is mission-critical during restaurant service, so answers that lead with operator business impact rather than internal process tend to land better.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Deep operator empathy. Restaurant operators work in a high-pressure, low-margin environment with little tolerance for technology friction. Interviewers want to see you have thought about their daily reality, not just their feature requests.

Data-driven decision making. Toast has rich data from millions of transactions. Candidates who naturally reach for metrics to frame problems and measure outcomes signal they can use that data asset well.

Comfort with hardware and software. Toast ships physical POS terminals alongside cloud software. Candidates who understand how hardware constraints affect release cadence and field support stand out at senior levels.

Marketplace thinking. Toast serves two sides: restaurant operators and diners. Strong answers show awareness that optimising for one side without considering the other creates downstream problems.

Payments and compliance awareness. Toast processes payments for restaurants, which involves regulatory considerations. You do not need to be a compliance expert, but showing that PM decisions in this space carry financial and legal implications is a positive signal.

Clear communication under pressure. Candidates report that interviewers often push back mid-answer to test how you handle challenge. Stay calm, acknowledge the pushback, and build on your answer rather than retreating from it.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week one: build domain knowledge. Read publicly available material on how restaurant POS systems work, how payment processing flows through a restaurant transaction, and what challenges independent restaurants face with technology adoption. Look up recent Toast press releases or earnings summaries to understand current strategic priorities.

Week two: practise product questions. Pick three to four of the questions listed above and write out full STAR or framework-based answers. Then practise saying them aloud. For each design or strategy question, work through the full framework within a strict time limit before reviewing what you missed. Focus on answers that include a concrete metric and a specific decision you personally made.

Week three: sharpen your stories. Identify your two or three strongest career stories that show operator empathy, cross-functional collaboration, and data-driven decision making. Tailor each story so it connects naturally to the restaurant technology context. Practise with a colleague or record yourself to catch filler words and vague phrasing.

Before each round: review the job description for the specific team you are interviewing with. Toast has PM roles across POS, payments, payroll, and digital ordering, and each team weights different experiences differently. Tailor your examples to the team's domain.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping the user before jumping to a solution. In design questions, many candidates immediately describe a feature rather than first establishing who the user is and what problem they are solving. Interviewers notice this pattern quickly.

Using 'we' throughout behavioural answers. If every action is 'we did this' and 'we decided that', the interviewer cannot tell what you personally contributed. Use 'I' for your own actions and credit the team for shared work.

Ignoring the two-sided nature of Toast's business. Answers that only consider the restaurant operator side and forget that diners are also users feel incomplete to interviewers who work on the marketplace daily.

Over-indexing on technology features. Restaurant operators are not software engineers. Answers that lead with technical sophistication rather than operator outcomes signal a mismatch with Toast's customer base.

Not quantifying results. Saying 'the product improved' is weaker than saying 'activation rates recovered above target within two sprint cycles.' Even rough, clearly qualified figures are better than vague outcomes.

Treating the POS-down scenario as a minor bug. A POS failure during peak service means serious revenue loss for the restaurant. Candidates who treat it as a routine support ticket rather than a critical business incident tend to fail this question.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Toast PM interview process typically have?

Candidates report the process typically includes a recruiter screen, a hiring manager conversation, a product exercise (take-home or live), and a panel round with cross-functional partners. Some teams add a second panel or a written case study. The exact structure varies by team and level, so it is worth asking your recruiter what to expect for the specific role you applied to.

For US-based Toast roles, publicly reported data on Glassdoor or levels.fyi gives a more accurate picture of total compensation.

Does Toast give a take-home product exercise or a live case study?

Candidates report both formats depending on the team. Some teams send a take-home prompt and ask you to present your solution in a later round. Others run a live discussion-style case during the panel. Ask your recruiter which format to expect for your role. Either way, practising the design framework aloud is more useful than writing polished documents.

How important is prior restaurant or hospitality experience for a Toast PM role?

Publicly reported feedback from Toast interviewers suggests prior restaurant experience is a plus but not a requirement. What matters more is demonstrating genuine empathy for the operator's daily reality, showing you have done research on how restaurants operate, and connecting your previous PM experience to the challenges Toast's customers face. Candidates from fintech, marketplace, or hardware-adjacent backgrounds tend to bring relevant transferable context.

What is the best way to prepare for the 'POS is down during dinner service' question?

Structure your answer in three phases: immediate containment (what you do right away to limit customer impact), root-cause investigation (how you diagnose the problem), and long-term fix with monitoring. Lead with the operator's business impact rather than internal process. Interviewers want to see that you understand a POS outage during peak hours is a serious financial event for the restaurant, not just a support ticket to be routed.
