02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions reflect patterns commonly reported by Android engineers interviewing at product-first tech companies. Use this list as your prep checklist.

  1. Walk us through how you would design the architecture for a feature that syncs data between the app and a backend in real time.
  2. How do you manage state in a screen that pulls from multiple data sources, such as a local database and a live API?
  3. Explain the Android Activity and Fragment lifecycle. Describe a bug you once traced back to a lifecycle issue.

  5. What is your approach to unit testing ViewModels or business logic layers?

  11. Walk us through a code review you gave or received that meaningfully improved the codebase.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time you identified and fixed a memory leak in a production Android app.

*Situation:* At my previous company, users started reporting that the app became slow and eventually crashed after extended use. Crash logs pointed to OutOfMemoryErrors appearing consistently.

*Task:* I was responsible for diagnosing and fixing the root cause before the next release.

*Action:* I used Android Studio's Memory Profiler to capture heap dumps during a simulated long session. I found that a static reference to a Context inside a singleton helper class was keeping entire Activity instances alive after screen rotation. I refactored the helper to use ApplicationContext instead and removed the static field. I also added a LeakCanary integration to the debug build so the team could catch similar issues early going forward.

*Result:* The memory leak was resolved, crashes disappeared in the next release, and LeakCanary caught two more potential leaks during development in the following sprint.

---

Q: How do you handle API errors and poor network conditions in your Android app?

*Situation:* On a project for a payments feature, we launched in a region where network connectivity was inconsistent, and users were seeing silent failures when transactions did not go through.

*Task:* I was asked to make the payment flow resilient to flaky networks without degrading the experience for users on stable connections.

*Action:* I introduced a retry mechanism with exponential backoff using a custom Retrofit interceptor. For critical transactions, I worked with the backend team to implement an idempotency key pattern so retries would not create duplicate charges. I also added offline-aware UI states so users would see a clear 'Retrying...' message rather than a spinner that never resolved.

*Result:* Failed transaction complaints dropped noticeably according to the support team, and the idempotency key approach became a standard pattern the team reused across other payment flows.

---

Q: Tell us about a feature you shipped under pressure. What trade-offs did you accept?

*Situation:* Two days before a major product launch, the product team requested a last-minute addition: a personalized referral banner on the home screen.

*Task:* I had to build, test, and merge this feature within one day while keeping the existing home screen stable.

*Action:* I scoped the feature down to its minimum viable version. I used a feature flag to hide it from users until fully verified, which let me merge the code safely. I deferred pixel-perfect polish for after launch and logged a follow-up ticket. I wrote a basic integration test to confirm the banner showed and hid correctly under the flag.

*Result:* The feature launched on time with the product release. The polish was completed in the next sprint, and the feature flag approach was adopted by the wider team for future high-risk changes.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For 'how would you design' questions: Start by clarifying requirements (offline support? real-time updates? target device range?). Then describe your data layer choice, your architecture pattern (MVVM or MVI), and how you would handle error states. Finish with how you would test it. Interviewers want structured thinking, not just a correct answer.

For behavioral questions: Use STAR format: Situation (one to two sentences of context), Task (what you were responsible for), Action (what you specifically did, not what the team did), Result (the measurable or observable outcome). Keep Situation and Task brief. Most of your answer should sit in Action and Result.

For debugging questions: Lead with the symptom you observed, walk through your debugging process step by step, and close with what you changed and what you learned. Interviewers care as much about your process as the fix itself.

For trade-off questions: Acknowledge both sides honestly. Name the constraint (time, team size, technical debt), explain what you chose and why, and mention what you tracked as follow-up work. Showing awareness of trade-offs signals engineering maturity.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Strong Android fundamentals. Interviewers at product-first companies typically probe lifecycle management, memory handling, threading (coroutines and Flow), and architecture patterns like MVVM or MVI. Surface-level answers are not enough at mid-level and above.

Product and user empathy. Candidates who only think in code rarely stand out. Interviewers want to hear you consider the end user: how does this feature behave on a slow network? What does the user see when an API call fails? This kind of thinking is especially valued at companies building apps for everyday financial or personal tasks.

Ownership mindset. Companies like aspora move fast and want engineers who take end-to-end responsibility. In your answers, emphasize what you personally did, not just what your team did. Show that you follow through after shipping.

Clean, testable code habits. References to unit testing, code review practices, and technical debt management signal that you can work in a professional team without creating a maintenance burden.

Clear communication. Many technical interviews are also evaluating whether you can explain complex ideas simply. Practice talking through your thought process out loud, not just arriving at the right answer in silence.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Core Android revision. Revisit Activity and Fragment lifecycles, ViewModels, LiveData and StateFlow, coroutines, and Jetpack Compose basics. Build or rebuild a small project from scratch to activate muscle memory rather than just reading documentation.

Week 2: Architecture and system design. Practice designing a feature end-to-end: data layer (Room, Retrofit), repository pattern, dependency injection (Hilt), and offline-first considerations. Draw out the architecture before writing code so you can explain it clearly in an interview.

Week 3: Behavioral preparation. Write out five to six real stories from your work history using STAR format. Cover at least one debugging story, one shipping-under-pressure story, one code-review story, and one time you pushed back on a bad technical decision.

Week 4: Mock interviews and gap-filling. Do at least two mock technical interviews, ideally with a peer or a platform that gives real feedback. Review any gaps you surface. Re-read the job description and map each requirement to a concrete project or story.

Before the interview: Research aspora's product and what problem it solves for its users. Prepare two or three thoughtful questions for the interviewer about the team, the tech stack, or the product roadmap. Arriving with genuine questions signals interest beyond just finding any job.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping straight into an answer on design questions. Taking a moment to ask about constraints (device targets, network assumptions, scale) shows senior-level thinking and often changes the right answer entirely.

Describing what the team did instead of what you did. Behavioral questions are about your judgment and actions. 'We decided to...' is far weaker than 'I proposed... and here is why.' Be specific about your individual contribution.

Giving textbook answers with no personal examples. Saying 'I always write unit tests' is less convincing than walking through a specific test you wrote, what it caught, and how it prevented a production issue.

Underselling the Result in STAR answers. Many candidates tell a good Situation and Action but end vaguely with 'it worked out.' Qualify the outcome: complaints dropped, the feature shipped on time, the team adopted the pattern.

Not asking questions at the end. Saying 'I have no questions' signals low interest. Prepare two or three genuine questions about the team, the tech stack, or the roadmap.

Ignoring non-functional requirements in design answers. Forgetting to mention error handling, accessibility, or performance on low-end devices leaves the impression that you only build for ideal conditions.
