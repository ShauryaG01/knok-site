01 Overview

Overview

Hyreo is a recruitment-technology company that builds AI-powered hiring tools used by enterprise clients across India. With 96 open DevOps roles as of mid-2026, they are actively scaling their engineering and infrastructure teams.

As a DevOps engineer at Hyreo, you would typically work on cloud infrastructure, CI/CD pipelines, containerised deployments, and the reliability of a SaaS platform that handles high-volume recruitment workflows. Candidates report the interview process typically includes a recruiter screening call, a technical round focused on hands-on skills, and a final discussion with a senior engineer or hiring manager.

The role sits in a fast-moving product environment, so interviewers look for people who can balance delivery speed with production stability. Familiarity with cloud-native tooling, scripting, and monitoring is important regardless of experience level.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How would you handle a production outage on a recruitment platform?

*Situation:* At my previous company, our candidate-tracking API went down during a large client's campus hiring drive, affecting live application submissions.

*Task:* I was on-call and needed to restore service within our SLA window while keeping the client team informed throughout.

*Action:* I checked our alerting dashboard to isolate whether it was a pod crash, a database connection issue, or a downstream dependency failure. It turned out a misconfigured config map update had caused pods to fail readiness checks. I rolled back the config map, verified pod health, and restored traffic routing through our load balancer. I also posted a running update in the client-facing Slack channel every few minutes so their team was never left guessing.

*Result:* Service was restored well within our SLA window. We then added a config-map validation step to our deployment pipeline to prevent the same class of error from recurring.

---

Q: Describe a time you reduced cloud costs without reducing reliability.

*Situation:* Our staging environments were running around the clock on the same instance sizes as production, which the team had never revisited after initial setup.

*Task:* I was asked to cut the cloud bill without impacting developer productivity or test coverage.

*Action:* I audited resource utilisation over several weeks using the cloud provider's native cost explorer. I introduced scheduled scaling to shut down non-production workloads outside business hours, right-sized instances based on actual CPU and memory metrics, and moved long-running batch jobs to spot instances with a fallback strategy.

*Result:* Teams using these methods publicly reported savings of 20-40% in industry surveys. Our team saw a meaningful reduction that finance acknowledged in the next quarterly review, and developer workflows were completely unaffected.

---

Q: How did you onboard a new microservice into an existing Kubernetes cluster safely?

*Situation:* Our team was adding a new notification microservice to an existing cluster that already ran several services for our recruitment product.

*Task:* My job was to write the Kubernetes manifests, set up network policies, and ensure the new service did not affect existing workloads.

*Action:* I used a dedicated namespace for the new service and applied resource quotas so it could not starve other pods. I wrote Helm charts following our existing conventions, configured liveness and readiness probes, and introduced a canary deployment so only a small slice of traffic hit the new service first. I ran integration tests in staging before promoting to production.

*Result:* The service went live with zero incidents. The canary approach caught a misconfigured environment variable before it could affect users, which we fixed before full rollout.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use STAR for scenario questions. Situation and Task together should take no more than a couple of sentences. Most of your answer should be the Action: what you specifically did, what tools you chose, and why. The Result should be concrete, even if the metric is qualitative rather than numerical.

For architecture or design questions, structure your answer in three steps: state your constraints first (cost, reliability, team size), then walk through your design choice, then explain the trade-offs you accepted. This shows senior-level thinking and avoids the trap of jumping straight to a tool name.

For 'how would you' questions, resist the urge to open with a product name. Start with 'the first thing I would do is understand the current state,' then layer in tooling. Interviewers at product companies like Hyreo want to see that you diagnose before you prescribe.

Keep answers to two or three minutes for technical rounds. Candidates report that Hyreo interviewers typically follow up with clarifying questions, so leave room for the conversation rather than trying to cover every edge case upfront.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Ownership mindset. Hyreo is a product company, not a services firm. Interviewers want to see that you treat reliability and deployment quality as your personal responsibility, not a ticket that belongs to someone else.

Practical depth over theory. Expect questions that ask you to walk through real configurations, not just name technologies. Saying 'I would use Terraform' is less useful than explaining how you structure your modules and handle state locking in a team environment.

Communication under pressure. Because Hyreo's platform affects clients' live hiring processes, interviewers care whether you can explain an outage clearly to a non-technical stakeholder while simultaneously working the problem.

Security awareness by default. Recruitment platforms handle personally identifiable information for large numbers of candidates. Interviewers typically probe whether you build security into your pipelines from the start rather than bolting it on later.

Collaboration with developers. DevOps at a SaaS company means working closely with backend and frontend teams. Show that you can write documentation, review developer PRs for infrastructure implications, and give useful feedback without being a blocker.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Solidify your core skills.
Revisit Kubernetes fundamentals: pods, deployments, services, ingress, and namespaces. Practice writing Helm charts from scratch. If you have not used Terraform recently, rebuild a small AWS or GCP environment to refresh your muscle memory before the interview.

Week 2: Go deep on CI/CD and observability.
Set up a sample pipeline in GitHub Actions or GitLab CI that includes linting, testing, image building, and a canary deployment step. Add Prometheus and Grafana to your local cluster and write a couple of alerting rules so you can speak about them from direct experience.

Week 3: Practice communication and system design.
Do at least three mock outage walk-throughs out loud, timing yourself. For each one, draft a short incident report as if you were writing to a client. Practice the 'how would you design' format for architecture scenarios relevant to a SaaS HR platform, such as handling data residency requirements or rolling deployments with zero downtime.

Before the interview, read Hyreo's public LinkedIn and any engineering blog posts if available. Understand what their platform does so you can tie your examples to their actual product context. Prepare a few questions to ask the interviewer about their deployment frequency, on-call culture, and how the DevOps team is structured relative to product engineering.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Naming tools without showing depth. Listing Kubernetes, Terraform, and Ansible on your resume is expected at this level. Failing to explain how you used them in a specific situation is what disqualifies candidates in the technical round.

Ignoring the product context. Generic infrastructure answers that could apply to any company miss the mark at Hyreo. Tailor your examples to scenarios involving uptime for client-facing features, data privacy for candidate records, and integration reliability with third-party HR systems.

Skipping the 'why'. When you describe a technical choice, always explain why you picked it over the alternatives. Interviewers use this to judge depth of understanding, not just familiarity with names.

Underestimating the soft-skills check. Candidates report that Hyreo interviewers pay close attention to how you communicate, especially in outage or conflict scenarios. Being technically correct but unclear or dismissive is a common reason for rejection that candidates often do not anticipate.

Not asking questions at the end. Leaving the 'do you have any questions?' slot empty signals low interest. Ask about deployment frequency, team structure, or how they handle on-call rotations. These questions show you are evaluating them as seriously as they are evaluating you.
