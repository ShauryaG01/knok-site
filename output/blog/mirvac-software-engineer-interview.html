01 Overview

Overview

Mirvac is an Australian integrated real estate group operating across property development, investment management, and construction. Their technology teams build digital products that support property transactions, asset tracking, customer portals, and internal platforms. Engineers at Mirvac work at the intersection of complex business domains and modern software delivery.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions surface consistently in Mirvac Software Engineer interviews, based on candidate-reported experiences:

  1. Walk us through a complex system you designed from scratch. What trade-offs did you make?
  2. How do you approach a production incident when you have limited visibility into the root cause?
  3. Tell us about a time you pushed back on a product or business requirement. How did you handle that conversation?
  4. How do you maintain code quality in a team that is moving fast?
  5. Describe your hands-on experience with cloud platforms (AWS, Azure, or GCP) in a production environment.
  6. Mirvac works across property development, investment, and construction. How would you model a property asset's data through its full lifecycle?
  7. How have you balanced pressure to ship quickly against the risk of accumulating technical debt?
  8. Walk us through how you would design a scalable API for a high-traffic property listings platform.
  9. How do you decide when to adopt a new technology versus sticking with what the team already knows?
  10. Tell us about a time you worked with non-technical stakeholders. How did you communicate a technical constraint or trade-off?
  11. Have you ever inherited a legacy codebase? How did you improve it without breaking existing behaviour?
  12. Describe a situation where you identified or prevented a security vulnerability in your application.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through a complex system you designed from scratch.

*Situation:* My team needed a real-time notification service for a logistics platform handling a high volume of daily shipments. There was no existing system, and the product team needed it live within one sprint.

*Task:* I was the lead engineer responsible for both the architecture and the first working version.

*Action:* I chose a pub/sub model using a managed message broker, designed event schemas that downstream consumers could extend without breaking changes, and added a dead-letter queue for failed deliveries. I wrote a short design document so senior engineers could review the approach before I wrote any code.

*Result:* The service launched on schedule, handled the initial load cleanly, and the design review caught one critical edge case early. The team later extended it to cover email and push notifications with minimal rework.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you pushed back on a requirement.

*Situation:* A product manager asked us to skip input validation on a form to ship a feature before a product demo.

*Task:* As the engineer owning that module, I needed to either comply or make a case for doing it properly.

*Action:* I set up a short call, walked the PM through two concrete examples of what malformed input could do to our database, and proposed a middle path: ship with basic server-side validation now and defer the polished front-end experience to the next sprint.

*Result:* The PM agreed. We shipped on time with validation in place. A few weeks later we caught a malformed request in the logs that would have caused data corruption without that check.

---

Q: Have you worked on a legacy codebase? How did you improve it without breaking existing behaviour?

*Situation:* I joined a team maintaining an ageing monolith with no test coverage on a critical billing module.

*Task:* The team wanted to refactor the billing logic, but no one wanted to touch it because breakages were invisible until a customer complained.

*Action:* I wrote characterisation tests first to document existing behaviour before changing anything. Then I extracted one small function at a time, each covered by its own unit tests. I used feature flags to roll out changes to a small slice of traffic before full deployment.

*Result:* We refactored the billing module over three sprints with no production incidents. The test suite became the foundation the next engineer on the team built on.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions. Structure every story as: Situation (brief context), Task (your specific responsibility), Action (what you personally did, not 'we'), Result (a clear outcome, quantified where possible). Interviewers follow this flow, so a missing Result leaves your answer feeling unfinished.

Think aloud for system design. For architecture questions, narrate your reasoning as you go: 'I am starting with the read/write ratio because that drives the caching decision.' Mirvac's tech teams work across complex domains, so showing structured thinking matters as much as the final design.

Clarify before you code. For any coding problem, restate the question in your own words, state your assumptions, and confirm the expected inputs and outputs before writing a single line. This mirrors how engineers work in practice and signals seniority.

Match depth to level. Entry-level answers can focus on what you did and what you learned. Mid-level and senior answers should include trade-offs considered, stakeholder impact, and what you would do differently with hindsight.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Domain curiosity. You do not need prior real estate experience, but interviewers notice candidates who ask sharp questions about the business context behind a technical problem. Mirvac's products span property, investment, and construction, so genuine curiosity about how those pieces connect is a clear signal.

Strong engineering fundamentals. Candidates report consistent emphasis on data structures, system design, and code readability. Products at Mirvac need to be maintained over years, so well-reasoned, clean code is valued over clever tricks.

Communication under ambiguity. Many problems span multiple teams and business units. The ability to ask the right clarifying question and explain a technical trade-off to a non-engineer comes up consistently in interview feedback.

Ownership and follow-through. Stories that show you saw a problem from identification to resolution, including the uncomfortable parts, resonate strongly. Avoid answers where 'the team' did everything and your specific contribution is unclear.

Practical cloud and API experience. Given the scale of Mirvac's digital products, hands-on experience with cloud-native architectures and RESTful or event-driven API design is a recurring theme in technical interviews.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Understand Mirvac's business. Read their investor reports and product announcements. Note where technology intersects with property development and investment operations. This context sharpens your answers to domain-specific design questions considerably.

Step 2: Map your experience to relevant skills. Candidates report Mirvac uses cloud-native infrastructure and modern web frameworks. Refresh your hands-on knowledge of the services and patterns you have actually used in production, rather than ones you have only read about.

Step 3: Build several strong STAR stories. Cover: a complex technical decision, a conflict or pushback, a failure and recovery, a process improvement, and a collaboration with non-technical stakeholders. A single well-crafted story can flex to answer several different questions.

Step 4: Practise system design out loud. Pick a domain relevant to real estate (property search, booking workflows, asset tracking) and design it while narrating your reasoning. The goal is to get comfortable stating trade-offs clearly and confidently.

Step 5: Do a mock interview with time pressure. Practise with a peer or an AI interviewer. The aim is not to memorise answers but to build the habit of structuring your thoughts quickly. Keep answers focused and avoid trailing off into unrelated details.

Step 6: Prepare sharp questions for the interviewer. Questions like 'What does the on-call rotation look like?' or 'How do engineering and product collaborate on roadmap decisions?' signal genuine interest and a senior mindset.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Vague STAR answers. Saying 'I worked on a microservices project' without describing your specific contribution, the constraints you faced, and the measurable outcome makes it hard for interviewers to gauge your level.

Skipping clarification in coding rounds. Candidates who jump straight into code often solve the wrong problem. Spend a moment restating the question and confirming assumptions before writing anything.

Underselling cross-functional work. Mirvac values engineers who communicate beyond their immediate team. If your answers focus only on code and never mention how you worked with product, design, or business stakeholders, you leave a key signal on the table.

Generic system design answers. Responses like 'I would use microservices and Kafka' without connecting to Mirvac's actual business context (property lifecycles, investment portfolios, customer portals) miss what the interviewer is testing for.

Not preparing questions for the end. Candidates who have nothing to ask at the close of an interview often appear unprepared or disengaged. Prepare at least a few thoughtful questions in advance so the conversation ends on a strong note.
