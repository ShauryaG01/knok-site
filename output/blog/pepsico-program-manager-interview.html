01 Overview

Overview

Pepsico currently has 26 open Technical Program Manager roles listed on knok jobradar, making it one of the more active hirers for this profile right now. The company runs large programs in digital transformation, supply chain technology, and data platforms, so TPM interviews here are not generic. Candidates typically report a multi-stage process: a recruiter or HR screen, one or two technical and behavioral panels, and a final round with senior leadership. Expect questions that go beyond delivery mechanics and push you to show how your programs created measurable business value. Pepsico's interviewers often probe your ability to work across business and engineering stakeholders in a matrixed, global organisation, so come prepared with stories that show cross-functional influence without direct authority. Knowing Pepsico's recent digital and sustainability agenda will help you contextualise your answers.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates who have interviewed at Pepsico for TPM roles report a consistent set of themes across behavioral and situational questions.

  1. Walk me through a complex, cross-functional program you led end to end. What was your role and how did you keep it on track?
  2. Pepsico runs programs across supply chain, commercial tech, and enterprise platforms. Tell me about a program that cut across multiple business domains. How did you manage alignment?
  3. Describe a time a program you owned was at serious risk of missing its deadline. What specifically did you do?
  4. How do you manage dependencies between engineering teams who have competing priorities and no shared manager?
  5. Tell me about a time you had to push back on scope creep from a senior stakeholder. How did you handle the conversation?
  6. How do you decide which risks to escalate to leadership versus resolve yourself?
  7. Describe how you communicate program status upward. How do you tailor the message for a C-suite audience versus an engineering team?
  8. Tell me about a time you used data to change the direction of a program or influence a key decision.
  9. How do you build credibility and trust with engineering teams when you don't manage them directly?
  10. Pepsico invests in large-scale technology rollouts globally. Describe a time you drove adoption of a new system or platform across a resistant user base.
  11. Tell me about a program where legacy systems or technical debt created significant blockers. How did you resolve it?
  12. Describe a situation where business goals and technology constraints were in direct conflict. How did you drive alignment?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use these as templates and adapt them to your own experience. Follow the STAR structure: Situation, Task, Action, Result.

Q: Walk me through a complex, cross-functional program you led end to end.

*Situation:* At my previous company, we needed to migrate a core order-management system to the cloud. The program involved engineering, security, legal, finance, and business operations teams across three offices.

*Task:* I was the program manager responsible for the roadmap, cross-team dependencies, and executive reporting.

*Action:* I set up a fortnightly steering committee with a structured agenda, created a shared risk register on Confluence that every team updated weekly, and introduced a one-page status report that went to the CTO each week. When the security team flagged a compliance gap partway through the program, I convened a focused working session, brought in legal, and we isolated a fix within the same sprint.

*Result:* The program launched on the committed date. The compliance issue was resolved before go-live, and the CTO cited the risk register process as a model for future programs.

---

Q: Describe a time a program was at serious risk. What did you do?

*Situation:* A third-party vendor delivering a critical API integration went silent for two weeks during a product launch program at my previous employer.

*Task:* I needed to protect the launch date without compromising quality or burning team trust.

*Action:* I escalated immediately to our vendor account manager and their VP while our engineering lead assessed what we could build internally as a fallback. I ran a daily stand-up with both the internal team and the vendor to restore visibility. I also briefed the product VP on the risk and presented two scenarios: a revised date with the vendor, or a reduced-scope launch on the original date.

*Result:* The vendor recovered and delivered. We launched on the original date with full scope. The stakeholder briefing meant leadership was never blindsided, which strengthened my credibility for future escalations.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you drove alignment between business and technology teams with conflicting goals.

*Situation:* Our marketing team wanted a personalisation engine live before a major campaign, but the data engineering team said the timeline was not realistic given data quality issues in the source systems.

*Task:* My job was to find a path that served both teams without forcing an unrealistic deadline or an indefinite delay.

*Action:* I organised a joint working session where both teams mapped out their constraints on a shared timeline. I introduced a phased delivery plan: a rule-based personalisation model in phase one using clean data, with the ML-driven engine following in phase two once data quality was addressed. I documented this as a formal agreement signed off by both heads.

*Result:* Marketing ran their campaign with the phase-one engine. The data team had a realistic runway to fix the underlying issues. Both teams described the process as collaborative rather than combative, and phase two launched on schedule.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

The STAR method is the foundation for all behavioral questions at Pepsico TPM interviews. Each answer needs four parts: Situation (brief context), Task (your specific responsibility), Action (what YOU did, not what the team did), and Result (the outcome, ideally tied to business impact).

A few additions that Pepsico interviewers specifically value:

Lead with 'I', not 'we'. Interviewers want to understand your individual contribution. Saying 'we delivered the program' leaves your role ambiguous. Say 'I designed the dependency tracking process, which the team then used.'

Tie outcomes to business value. Pepsico is a commercial organisation. Saying 'the program launched on time' is fine. Saying 'the program launched on time, enabling the seasonal product line to hit shelves before the competitor' is stronger. Connect your delivery to revenue, cost, risk, or market timing.

Show stakeholder complexity. The more your story involves senior stakeholders, cross-functional conflict, or global teams, the more relevant it is to a Pepsico TPM role. Stories with a single team and no tension will not stand out.

The RACI framing is useful for questions about cross-team coordination. Briefly explaining who was Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed shows structured thinking and signals you can operate in a matrixed environment.

For risk questions, use a simple three-part structure: how you identified the risk, how you assessed severity and likelihood, and what your mitigation or escalation path was. Avoid vague answers like 'I flagged it to the team'. Name the stakeholder, describe the conversation, and share the outcome.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Pepsico's TPM interviewers typically look for a specific combination of skills, not just general program management experience.

Delivery credibility. Can you point to programs you personally drove to completion, with clear ownership of the roadmap, risks, and stakeholder communication? Vague answers about 'supporting' programs will not score well.

Influence without authority. Pepsico TPMs rarely have direct reports across all the teams they coordinate. Interviewers want to see that you can drive engineering, product, and business teams toward a shared goal using structure, communication, and trust rather than hierarchy.

Commercial awareness. Pepsico is a global consumer goods company. Interviewers are drawn to candidates who frame program outcomes in business terms: market timing, cost avoidance, revenue enablement, or supply chain reliability. Pure technical delivery stories with no business context tend to fall flat.

Structured risk thinking. Program managers who escalate everything annoy senior leaders. Those who never escalate create nasty surprises. Interviewers want to see that you have a principled approach to deciding when to absorb a risk, when to mitigate, and when to escalate.

Communication clarity. TPMs at Pepsico interface with C-suite stakeholders, global engineering teams, and external vendors. Interviewers assess whether you can adjust your communication style for each audience and whether your written and verbal updates are crisp and decision-oriented.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Start with the job description. Read every line and note the exact capabilities mentioned: program governance, stakeholder management, technical fluency, digital transformation, or supply chain technology. Map each capability to a story from your own experience.

Research Pepsico's technology agenda. Pepsico has publicly discussed investments in digital manufacturing, direct-to-consumer platforms, and data-driven supply chain programs. Knowing this context lets you frame your answers in terms that resonate with the interviewer. Search for recent Pepsico technology announcements or their annual report for reference.

Prepare five to seven core stories. Each story should be versatile enough to answer multiple question types. A good program delivery story can be adapted to answer questions about risk management, stakeholder communication, or cross-team alignment. Prepare stories that show scope, complexity, and measurable outcomes.

Practice the STAR structure out loud. Reading your answers is not the same as saying them. A strong STAR answer typically runs a couple of minutes. Too short suggests superficiality; too long loses the interviewer.

Prepare smart questions for your interviewer. Ask about the current programs the team is running, how success is measured for a TPM at Pepsico, and how technology and business teams collaborate day to day. These questions show genuine interest and give you useful information about the role.

Review your resume line by line the evening before the interview. Every bullet point is fair game. If your resume mentions a tool, a methodology, or a program, be ready to go deep on it.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Using 'we' throughout your answers. Interviewers cannot assess your individual contribution if every sentence starts with 'we'. Practise replacing 'we' with 'I' and specifying exactly what you did.

Telling a story with no conflict or tension. The best STAR answers have a real problem in them: a missed dependency, a difficult stakeholder, a vendor who went dark, a scope fight. If your story has no obstacle, it sounds untested.

Skipping the business outcome. Saying 'the program delivered on time' is the minimum bar. Saying what that delivery enabled for the business is what sets you apart. Always connect your result to a business outcome, even if you can only describe it qualitatively.

Being too technical or too vague. TPM interviews sit in the middle: you need to show enough technical understanding to earn credibility with engineering teams, but enough business fluency to communicate with leadership. Err toward neither extreme.

Not knowing Pepsico's context. Walking into a Pepsico interview without knowing they are a consumer goods company running major digital and supply chain programs signals low interest. Spend time understanding the industry and the company's recent technology direction.

Answering the question you wished they asked. If the interviewer asks about a risk you escalated, answer that question precisely. Do not pivot to a different story because it sounds better. Listen carefully, answer directly, and add context only if it strengthens the answer.

How many rounds does a Pepsico TPM interview typically have?

Candidates typically report a process involving a recruiter or HR screen, followed by one or two technical and behavioral panel interviews, and then a final round with senior leadership. The exact number of rounds varies by team and location. Some candidates report the entire process completing within a few weeks, while senior-level roles can take longer.

Does Pepsico ask coding or technical design questions for TPM roles?

Pepsico TPM interviews are typically not coding-heavy, but technical fluency is expected. Candidates report being asked to explain system design concepts, describe how they have worked with engineering teams on architectural decisions, and discuss tools like JIRA, Confluence, or data platforms. You are unlikely to be asked to write code, but shallow technical answers will hurt your score.

How should I prepare if I am moving from a project manager role to a Technical Program Manager role?
