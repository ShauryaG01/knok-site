02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly in DevOps interviews at product companies like Rapido. Candidates who have interviewed there report a strong tilt toward operational scenarios, cost awareness, and reliability at scale.

  3. Describe a production incident you handled. How did you detect it, contain it, and prevent it from recurring?

  5. What is your approach to container image security, from build to runtime?
  6. How have you optimised cloud infrastructure costs without impacting reliability or performance?
  7. Explain how you would set up centralised logging and alerting for a large fleet of microservices.
  8. Rapido runs on a mix of managed and self-managed infrastructure. How do you decide which workloads go where?
  9. How do you handle secret management in a Kubernetes environment? Walk through your preferred approach.

  11. What metrics do you track to measure the health of a DevOps pipeline, and how do you act on them?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a production incident you handled. How did you detect it, contain it, and prevent recurrence?

*Situation:* Our ride-dispatch service started throwing 5xx errors late on a Friday night. Alerts fired in our monitoring system quickly after the spike began.

*Task:* I was on-call and needed to identify root cause fast, restore service, and make sure the same thing did not happen again.

*Action:* I checked our Grafana dashboards first and saw database connection pool exhaustion. I immediately increased the pool limit via a config change, which bought us time. Then I traced the cause to a newly deployed service that had a connection leak. I rolled it back using our standard rollback script, confirmed error rates dropped to zero, and wrote a detailed postmortem by end of that same day. I also added a connection count alert so the team would catch this class of problem earlier next time.

*Result:* Downtime was contained to a short window. The postmortem led to adding connection leak detection to our pre-deploy checklist, and we have not had a repeat incident since.

---

Q: How have you optimised cloud infrastructure costs without impacting reliability?

*Situation:* My team's AWS bill had grown sharply quarter-on-quarter as we scaled. Leadership asked us to find savings without touching SLAs.

*Task:* I owned the cost optimisation initiative across our Kubernetes and RDS footprint.

*Action:* I ran a full audit using AWS Cost Explorer and identified three quick wins: over-provisioned node groups, idle NAT gateways, and on-demand instances running workloads perfectly suited for Spot. I moved batch and non-critical jobs to Spot instances, right-sized node groups based on actual CPU and memory metrics, and eliminated redundant NAT gateways by routing through a shared one. I tracked every change in a shared doc so the team could see the rationale.

*Result:* We saw meaningful savings. Industry surveys commonly cite 20-30% cloud cost reductions from similar optimisation efforts at comparable companies. Our exact figures are internal, but reliability metrics stayed flat throughout the process.

---

Q: How do you manage Kubernetes cluster upgrades with zero downtime?

*Situation:* We were two minor versions behind on our EKS cluster and needed to catch up before support ended.

*Task:* I had to plan and execute the upgrade across staging and production without affecting live services.

*Action:* I started by reviewing the Kubernetes changelog for deprecations that could break our workloads. I updated Helm charts and manifests to remove deprecated API versions first. I ran a full dry run in staging, validated all workloads came up healthy, and then ran a rolling node replacement in production during a low-traffic window. I used PodDisruptionBudgets to ensure no service lost more than one replica at a time. The whole process went into a runbook so the next person could repeat it without guesswork.

*Result:* The upgrade completed with no user-visible downtime and no support tickets raised. The runbook is now part of our standard SRE playbook.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use STAR for every scenario question. STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) gives your answer a clear shape. Rapido interviewers, like most product-company interviewers, want to hear what you specifically did, not what 'we' did as a team. Keep Situation and Task brief, spend most of your time on Action, and always close with a concrete Result.

For system design questions, use a structured approach. Start with requirements (clarify scale, SLAs, constraints), then sketch the high-level architecture, discuss data flows, identify bottlenecks, and walk through how you would optimise. For a company like Rapido, always factor in cost and peak-load behaviour from the start.

For cost and trade-off questions, structure your answer as: here is the problem I observed, here are the options I considered, here is what I chose and why, and here is the outcome. Interviewers want to see that you weigh reliability against cost, not just chase savings.

For 'how do you handle X' questions, anchor your answer in a real example. Saying 'I would use Terraform' is far weaker than 'I used Terraform to manage our GKE clusters, and here is one specific dependency issue I debugged with it.' The story is what sticks.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates report about Rapido engineering interviews, interviewers are typically looking for the following qualities.

Ownership at scale. Rapido's systems handle millions of requests. Interviewers want to see that you have managed infrastructure that had real consequences when it went down, and that you owned incidents end-to-end rather than handing them off.

Kubernetes depth, not just exposure. Many candidates can describe Kubernetes concepts. Rapido interviewers tend to probe past surface knowledge: how do your PodDisruptionBudgets actually work, how do you handle etcd backup, what happens when a node goes NotReady? Be ready for follow-up questions on whatever you mention.

Cost consciousness. As a growth-stage company, Rapido cares about cloud spend. If you have driven cost optimisation initiatives, make those stories ready. Numbers help, but explain the method even if the exact figures are internal.

CI/CD as a product mindset. Treat your pipeline as a product used by developers every day. Interviewers respond well to candidates who think about developer experience, pipeline reliability, and build times, not just deployment mechanics.

Clear communication under pressure. Incident handling questions are partly a test of how you think out loud. Walk through your reasoning step by step, name the tools you use, and explain why, not just what.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Core infrastructure and Kubernetes

Revise Kubernetes internals: scheduling, networking, storage, RBAC, and cluster upgrades. Set up a local cluster with Minikube or kind and practice rolling updates, node drains, and PodDisruptionBudgets by hand. Review your own past work and pick 3-4 stories that clearly show ownership of live systems.

Week 2: CI/CD, observability, and security

Build or rebuild a CI/CD pipeline using tools you know (Jenkins, GitHub Actions, ArgoCD, or similar). Review how you would set up centralised logging (ELK or Loki are commonly used) and alerting (Prometheus and Alertmanager come up often in interviews). Revise secret management approaches: HashiCorp Vault and Kubernetes Secrets with external secret operators are frequently discussed.

Week 3: System design and company context

Practise designing a ride-dispatch or location-tracking backend at scale. Think through auto-scaling, database failover, and multi-region considerations. Check LinkedIn for posts by Rapido's engineering team to get a feel for their current stack and problems. Review your cost optimisation stories and make sure you can explain the method, not just the outcome.

Before the interview

Prepare genuine questions for the interviewer. Good ones include: how does the team handle on-call rotations, what does a typical deployment look like today, and what is the biggest infrastructure challenge the team is actively solving.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Staying too high-level. The most common mistake is describing what a technology does rather than what you did with it. 'We used Terraform for IaC' tells the interviewer nothing. 'I wrote Terraform modules for our VPC and EKS setup, and here is one tricky dependency issue I debugged' shows actual experience.

Saying 'we' throughout. Collaborative work is fine, but interviewers are evaluating you personally. Be clear about what your role was in each story. 'My team did X, I specifically owned Y' works well and is honest.

Skipping the Result. STAR answers without Results feel unfinished. If you cannot share exact figures, say 'the exact numbers are internal, but reliability improved as measured by our SLA dashboard' or describe the qualitative outcome clearly.

Over-indexing on tools. Rapido interviewers are not looking for a list of tools you have touched. They want to see judgment: why did you choose this tool, what were the trade-offs, and what would you do differently now?

Not asking about the team or the problem space. Interviews are two-way. Candidates who ask nothing come across as uninterested. Prepare at least two genuine questions about the team's current challenges or the infrastructure they are building.
