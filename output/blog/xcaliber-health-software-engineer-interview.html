02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates who have interviewed at health-tech product companies typically encounter questions across three areas: technical depth, domain awareness, and behavioural fit. Here are 12 questions commonly reported for Software Engineer roles at health-focused companies:

  1. Walk me through a backend service you built from scratch. What tradeoffs did you make?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time you debugged a production issue under pressure.

*Situation:* Our team's API started returning errors for a subset of users on a Friday evening, affecting a core feature used by healthcare staff.
*Task:* I was the on-call engineer and needed to identify the root cause quickly, then either fix or roll back before the problem worsened.
*Action:* I pulled logs immediately, narrowed the failure to a single endpoint, and traced it to a recent deploy that introduced a null-pointer case for users with incomplete profile data. I wrote a targeted patch, tested it in staging, and deployed with the team lead's approval.
*Result:* The fix went live within a couple of hours. I then wrote a post-mortem and added a regression test so the same scenario could not recur silently.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you improved the performance of a system.

*Situation:* A report generation feature was timing out for large datasets, causing user complaints and a growing support ticket queue.
*Task:* I was asked to diagnose and fix the issue without breaking existing functionality or changing the API contract.
*Action:* I profiled the queries using the database explain plan, found several missing indexes, and rewrote one query that was fetching far more columns than needed. I also added pagination to the API response to prevent oversized payloads.
*Result:* Query time dropped significantly for large datasets. The fix was reviewed and merged within the same sprint, and timeout errors stopped appearing in our monitoring dashboards.

---

Q: Describe a situation where you disagreed with a technical decision.

*Situation:* During a sprint, the team decided to store audit logs in the same primary database to save time on infrastructure setup.
*Task:* I felt this would create scaling problems down the line and raised the concern in our design review, even though it risked slowing the sprint.
*Action:* I prepared a short comparison showing how high write volume from logs could affect read performance for core features. I proposed separating logs into a write-optimised store and offered to set it up myself to reduce the team's overhead.
*Result:* The team agreed to the separate store. The decision came up again several months later when log volume grew, and the architect noted that separating early had saved a painful live migration.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For behavioural questions, use the STAR structure: Situation (one or two sentences of context), Task (your specific responsibility), Action (what you personally did, using 'I' not 'we'), Result (what changed, with a concrete outcome wherever possible). Keep each answer to under three minutes when spoken aloud.

For system design questions, work through four steps. First, clarify requirements and constraints (ask about scale, expected users, and data sensitivity before drawing anything). Second, sketch the high-level components. Third, go deeper on the parts the interviewer shows interest in. Fourth, call out data privacy considerations (encryption, access controls, audit trails) proactively, since these matter in any health-tech context.

For coding questions, narrate your thinking as you go. State your approach before writing the first line of code, check edge cases aloud, and mention time and space complexity once you reach a working solution. Product company interviewers often value clear reasoning as much as a perfect implementation.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Technical reliability. Health-tech products handle sensitive data and must work correctly. Interviewers look for engineers who test their code, think through failure cases, and care about correctness, not just shipping speed.

Domain awareness. You do not need a medical background, but showing that you understand why data privacy matters in healthcare (patient trust, regulatory obligations, breach consequences) signals that you will make better engineering decisions day-to-day.

Ownership. Candidates who describe their personal contribution clearly (using 'I' and specific actions) come across as more credible than those who speak only in terms of 'we did everything together.' Interviewers are assessing your judgment and your initiative, not just the team's output.

Communication. Whether it is a design discussion or a difficult conversation with a stakeholder, interviewers want to see that you can explain technical ideas clearly to different audiences, including people who are not engineers.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Technical foundations. Review data structures, algorithms, and common system design patterns. Focus on topics relevant to health-tech: API design, database schema design for structured records, caching strategies, and handling sensitive datasets securely.

Week 2: Domain and company research. Read about how software powers healthcare workflows (scheduling, records, billing, lab results). Understand why data-privacy frameworks exist for health data and what they mean for an engineer practically (encryption at rest and in transit, role-based access controls, audit logs). Look at XCaliber Health's public presence (website, LinkedIn, any published content) to understand their product focus and customer segment.

Week 3: Behavioural preparation. Write down three to five work stories that cover: solving a hard technical problem, handling a disagreement or conflict, improving a process or system, and collaborating with non-engineers. Practice each story in STAR format until it takes under three minutes to tell.

Before the interview. Prepare two or three thoughtful questions for the interviewer about the team's engineering practices, how they handle production incidents, or how product decisions are made. Asking good questions signals genuine interest and preparation.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

  1. Skipping the 'why' on data decisions. Candidates sometimes describe how they stored or processed data without explaining the reasoning. In health-tech interviews, the rationale behind a design choice often matters as much as the choice itself.
  1. Using 'we' for everything. Saying 'we built the API' tells the interviewer nothing about your personal contribution. Be specific: 'I designed the endpoint contract and wrote the core handler; a colleague handled the auth middleware.'
  1. Treating system design as purely theoretical. Candidates who sketch a technically elegant design but never mention data privacy, audit trails, or failure recovery can appear naive about what health-tech engineering actually requires in practice.
  1. Not asking clarifying questions. Jumping straight into a coding or design answer without checking assumptions is a common mistake. Interviewers often leave requirements deliberately open-ended to see whether you ask before building.
  1. Underestimating the behavioural round. Some engineers prepare only for coding and system design. Behavioural interviews at product companies carry real weight, and candidates who have no concrete stories ready often lose offers at this stage.
