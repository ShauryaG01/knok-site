01 Overview

Overview

Hotelogix is a cloud-based hotel property management SaaS company that serves independent hotels and small hotel chains around the world. Their platform is always-on: hotel front desks, booking engines, and billing systems rely on it at every hour, so reliability engineering is a core priority for the DevOps team, not an afterthought. As of mid-2026, Hotelogix has 44 open roles, with DevOps being a key area as they scale infrastructure to support a growing global hospitality customer base.

The interview process typically involves two to three rounds. Candidates report a recruiter screening call first, then one or two technical rounds covering system design, CI/CD pipeline discussions, and hands-on troubleshooting or debugging exercises. Some candidates also report a final conversation with a hiring manager or team lead. The overall tone is practical and product-focused, so expect questions that connect infrastructure decisions to real hotel operator needs rather than purely academic problems.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These are the questions candidates most commonly report from Hotelogix DevOps interviews. Prepare a concrete example for each.

  1. Walk us through a CI/CD pipeline you built or improved. What tools did you choose and why?
  2. How would you design a deployment pipeline for a multi-tenant hotel management SaaS where a bad deploy must be rolled back without disrupting active hotel check-ins?
  3. How do you manage infrastructure as code? Which tools have you used: Terraform, Pulumi, or CloudFormation?
  4. Describe how you set up monitoring and alerting for a production service. What metrics matter most to you?
  5. Tell us about an incident you handled. How did you diagnose it and what did the post-mortem look like?
  6. How do you handle database migrations in a live SaaS environment without downtime?
  7. Our platform sees booking spikes around holidays and festivals. How would you design auto-scaling to handle that load?
  8. How do you manage secrets and credentials across multiple environments?
  9. Describe your experience with container orchestration. How have you used Kubernetes or Docker in a production setup?
  10. How do you approach cloud cost optimisation? Give a concrete example of savings you achieved.
  11. How do you ensure security and compliance across your cloud infrastructure?
  12. A hotel client reports their property management system is slow during peak morning check-out hours. How do you investigate this?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through a CI/CD pipeline you built or improved.

*Situation:* My previous company ran a multi-tenant SaaS product where deployments were fully manual and took well over two hours. Developers were hesitant to release frequently and small bug fixes piled up for weeks.

*Task:* I was asked to build an automated pipeline that would let the team deploy safely multiple times a day without manual steps or deployment anxiety.

*Action:* I set up a pipeline using GitLab CI with separate stages for unit tests, integration tests, Docker image builds, and deployment to staging. I introduced feature flags to separate deployment from release, so code could reach production without being visible to customers immediately. I also added automated rollback triggers that fired if post-deploy health checks failed within a set window.

*Result:* Deploy frequency increased substantially. The team reported far less anxiety around releases and we caught two critical bugs in staging before they reached hotel clients.

---

Q: Tell us about an incident you handled.

*Situation:* At my last job, our SaaS API started returning errors for a subset of customers on a Tuesday evening. No code had been deployed that day, so the cause was not immediately obvious.

*Task:* I was on-call and needed to identify the root cause quickly and restore service with minimal disruption.

*Action:* I pulled logs from our centralised logging system and spotted a spike in database connection timeouts from one region. I found that a long-running report query was holding locks and causing connection pool exhaustion. I killed the query, scaled up the read replicas temporarily, and added a query timeout rule to prevent recurrence.

*Result:* Service was restored within about 30 minutes. I added the long-running query pattern to our alerting rules and scheduled the heavy report to run in an off-peak window going forward.

---

Q: How would you handle database migrations in a live SaaS environment without downtime?

*Situation:* We needed to add a new column to a core bookings table with millions of rows while the platform stayed live for hotel clients around the world.

*Task:* My job was to design a migration strategy that would not lock the table or cause any visible slowdown for hotel staff mid-shift.

*Action:* I used a three-phase approach. First, add the column as nullable with no default so the ALTER TABLE completed almost instantly. Second, backfill the data in small batches using a background job that ran during low-traffic periods. Third, once the backfill was complete, update application logic to write to the new column and make it non-nullable in a later release.

*Result:* Zero downtime, no client complaints, and the migration completed over two days of controlled batched backfills.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for experience questions. Most DevOps questions at Hotelogix begin with 'Tell us about a time...' or 'Walk us through...'. Use Situation (one sentence of context), Task (what you were responsible for), Action (the specific steps you personally took), and Result (measurable outcome or key learning). Keep each section brief. Interviewers lose interest quickly during long Situation sections, so get to the Action fast.

Diagnose-then-design for system design questions. When asked 'How would you design X?', resist jumping to tools immediately. Start with two or three clarifying questions about scale, traffic patterns, failure tolerance, and budget constraints. State your assumptions clearly before proceeding. Then present your design in layers: compute, storage, networking, monitoring. Always tie the design back to the hospitality context: always-on availability, seasonal booking spikes, multi-tenant data isolation.

Show trade-offs for tool questions. If asked why you chose Terraform over Pulumi, or Prometheus over Datadog, do not just praise your choice. Briefly acknowledge what the alternative does well, then explain why your choice fit the specific constraint you faced. This signals engineering maturity rather than tool loyalty.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Reliability mindset first. Hotelogix runs an always-on SaaS platform that hotel front desks depend on at every hour of the day. Interviewers want to see that you instinctively think about availability, rollback plans, and incident response rather than treating these as edge cases.

Comfort with multi-tenant SaaS operations. Hotel clients share infrastructure but must never see each other's data or be affected by each other's issues. Questions around tenant isolation, safe deploys that do not affect all tenants simultaneously, and live database migration strategies probe whether you have operated this kind of system before.

Cost awareness. Hotelogix serves a price-sensitive hospitality market, so cloud cost optimisation is a genuine engineering priority. Candidates who can speak to right-sizing, spot instance strategies, or database tier choices tend to stand out.

Collaboration across teams. Candidates report that Hotelogix values engineers who communicate clearly during incidents, write useful post-mortems, and can work effectively with non-technical hotel-client support staff.

Domain curiosity. You do not need prior hospitality experience. Showing that you spent time learning how property management systems work, what peak traffic looks like for a hotel, or what SLA commitments matter to hotel operators signals genuine interest and preparation.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week one: product and domain research. Spend time on the Hotelogix website and any available demo videos to understand what the platform does. Note the core modules (front desk, reservations, billing, channel manager) and think about what each one demands from an infrastructure perspective. Reading a few reviews from hotel operators helps you understand real client pain points.

Week one: brush up on core tools. Review the CI/CD tool you know best and be ready to draw a pipeline on a shared screen. Revise Kubernetes concepts: pods, deployments, services, ingress, resource limits, and rolling updates. If you have used Terraform, review state management and module structure so you can speak to it confidently.

Week two: system design practice. Work through one system design problem per day focused on SaaS reliability. Examples: 'Design a deployment pipeline with zero-downtime rollback', 'Design monitoring for a multi-tenant application', 'Design a database migration strategy for a live system'. Time yourself to around 30 minutes per problem and practise articulating trade-offs out loud.

Week two: behavioural prep. Write out three to four STAR stories covering an incident you resolved, a pipeline or automation you built, a time you improved reliability or reduced costs, and a cross-functional challenge you navigated. Practise saying them aloud until they flow naturally and stay concise.

Day before the interview. Check Glassdoor and community forums for recent Hotelogix interview experiences. Review your resume and be ready to walk through any project listed on it in detail. Set up your environment for a possible live coding or troubleshooting exercise.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Giving generic DevOps answers without tying them to the SaaS and hospitality context. If asked about monitoring, do not just say 'I use Prometheus and Grafana.' Add context: 'For a hotel SaaS, I would track booking API latency, reservation confirmation rate, and channel manager sync failures because those are the metrics that directly affect hotel revenue.'

Skipping rollback and failure planning in system design. Candidates who design only the happy path raise flags with interviewers. Always mention: what happens if this deploy fails, how do you detect it quickly, and how do you roll back without disrupting active hotel sessions.

Over-claiming team achievements as personal work. Use 'I' when describing what you specifically did. Interviewers probe further when they suspect a candidate is attributing the team's work to themselves, and it erodes trust quickly.

Not asking clarifying questions during system design. Jumping straight to an architecture without asking about scale, SLA requirements, or budget constraints reads as junior. Two or three well-chosen clarifying questions signal engineering maturity and are expected at mid-to-senior level.

Ignoring seasonal traffic patterns. Hotelogix clients face predictable spikes around holidays, long weekends, and festivals. Failing to mention this context when discussing auto-scaling or capacity planning is a missed opportunity to show you understand the business.

No. The technical bar is about infrastructure, CI/CD, and reliability skills rather than hospitality domain knowledge.
