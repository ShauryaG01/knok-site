02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions reflect what candidates typically encounter at sustainability-focused product companies. Expect a mix of product strategy, execution, and domain curiosity.

  1. How would you define and measure the core value proposition of a sustainability reporting platform for enterprise customers?
  2. Sustainiam's paying customers are enterprises, but the end beneficiary is the environment. How do you balance these competing stakeholder interests when making product decisions?
  3. Walk us through how you would prioritise features for a carbon tracking product when engineering capacity is limited and a regulatory deadline is approaching.
  4. How would you design a success metric framework that shows real-world environmental impact rather than just software adoption?
  5. Describe a time you launched a product or feature into a market that was not yet mature. What did you learn?
  6. A large enterprise client wants a custom feature that only they will use. How do you handle this without derailing the roadmap?
  7. How do you stay current with sustainability regulations such as BRSR in India or CSRD in Europe, and translate them into product requirements?
  8. How would you approach pricing strategy for a sustainability SaaS product targeting Indian enterprises?
  9. Tell me about a time you had to shut down a project or kill a feature midway. How did you make that call and communicate it?
  10. How would you conduct user research for a product where the primary users are sustainability officers who are still defining their own workflows?
  11. If Sustainiam wanted to expand into a new customer segment, how would you decide which one to prioritise first?
  12. How do you keep a cross-functional team aligned when product goals and engineering constraints are in direct tension?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format for every behavioural question. These three examples show how to frame your answers for a sustainability-focused PM interview.

Q: Tell me about a time you launched a product into a market that was not yet mature.

*Situation:* I was PM for a B2B analytics tool at a mid-size software company. We were entering a segment where potential customers understood the problem in theory but had no standard way of solving it yet.

*Task:* My goal was to sign our first set of paying customers within two quarters and gather enough learning to shape the next product iteration.

*Action:* I ran structured discovery interviews with a group of potential buyers, focusing on their current workarounds rather than asking what features they wanted. I worked with engineering to ship a thin slice of the product covering just one core workflow, and paired it with hands-on onboarding for early customers. I also set up a fast internal feedback loop so that every support ticket came back to the product team quickly.

*Result:* We closed several paying customers in the first quarter. Two of those customers became reference accounts used in later sales conversations, and our discovery insights led us to reprioritise two items on the roadmap that were not actually solving the real pain.

---

Q: A large enterprise client wants a feature only they will use. How do you handle this?

*Situation:* At a previous company, our largest account asked for a custom reporting module tied to their internal data taxonomy.

*Task:* I had to decide whether to build it, push back, or find a middle path without risking the relationship or the roadmap.

*Action:* I set up a call with the client's product champion and their procurement lead to understand the underlying need. I found that what they really needed was their data in a specific export format, not a custom UI. I proposed a configurable export feature that would serve them and several other accounts already in our pipeline. I scoped it with engineering and took it to our roadmap review with a business case showing revenue potential across multiple accounts.

*Result:* The configurable export shipped within a few weeks. The original client adopted it, and two pipeline accounts cited it as a factor in their decision to sign. The roadmap stayed intact and we avoided building a dead-end feature.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you had to shut down a project.

*Situation:* Several months into building a marketplace feature, usage data showed that neither buyers nor sellers were finding consistent value. The team had invested significant effort and was emotionally attached to the work.

*Task:* I needed to make a recommendation to leadership on whether to continue, pivot, or sunset the feature, and then communicate the outcome to the team.

*Action:* I pulled together a data review covering activation rates, retention, and qualitative feedback from user interviews. I benchmarked our numbers against industry surveys on similar two-sided marketplace features. The picture was clear. I prepared a one-page summary and made the case for sunsetting the feature, with a proposal to redirect the team to a higher-priority initiative. I was direct with the team about what the data showed and why I believed the decision was right for users.

*Result:* The feature was shut down. The team moved to the next initiative and shipped it ahead of schedule because focus was no longer split. Being transparent about the reasoning raised team trust rather than lowering morale.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions: Structure every story as Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task brief (two or three sentences together) and spend most of your time on Action and Result. The result must show an outcome, not just an output.

CIRCLES for product design questions: When asked to design a product or feature, work through: Comprehend the situation, Identify the customer, Report customer needs, Cut through prioritisation, List solutions, Evaluate trade-offs, Summarise. This prevents the common mistake of jumping to features before defining the user.

North Star Metric framing: For any question about measuring success, name one north star metric first, then supporting metrics, then guardrail metrics. For a sustainability product, the north star might be verified emissions reductions tracked through the platform, with supporting metrics like report completion rates and data freshness.

Stakeholder map for cross-functional questions: When asked about alignment or conflict, briefly map out who is affected, what each party cares about, and where the tension lives. This shows structured thinking before you give your answer.

The 'So What' test: Before finishing any answer, check that your result actually mattered. 'We shipped the feature' is weak. 'The compliance risk was removed and customer retention improved' is strong. Sustainiam interviewers will care about ground-level impact, not just delivery.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Sustainability context awareness: Interviewers will probe whether you understand the ESG and climate reporting landscape. You do not need to be a domain expert, but you should know enough about BRSR or CSRD to discuss how regulations shape product requirements. Candidates who have done this reading before the interview stand out clearly.

Customer empathy at two levels: Sustainiam's customers are enterprises, but the ultimate stakeholder is the environment. Candidates who can hold both in mind and articulate how product decisions serve both tend to make a strong impression.

Structured problem-solving: Open-ended questions have no single right answer. Interviewers are watching how you frame the problem, what assumptions you surface, and how you trade off competing priorities. Speaking your thinking out loud is valued here.

Data-driven instincts: You should be comfortable questioning what a metric actually measures and proposing proxies when direct measurement is hard. For a sustainability product, 'impact' is often harder to measure than 'engagement,' which makes this skill especially relevant to the role.

Communication clarity: PM roles at growth-stage companies require you to explain product decisions to engineers, sales teams, and leadership in the same week. Interviewers often judge how clearly you communicate in the interview itself as a proxy for how you will perform in that context.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Domain and company research
Read Sustainiam's public website, blog posts, and any press coverage you can find. Understand what problem they solve, who their customers are, and how they talk about impact. Look up BRSR requirements in India and CSRD in Europe, as these regulations are a key driver of demand for sustainability software.

Week 2: Product case practice
Pick two or three sustainability products you are familiar with and practise a product teardown: identify the north star metric, one thing done well, and one clear gap. Then practise a timed product design exercise using the CIRCLES framework with a friend or mentor.

Week 3: Behavioural story bank
Write out several STAR stories from your past covering: a product launch, a prioritisation decision, a stakeholder conflict, a failure or shutdown, a data-driven insight, and a cross-functional win. Practise telling each one in under three minutes.

Week 4: Mock interviews and logistics
Do at least two full mock interviews, one focused on product design and one on behavioural questions. Prepare sharp, specific questions to ask at the end of each round. Confirm with the recruiter how many rounds there are and whether there is a take-home case.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping the sustainability context: Many strong PM candidates walk in without spending time understanding ESG regulations or what a sustainability officer's day actually looks like. This signals to interviewers that you are not genuinely curious about the domain and are treating it like any other PM role.

Jumping to solutions too fast: For product design questions, candidates often start describing features before defining the user, the problem, or the success metric. Pause, ask a clarifying question, and frame the problem before you try to solve it.

Weak results in STAR answers: The most common gap is ending a STAR story with an output ('we shipped it') rather than an outcome ('retention improved' or 'the compliance risk was removed'). If you do not know the exact number, describe the directional impact honestly and say so.

Treating the enterprise client as the only stakeholder: At a company like Sustainiam, the environment is a genuine stakeholder in product decisions. Candidates who only talk about enterprise customer needs without connecting them to the underlying mission tend to miss the mark.

Not asking sharp questions: Generic questions like 'what does the roadmap look like' are forgettable. Sharp questions, such as how the team measures environmental impact versus software adoption, or how PM decisions are made when sales and product disagree, show real curiosity and help you stand out.

Over-claiming metrics: If you cite a result from a past role, be ready to explain exactly how it was measured and what the sample size was. Vague or inflated numbers undermine credibility with interviewers who care deeply about data integrity.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many interview rounds does Sustainiam typically have for PM roles?

Candidates report a process that typically includes a recruiter screen, a product case or take-home assignment, and two or three structured rounds. The exact format can vary by role level and hiring manager. It is worth asking the recruiter after your first call to confirm the number of rounds and what each one is designed to test.

Is a take-home product case common in Sustainiam's PM hiring process?

Candidates report that a product case or take-home exercise is a common part of the PM interview process at sustainability-focused companies, though this is not confirmed for every opening at Sustainiam. Typically these exercises ask you to analyse a product problem, propose a solution, and present your reasoning clearly. Preparing at least one sustainability-specific product case before your first call is a good use of time.

What salary can I expect as a PM at Sustainiam?

Based on knok jobradar data, PM compensation in India ranges from 12-20 LPA at the Associate level to 24-40 LPA for mid-level PMs with 3-6 years of experience, and 40-60 LPA for Senior PMs. Actual offers depend on your specific level, experience, and the company's funding stage. Confirm the band with the recruiter early so there are no surprises at the offer stage.

Do I need a background in sustainability or climate tech to get this PM role?

A sustainability background is helpful but candidates report it is not always a hard requirement, especially for strong PM profiles who can demonstrate fast domain learning. What matters more is showing genuine curiosity about ESG reporting, some baseline knowledge of regulations like BRSR, and the ability to translate those constraints into product decisions. Spending a week reading about the company's problem space before your first call can make a real difference.

How competitive is the PM job market in India right now?

The knok jobradar tracked 2,009 Product Manager openings across India as of July 2026, with the highest concentration in Bangalore at 271 roles and Delhi at 177. Demand is healthy but competition is real. Differentiating yourself through domain knowledge, strong STAR stories, and a structured approach to product design questions will meaningfully improve your chances.

How should I handle a product design question about a sustainability product I have never used?

Start by defining the user and their core job to be done before touching any feature ideas. For sustainability products, the user is often a sustainability manager or CFO trying to meet a compliance deadline or prepare an ESG report. Use the CIRCLES framework to stay structured, and be explicit when you are making an assumption rather than stating it as a fact. Interviewers value structured reasoning over domain expertise in these rounds.
