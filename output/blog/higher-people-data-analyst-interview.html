02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions are compiled from candidate reports and Higher People's analytics-heavy product focus. Specific rounds and sequencing can vary by team.

  1. Walk me through a data project you built from raw data to a final business recommendation.
  2. How do you handle missing or inconsistent records in a large dataset?
  3. Write a SQL query to find duplicate entries in a table and explain how you would remove them.
  4. How would you design a dashboard to track recruiter performance? What metrics would you include and why?
  5. Tell me about a time you found an unexpected insight in data. What did you do with it?
  6. How do you decide which metrics matter most when building a hiring-funnel report?
  7. Describe your experience using Python or R for data cleaning and exploratory analysis.
  8. How would you handle a situation where two senior stakeholders want conflicting versions of a report?
  9. What steps do you take before presenting findings to a non-technical audience?
  10. How do you validate that your analysis is correct before sharing it with the business?
  11. Tell me about a time you delivered analysis with incomplete or ambiguous requirements.
  12. How would you measure whether a new feature in a recruitment platform is having an impact?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use these as starting templates and replace the project details with your own experience.

Q: Walk me through a data project you built end-to-end.

*Situation:* My team had no visibility into why cart abandonment was rising on a mid-size e-commerce platform.

*Task:* I was asked to investigate and present a recommendation to the product lead within a tight deadline.

*Action:* I pulled clickstream data from BigQuery, cleaned it in Python using pandas, and joined it with order data. I built a funnel analysis in Tableau that broke down drop-off by device type and payment method, and found that mobile users were abandoning at the payment step at a notably higher rate than desktop users.

*Result:* The product team shipped a simplified mobile checkout flow. The following quarter's reports showed a meaningful improvement in mobile conversion. I also set up an automated weekly report so the team could track progress without coming back to me each time.

---

Q: How would you handle two stakeholders who want conflicting reports?

*Situation:* The sales head and the finance head at my previous company each wanted a 'revenue' report, but defined revenue differently: one counted contracts signed, the other counted cash collected.

*Task:* Both were senior leaders and I could not simply pick one definition and ignore the other.

*Action:* I arranged a short alignment meeting, put both definitions on the table with concrete examples, and proposed a single report with two clearly labeled columns: 'contracted revenue' and 'collected revenue.' I added a definitions section to the report header so any reader could understand the difference without asking me.

*Result:* Both leaders accepted the unified format. It became the standard revenue report used across the company and significantly reduced back-and-forth on revenue figures.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you worked with incomplete requirements.

*Situation:* A product manager asked me to 'analyse user engagement' for a new feature but gave no specifics on what engagement meant or what decision the analysis would support.

*Task:* I needed to deliver something useful without the risk of spending days building the wrong analysis.

*Action:* I sent a short document back to the PM listing three possible interpretations: daily active users, session length, and feature-specific clicks, each with a one-line note on what decision it would answer. I asked them to confirm which one to proceed with.

*Result:* The PM clarified within a few hours. The analysis was completed in a day rather than the multiple days it would have taken had I guessed wrong and needed to redo the work.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the most reliable structure for behavioural questions. Keep Situation and Task to one or two sentences each. Spend most of your answer on Action (what you specifically did, not what the team did) and Result (a concrete outcome, even a relative one if you cannot share exact figures due to confidentiality).

For technical questions, use a three-step approach: restate the problem in your own words to confirm your understanding, walk through your reasoning step by step, then call out edge cases or trade-offs you considered. Interviewers at data-driven companies like Higher People care more about your reasoning process than whether you produce perfect syntax on the first attempt.

For business-case questions, lead with the metric you would use to define success. Then explain where you would source the data, which stakeholders you would align with, and how you would present or act on the findings. This shows you think like an analyst who drives decisions, not just someone who runs queries.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Candidate reports and Higher People's product focus point to a consistent set of qualities beyond technical skill.

Business curiosity. Interviewers want to see that you ask 'so what?' after every number. Analysts who connect findings to business impact stand out far more than those who can only describe the data.

Ownership over process. Higher People's work moves quickly. They favour candidates who can define their own scope, flag blockers early, and drive a project to completion without needing to be prompted at every step.

Clear communication. A recurring theme in candidate feedback is that interviewers probe how you would explain a complex finding to a recruiter or HR manager who is not a data expert. Practise simplifying your explanations before the interview.

SQL and Python depth. Expect at least one hands-on task. Candidates report SQL window functions, aggregations across multiple joins, and Python data-cleaning tasks using pandas as common areas of focus.

Data-first mindset. Higher People uses analytics in its core product. They want analysts who treat data as a decision-making tool, not just a reporting obligation.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused week of preparation covers most of what you need for a mid-level Data Analyst interview at Higher People.

Days 1 and 2: Technical foundation. Revise SQL window functions (ROW_NUMBER, RANK, LAG, LEAD), multi-table joins, and subqueries. In Python, review pandas for handling nulls, deduplication, groupby aggregations, and merging dataframes.

Days 3 and 4: Business and product thinking. Read about how recruitment platforms measure success. Think through a hiring funnel: applications, screenings, interviews, offers, and joins. Be ready to discuss trade-offs between metrics and explain why some matter more at different stages.

Day 5: Story preparation. Write out three or four project stories in STAR format. Make sure at least one covers catching a data quality issue, one covers working with a non-technical stakeholder, and one covers delivering analysis under time pressure.

Day 6: Mock interview. Do at least one mock round with a friend or an online platform. Pay attention to whether your answers are concise and whether you lead with the outcome rather than the process.

Day 7: Company research. Look at Higher People's product and open roles beyond the one you applied for. Prepare one or two specific observations about how your background connects to what they are building.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping into code before clarifying the question. In SQL or Python tasks, candidates who start coding immediately often solve the wrong problem. Pause, restate the question, confirm your assumptions, and then write.

Vague results in STAR answers. 'The project went well' tells an interviewer nothing. If you cannot share exact figures due to confidentiality, say 'the metric improved noticeably' or 'it saved the team several hours a week.' Relative specificity signals credibility.

Treating every question as purely technical. Higher People values business judgment. Even a SQL question can be a test of whether you understand why a business would want that data. Connect your technical answer to a real use case.

Not asking clarifying questions. Accepting every prompt at face value misses an opportunity to show analytical thinking. A well-placed question such as 'is this for a one-time report or an ongoing dashboard?' signals the kind of thinking they are hiring for.

Underplaying data quality work. Cleaning and validating data is a significant part of the job. Do not frame it as routine prep. Treat your data quality decisions as deliberate choices that protected the integrity of the analysis.

Forgetting to close the loop on stakeholder impact. Analysis that never drives a decision has no value. Always end your project stories by describing how the output was used or acted on.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Higher People Data Analyst interview typically have?

Candidates typically report two to four rounds. The process usually starts with a recruiter screening call, followed by a technical round covering SQL and Python, and then one or two rounds with a hiring manager or business stakeholder. The exact structure can vary by team and level of seniority.

Is there a take-home assignment in the Higher People interview?

Many candidates report receiving a take-home case or a timed online assessment as part of the technical round. These typically involve SQL queries, a short Python data-cleaning task, or a business case where you analyse a small dataset and present findings. Treat the take-home as seriously as any live round, since the quality of your thinking and presentation matters as much as getting the right answer.

What salary can I expect as a Data Analyst at Higher People?

Based on knok jobradar data, Data Analyst roles broadly fall in the 5-10 LPA range for entry-level (0-2 years), 10-18 LPA for mid-level (3-5 years), and 18-30 LPA for senior profiles (6-9 years). Lead roles are commonly cited at 28-45+ LPA. Your specific offer will depend on your skills, prior experience, and the scope of the role.

Does Higher People use puzzle or brainteaser questions?

Candidates do not commonly report puzzle-style questions in recent interview cycles. The focus appears to be on practical SQL and Python tasks, business case analysis, and behavioural questions. That said, some analytics teams use estimation or back-of-the-envelope questions to test structured thinking, so it is worth practising a few of these as well.

How important is Python compared to SQL for this role?

Both come up in candidate reports, with SQL appearing more frequently in technical assessments. Python is typically tested for data cleaning, transformation, and exploratory analysis using pandas. If you are stronger in one than the other, invest your remaining prep time in SQL first, but make sure you can write basic pandas operations confidently.

How quickly does Higher People move after the final round?

Candidate experiences vary, but many report hearing back within one to two weeks of the final round. If you have not received a response within that window, a polite follow-up to your recruiter contact is perfectly acceptable. Higher People currently has 42 open Data Analyst roles, which suggests active hiring and generally faster-moving processes than employers with only a handful of open positions.
