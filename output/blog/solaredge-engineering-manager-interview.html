01 Overview

Overview

SolarEdge Technologies is a global leader in smart energy, known for its DC-optimized inverters, solar power optimizers, energy storage systems, and EV charging solutions. The company has a substantial engineering presence in India and is actively hiring: knok jobradar currently lists 144 open roles at SolarEdge, including Engineering Manager positions across multiple functions.

The EM role at SolarEdge typically sits at the intersection of hardware and software engineering, making interviews more technically diverse than a pure software company. Candidates report that the process includes an initial HR or recruiter screen, one or two technical depth rounds, a people-management and leadership discussion, and often a final cross-functional panel. Rounds are not always named formally, so prepare for any combination of these.

Salary ranges for Engineering Manager roles in India are in the 35-60 LPA band, rising to 55-90 LPA at Senior Manager level and 90-150+ LPA at Director level, based on knok jobradar data. The market for EM talent is active: 975 Engineering Manager openings are tracked across India, with Bangalore leading at 182 openings.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

SolarEdge interviewers focus on leadership depth, technical range, and cross-functional delivery. Candidates report these questions coming up most often:

  1. Tell us about a complex project you led from planning through delivery. What was your role and what was the outcome?
  2. How do you balance staying technically hands-on with leading your team effectively?
  3. SolarEdge builds products that combine hardware and software. How have you managed engineering teams working across these boundaries?
  4. Describe a time your team missed a commitment. What went wrong, and what did you change afterward?
  5. How do you approach building or scaling an engineering team, including hiring and onboarding?
  6. Walk us through how you handle an underperforming engineer on your team.
  7. SolarEdge works across geographies and time zones. How have you led distributed or cross-site teams?
  8. How do you prioritize engineering work when capacity is limited but the roadmap is demanding?
  9. Describe a time you pushed back on a product or business decision. How did you frame your concern and what happened?
  10. How do you build a culture of engineering quality and continuous improvement within your team?
  11. Walk us through how your team handles a major production incident. What is your role in the process?
  12. How do you keep engineers motivated and growing during periods of high pressure or organizational change?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time your team missed a commitment. What went wrong and what did you change?

*Situation:* My team was responsible for delivering a critical API integration for a partner launch. Midway through the quarter, a key engineer left unexpectedly and we also discovered the partner's spec had changed significantly.

*Task:* I needed to reset expectations with stakeholders, protect the team from burnout, and still deliver something meaningful by the original date.

*Action:* I called an immediate scoping session with product and the partner team to agree on a reduced but viable scope for the launch date. I redistributed work across the team, brought in a contractor for two weeks to cover the knowledge gap, and increased the cadence of check-ins to catch blockers early. I also set up a clear post-launch plan for the deferred features so stakeholders knew nothing was dropped permanently.

*Result:* We launched on the original date with a feature-complete core integration. The remaining scope shipped the following cycle. The partner relationship stayed intact, and I documented the process change in our team's risk management playbook.

---

Q: How do you handle an underperforming engineer on your team?

*Situation:* A senior engineer on my team was consistently missing code review timelines and their design documents lacked the depth expected at their level. The rest of the team was quietly compensating.

*Task:* I needed to address this directly while being fair to the engineer and protecting team morale.

*Action:* I set up a private one-on-one and named the specific gaps with examples, without making it personal. I worked with them to identify whether the root cause was a skill gap, unclear expectations, or something personal. It turned out they were struggling with the scope expansion after a recent promotion and felt they could not ask for help. I created a structured growth plan with weekly check-ins, paired them with a senior principal for design reviews, and adjusted their near-term scope to build confidence. I also made clear what the expectations were at their level going forward.

*Result:* Within one quarter their review turnaround improved significantly, their design documents met the bar, and they mentioned in a retrospective that the clarity had reduced their stress. They are now one of the more consistent contributors on the team.

---

Q: How do you prioritize when engineering capacity is limited but the roadmap is ambitious?

*Situation:* My team had just absorbed a new product area after a reorg, doubling our roadmap scope without any increase in headcount.

*Task:* I had to make hard prioritization calls quickly and align stakeholders across two product lines.

*Action:* I ran a joint session with both product managers to map every initiative to customer impact and technical dependency. I introduced a simple scoring framework covering business value, urgency, and effort, rated by the group rather than by me alone. This created shared ownership of the tradeoffs. I also identified three initiatives that could be deferred with minimal customer impact and two that could be handed to a partner team with the right context transfer. I was transparent with engineering leadership about what we were choosing not to do.

*Result:* We delivered the top-priority items on schedule and the deferred work was picked up the next planning cycle. The clarity also reduced mid-quarter interruptions because stakeholders understood the rationale behind the decisions.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For leadership and conflict questions, use the STAR structure (Situation, Task, Action, Result) but spend most of your time on Action. SolarEdge interviewers want to see how you think and decide, not just what happened.

For 'how would you approach your first weeks in this role' type questions, structure your answer in clear phases without locking yourself to rigid timelines. Describe an early listening phase (meeting stakeholders, reading existing docs, understanding team dynamics), a diagnosis phase (identifying gaps, quick wins, and longer-term bets), and an execution phase (delivering early results while building toward larger goals). Be honest that the exact pacing depends on what you find on the ground.

For technical depth questions, avoid going too deep into implementation details. Your job is to show you can ask the right questions, identify risk, and make sound architectural tradeoffs. Use phrases like 'I would want to understand the constraints before deciding' to signal engineering judgment.

For cross-functional and prioritization questions, show that you involve stakeholders in the tradeoff rather than handing them a decision. Frameworks like impact-vs-effort or customer-impact-vs-technical-risk work well. What matters is that you make the reasoning visible.

For culture and team-building questions, be specific. Name practices you have actually used: structured onboarding, design review processes, blameless post-mortems, career ladders, or regular skip-level meetings. Generic answers about 'psychological safety' without concrete examples do not land well.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates report from SolarEdge EM interviews, here is what the panel is typically looking for:

Technical credibility without micromanagement. SolarEdge builds complex systems that mix embedded software, cloud platforms, and hardware. They want an EM who can have a real technical conversation with engineers, spot bad decisions early, and earn team respect, but who does not block progress by hovering over implementation details.

Ownership and accountability. SolarEdge is known for a high-ownership culture. Interviewers look for managers who say 'I decided' or 'I missed this,' not those who deflect to process or circumstance.

Cross-functional fluency. EM candidates are expected to work closely with product, hardware, QA, and sometimes customer-facing teams. Interviewers want to see that you communicate well outside your engineering bubble and can represent both technical constraints and business needs in the same conversation.

Scalability thinking. SolarEdge is growing rapidly. They are interested in managers who have hired, onboarded, and developed engineers at scale, not just managed steady-state teams.

Comfort with ambiguity. Projects at SolarEdge often span geographies and involve unclear specs or shifting hardware constraints. Candidates who demonstrate structured thinking under uncertainty score well.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Research SolarEdge deeply before any round. Read their investor materials, product announcements, and any public engineering content you can find. Understand their core product lines: optimizers, inverters, energy storage, and EV charging. Knowing that they operate in a regulated, safety-critical domain helps you frame your experience in terms they care about.

Map your experience to their context. SolarEdge is not a pure software company. If you have experience managing teams that work close to hardware, embedded systems, or safety-critical software, make that visible early. If you do not, prepare to explain how you would learn and adapt quickly.

Prepare a portfolio of leadership stories. Have at least six strong STAR stories ready covering: a delivery miss and recovery, a conflict with a peer or product manager, a hiring or team-building win, a technical decision you influenced as a manager, a performance management situation, and a time you drove a culture or process change.

Practice live with a peer. SolarEdge rounds are typically conversational. Practicing out loud is more effective than writing answers. Focus on keeping answers to two to three minutes, then pausing for follow-up questions.

Prepare smart questions for each round. Ask about the current engineering challenges in the team you would own, the biggest cross-functional friction points, and how success is measured for a new EM in the first few months. These questions signal that you are already thinking like an owner.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Staying too technical. Many candidates who come from strong individual-contributor backgrounds spend too much time in code-level details. Interviewers want to see that you have made the mindset shift from 'best engineer in the room' to 'engineer who multiplies the team.'

Vague answers about people management. Saying you 'prioritize psychological safety' or 'believe in servant leadership' without a concrete example is a red flag. Every claim needs a specific story behind it.

Not acknowledging failure. SolarEdge interviewers, like most strong engineering cultures, are skeptical of managers who only share wins. If you cannot describe a real miss with honesty and a clear lesson, your credibility drops.

Underestimating the hardware-software context. Candidates who treat this like a pure software EM interview and ignore the product domain often struggle in the technical discussion. Even if your background is cloud or mobile, show genuine curiosity about the SolarEdge product.

Over-indexing on process. Describing elaborate frameworks and rituals without connecting them to outcomes suggests you manage process rather than results. Always tie your process choices to a measurable outcome.

Not having questions ready. Arriving with no thoughtful questions reads as low interest. In a competitive role, engagement matters.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the SolarEdge Engineering Manager interview typically have?

Candidates report a process that typically includes an initial HR or recruiter screen, at least one technical discussion, a dedicated people-management round, and a final panel or leadership review. The exact number of rounds can vary by team and level, so confirm the structure with your recruiter after the first call. Typically the full process spans a few weeks from first screen to offer.

What salary should I expect for an Engineering Manager role at SolarEdge India?

Based on knok jobradar data, Engineering Manager roles in India are in the 35-60 LPA range, Senior Manager roles in the 55-90 LPA range, and Director-level positions at 90-150+ LPA. Exact compensation at SolarEdge depends on your level, experience, and negotiation. It is worth checking publicly reported figures on Glassdoor or levels.fyi for additional data points specific to SolarEdge before your negotiation.

Does SolarEdge expect Engineering Managers to still write code?

Candidates report that SolarEdge values technical depth in its engineering managers and may ask you to review code, weigh in on architecture, or work through a design problem during the interview. That said, the expectation is typically that you are a technical leader and decision-maker, not a primary contributor. Showing that you earn engineer respect through sound judgment rather than hands-on output is the right signal to send.

How important is hardware or energy-domain experience for this role?

SolarEdge builds products that span embedded firmware, power electronics control, cloud monitoring, and consumer apps, so domain exposure helps. Candidates without hardware backgrounds have reported success by demonstrating genuine curiosity about the product and showing strong fundamentals in system design and cross-functional collaboration. Preparing a few questions about the hardware-software interface in your target team signals interest and helps offset a gap.

How should I prepare for the cross-functional or leadership panel round?

Candidates report that cross-functional panels at companies like SolarEdge often include a product manager, a peer EM, or a senior engineering leader. Prepare stories that show you can navigate disagreement with product, represent engineering constraints to non-technical stakeholders, and build alignment without formal authority. Practice keeping your answers concise, since panels typically have less time per question than a one-on-one round.

Is it worth applying to multiple SolarEdge roles at once?

With 144 open roles currently at SolarEdge across functions and levels, there may be more than one EM-adjacent position that fits your background. Applying to two or three well-matched roles is reasonable, but tailor each application to the specific team context rather than sending a generic resume. If you progress with multiple teams simultaneously, be transparent with your recruiter so they can coordinate internally.
