02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates at abra report a mix of leadership, people management, and technical judgment questions. Here are the questions that come up most often:

  1. Walk me through how you handled a low-performing engineer. What steps did you take and what was the outcome?
  2. How do you stay technically relevant while managing a team full-time? Where do you draw the line between coding and managing?
  3. Describe a time you pushed back on a product deadline. How did you frame that conversation with stakeholders?
  4. How do you approach building a team from scratch, from sourcing to onboarding to setting culture?
  5. Tell me about a cross-functional conflict between your engineers and another team. How did you resolve it?
  6. How do you connect engineering work to business outcomes and communicate that upward to leadership?
  7. Walk me through how you prioritise technical debt alongside feature work.
  8. How do you create an environment where engineers raise problems early instead of hiding them?
  9. Tell me about a major production incident you owned as a manager. What was your specific role?
  10. How do you support engineers at different career stages, from junior hires to senior engineers pushing for promotion?
  11. Describe a time you delivered difficult feedback upward, to your own manager or to senior leadership.
  12. How do you make build-vs-buy decisions when your team is under time pressure?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk me through how you handled a low-performing engineer.

*Situation:* I had a mid-level engineer who was consistently missing sprint commitments and not communicating blockers. The team was picking up the slack silently, which was starting to affect morale.

*Task:* My job was to address the performance gap without losing someone who had strong domain knowledge, while also being fair to the rest of the team.

*Action:* I started with a direct one-on-one where I named the specific pattern I was observing, not a general 'you need to improve' conversation. We put together a time-bound written plan with clear weekly checkpoints. I also dug into root causes and found the engineer was dealing with an unclear spec process that was causing rework. I fixed that process for the whole team.

*Result:* By week six, delivery improved and the engineer went on to lead a small sub-project. The spec process change also reduced rework across the whole team.

---

Q: Describe a cross-functional conflict between your engineers and another team.

*Situation:* My team owned a shared data pipeline. The data science team was making direct changes to it without coordinating with us, causing two production incidents in one quarter.

*Task:* I needed to stop the incidents without damaging our relationship with a team that was a key partner for our roadmap.

*Action:* I asked both teams to join a working session, not a blame meeting. We mapped out the dependency clearly and agreed on a lightweight change-request process. I also assigned one engineer from each team as a point of contact to review changes together.

*Result:* Zero incidents from that dependency in the following two quarters. The collaboration model became a template that two other teams adopted for similar shared-service situations.

---

Q: Tell me about a major production incident you owned as a manager.

*Situation:* A payment processing service went down during a high-traffic event. Revenue impact was real and the outage lasted nearly two hours.

*Task:* As the engineering manager on call, I had to coordinate the fix, manage communication to leadership and the business team, and make sure my engineers were not burning out under pressure.

*Action:* I created an incident channel immediately, assigned one engineer to the fix and another to monitoring, and gave leadership a brief status update every 20 minutes. I stayed out of the technical fix itself, trusting my team, and focused on clearing blockers and translating technical status into business language.

*Result:* Service was restored within two hours. The post-incident review produced a runbook that cut average resolution time for similar issues in the months that followed.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the baseline for behavioural questions. Keep each component roughly equal in length. Interviewers at this level want to hear your specific judgment calls inside the story, not just the outcome.

Situation-Complication-Resolution works well for conflict or negotiation questions. State what was happening, describe the complication that raised the stakes, then explain your resolution path.

Problem-Approach-Learning fits questions about failure or technical debt. Abra interviewers, candidates report, tend to probe what you learned and what you changed afterward, not just what went wrong.

For technical judgment questions (build-vs-buy, architectural trade-offs), use a structured 'here are the factors I weigh' format. Name the trade-offs explicitly: cost, time-to-market, maintenance burden, team skill fit. Avoid picking a side before walking through the reasoning.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Engineering Manager interviews at abra, based on candidate reports, focus on three things more than anything else.

People leadership depth. Can you show a clear, repeatable approach to performance management, career development, and team health? Vague answers like 'I have regular one-on-ones' are not enough. They want to hear the specific system you use and what you do when it stops working.

Cross-functional influence without authority. Abra has multiple product, data, and platform teams working in parallel. Interviewers want evidence that you can align stakeholders, negotiate trade-offs, and deliver outcomes without relying on hierarchy.

Technical credibility. You are not expected to be the best coder in the room, but you need to show you can engage meaningfully in system design discussions, ask the right questions during architectural reviews, and make sound build-vs-buy or prioritisation calls. Candidates who cannot speak to technical trade-offs at a senior level typically do not clear this bar.

Cultural fit at abra reportedly leans toward managers who take ownership broadly, communicate clearly under pressure, and involve the team in decisions without losing personal accountability.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Stories and structure

Write out eight to ten leadership stories using the STAR format. Cover: low performer, conflict, failed project, successful delivery under pressure, technical trade-off, and upward feedback. Having these written down means you are not constructing them under interview pressure.

Week 2: Technical and product judgment

Review system design concepts at the EM level: how you evaluate architecture proposals, where you push back, and how you communicate trade-offs to non-technical stakeholders. Read any publicly available engineering writing or talks from abra's engineering team to understand how they think about scale and ownership.

Week 3: Research and mock rounds

Review abra's Engineering Manager job descriptions carefully. Note the language they use around ownership, cross-functional work, and technical bar. Do at least two mock interviews with a peer or coach, ideally someone who has been through a similar process. Candidates report that having a trusted person reflect your answers back makes a significant difference in how you refine your delivery.

Before each round: Prepare two to three questions for the interviewer that show you have thought about their team's specific challenges, not generic questions every candidate asks.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Leading with process instead of judgment. Saying 'I use weekly one-on-ones and OKRs' is not an answer. Interviewers want to know what you decided inside that process and why.

Avoiding ownership in failure stories. Candidates sometimes frame failures as team or external problems. Interviewers are looking for what you personally would do differently. Take clear ownership.

Underselling scope. Indian candidates, particularly those transitioning from IC roles, often describe their impact conservatively. Be specific about team size, delivery complexity, and business outcomes without understating what you drove.

Ignoring the technical bar. Some candidates prepare almost entirely on people management and get caught out on technical depth questions. Abra, candidates report, expects EMs to engage meaningfully with architectural and prioritisation discussions.

Not asking good questions. Interviews are two-way. Candidates who ask sharp, specific questions about the team's current challenges, tech stack decisions, or roadmap signal genuine interest and seniority.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the abra Engineering Manager interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically involves four to six rounds. These commonly cover an introductory call, leadership and people management scenarios, technical depth, a cross-functional or stakeholder round, and a final panel. Round structures vary by team and hiring manager, so treat any description as a general guide rather than a guarantee.

What salary can I expect as an Engineering Manager at abra?

Based on the salary bands tracked in India, Manager-level roles typically fall in the 35-60 LPA range, Senior Manager in the 55-90 LPA range, and Director-level roles at 90-150+ LPA. Actual offers depend on your experience, the team scope, and your negotiation. It is worth benchmarking your specific level against publicly reported data on Glassdoor or levels.fyi before you enter compensation discussions.

Does abra test coding during the Engineering Manager interview?

Candidates report that abra does not typically ask EMs to write code in interviews. However, you are expected to demonstrate technical credibility through system design discussions, architectural trade-off reasoning, and meaningful engagement with your engineers on technical decisions. Being completely hands-off on technical topics is unlikely to clear the bar.

How do I prepare for abra's people management questions?

Write out specific stories for the scenarios that come up most: low performer, team conflict, missed deadline, difficult upward conversation, and hiring decision. Use the STAR format and make sure your answer names what you personally decided, not just what the team did. Vague answers about process without specific judgment calls tend not to land well in these interviews.

Is Bangalore the best city for Engineering Manager roles at abra?

Bangalore has the most Engineering Manager openings in India overall, with 182 of the 975 tracked roles based in the city. Delhi (53 openings) and Pune and Chennai (20 each) also have active hiring. Abra specifically has 128 open roles across locations, so it is worth checking current listings to see which cities are active for their teams at the time you apply.

What is the difference between an Engineering Manager and a Senior Engineering Manager interview at abra?

Candidates report that Senior Manager interviews place greater weight on org-level thinking, managing other managers, and influencing strategy across multiple teams or products. The people management and technical depth components are similar, but the scope of your examples is expected to be larger. If you are applying for a Senior Manager role, prepare stories that reflect cross-team ownership and business-level impact rather than single-team delivery.
