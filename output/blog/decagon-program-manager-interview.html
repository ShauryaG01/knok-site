01 Overview

Overview

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates who have interviewed at Decagon for TPM roles typically report questions across three broad themes: technical depth, cross-functional leadership, and handling ambiguity in AI-product environments.

  1. Tell me about a complex, multi-team program you owned end-to-end. How did you structure it and what did delivery look like?
  2. Decagon builds AI agents for enterprise clients. How would you manage a program where the core technology (an LLM feature) is still evolving while a client deadline is fixed?
  3. Describe a time you had to push back on a product or engineering decision. How did you handle the disagreement and what happened?
  4. How do you track program health across multiple teams? Walk me through the tools, rituals, and signals you use.
  5. Tell me about a time a dependency you did not own caused your program to slip. What did you do?
  6. How do you communicate bad news (a delay, a scope cut) to a senior executive or a key customer?
  7. Describe a situation where you had to make a decision with incomplete information. What was your process?
  8. Decagon's customers are enterprises with strict SLAs. How have you managed external stakeholder expectations during an unstable release cycle?
  9. Walk me through how you would set up a brand-new program from scratch, from kick-off to first milestone.
  10. Tell me about a time you improved a process that was slowing the team down. What was the before-and-after?
  11. How do you balance velocity (shipping fast) against quality in a high-stakes product environment?
  12. What metrics do you use to measure the health and success of a program, and how do you adapt them when the product is still finding product-market fit?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for every behavioral question. Keep your answers focused on your role and your decisions, not what the team did collectively.

Q: Tell me about a complex, multi-team program you owned end-to-end.

*Situation:* My company was integrating a third-party payments platform into our core product. Three engineering teams, a security review, legal, and two external vendors were all involved.

*Task:* I was the TPM responsible for bringing this to launch without delaying a partner go-live date that had already been communicated externally.

*Action:* I mapped every dependency into a single source of truth, ran a weekly cross-functional sync with a rotating 'blocker spotlight,' and set up a separate async channel with the vendor for rapid issue triage. When the security review flagged a design concern, I organized a focused working session to resolve it rather than letting it become a standing blocker.

*Result:* The integration launched on the original date. Post-launch, the team adopted the dependency-mapping template I had built for future programs.

---

Q: Describe a time you had to push back on a product decision.

*Situation:* The product team wanted to add three new features to an already-scoped release that was close to code freeze.

*Task:* My job was to protect the release timeline while keeping the product team feeling heard and aligned.

*Action:* I ran a quick impact analysis showing which items had the highest risk of destabilizing existing features, then facilitated a prioritization session with engineering leads and the product manager. I proposed descoping two of the three additions into the next sprint, with a concrete plan for how they would land there.

*Result:* Leadership approved the descoping. The release shipped on schedule, and both features landed in the following sprint as planned, which gave them extra polish time.

---

Q: Tell me about a time a dependency you did not own caused your program to slip.

*Situation:* An infrastructure team responsible for provisioning our staging environment fell behind due to an unplanned incident on their side. This blocked two engineering squads on my program.

*Task:* I needed to minimize the impact on our milestone without taking over work that was not mine to own.

*Action:* I escalated transparently to both program sponsors as soon as I confirmed the slip, proposed a parallel-track plan where teams worked on non-blocked items, and set a daily check-in with the infra lead to track recovery progress. I also revised the program timeline with a clear explanation of root cause so stakeholders were not surprised.

*Result:* The program slipped by a few days rather than the full week the initial incident suggested. Stakeholders appreciated the early heads-up and the revised plan.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

The STAR method is the baseline for behavioral questions, but strong TPM candidates at companies like Decagon typically layer in additional structure.

For 'how do you manage X' questions, use a three-part answer: the system you set up (rituals, tools, artifacts), how you detect problems early, and how you escalate or resolve them. This shows operational maturity, not just intent.

For ambiguity questions, use a 'clarify, constrain, commit' frame. Show that you ask the right questions first, then define the smallest decision that unblocks the team, then communicate clearly. Avoid answers that suggest you wait for ambiguity to resolve itself.

For stakeholder conflict questions, lead with the business context (why both sides had a valid point), then walk through how you built shared understanding before proposing a resolution. Interviewers want to see that you do not simply enforce your own view.

For metrics questions, distinguish between leading indicators (things that predict a slip before it happens) and lagging indicators (things that confirm one after it has happened). Decagon's enterprise context means you should mention external-facing SLAs or customer commitments as a dimension of program health, not just internal velocity.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Candidates who have progressed at Decagon report that interviewers are looking for a specific combination of qualities.

Comfort with AI-product ambiguity. Decagon's core product is built on LLM technology that changes rapidly. Interviewers want to see that you can hold a program together when the underlying technology is still shifting, not just when the scope is stable.

Enterprise empathy. Decagon serves large enterprise clients who have strict SLAs and procurement processes. TPMs who can speak to managing external stakeholder expectations, not just internal ones, tend to stand out.

Structured thinking under pressure. Interviewers typically ask follow-up questions to stress-test your answers. They want to see how you think when pushed, not just your prepared narrative.

Influence without authority. As a TPM, you rarely control the engineers or PMs you work with. Interviewers look for examples where you moved a team forward through clarity, credibility, and good process, not positional power.

Bias toward action. Decagon is a fast-moving startup. Candidates who demonstrate a habit of making reversible decisions quickly and course-correcting tend to resonate more than those who optimize for perfect information before moving.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Start with the product. Read everything publicly available about Decagon, including their product, customer case studies, and any engineering blog posts or founder interviews. Understand what 'autonomous customer support' means technically so you can speak the product language.

Build your story bank. Prepare real examples that cover: a program you owned end-to-end, a time you managed a major dependency risk, a stakeholder conflict you resolved, a process you improved, and a time you delivered bad news well. Map each to the STAR format before your interview.

Practice metrics fluency. Decagon's enterprise focus means program health metrics matter. Be ready to discuss leading vs. lagging indicators, how you would track a rollout to an enterprise client, and how you would report status to a CTO-level audience.

Prepare your own questions. Ask interviewers how the TPM role interfaces with engineering leadership, what a successful first few months looks like, and how program reviews are structured. Good questions signal genuine interest and help you assess fit.

Mock interviews. Practice your STAR answers out loud with a peer or in front of a camera. TPM interviews are conversational, and polished delivery matters more than most candidates expect.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Staying too high-level. TPM interviews reward specificity. Saying 'I managed stakeholders well' without a concrete example, a clear action you personally took, and a measurable result will not land.

Confusing PM and TPM scope. Decagon is hiring a TPM, not a product manager. Keep your answers focused on delivery, dependencies, timelines, and cross-team coordination, not on product strategy or feature prioritization (unless asked).

Skipping the result. Every STAR answer needs a result. If you cannot describe what changed because of your actions, the story feels incomplete. Even soft outcomes ('the team adopted this process going forward') count.

Over-crediting the team. Collaboration is important, but interviewers want to understand your specific contribution. Use 'I' alongside 'we,' and be clear about what decisions you made personally.

Not asking about the product. Candidates who ask no questions about Decagon's product, customers, or technical environment signal low interest. Enterprise AI is a specific context; showing curiosity about it signals you will ramp up quickly.

Treating every question as a crisis story. Not every example needs to be a dramatic rescue. Interviewers also value stories that show disciplined process and early risk management, situations where things went smoothly because you set them up correctly, not just situations where you saved a failing program.
