01 Overview

Overview

IATA (International Air Transport Association) is the global trade body for the airline industry. Its product roles sit at the crossroads of aviation standards, enterprise B2B software, and global regulatory frameworks. For Indian PMs, this is a rare opportunity to work on products used by airlines, airports, travel agencies, and governments worldwide.

Candidates report a process that typically spans two to four rounds, mixing product sense questions with aviation domain knowledge checks. With 38 open roles currently tracked at IATA, demand is active. The candidate pool tends to be smaller than at consumer tech companies, because interviewers look for both PM fundamentals and familiarity with aviation systems like NDC (New Distribution Capability), IATA ONE Order, or BSP settlement.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

IATA interviews blend standard PM questions with aviation-specific scenarios. Candidates report the following coming up frequently:

  1. How would you define the product vision for IATA's NDC platform over the next three years?
  2. Walk through how you would prioritize features for an airline billing and settlement system used by carriers with competing needs.
  3. A large member airline refuses to adopt a new IATA digital standard you are shipping. How do you handle this?
  4. How do you balance the needs of airlines (the paying members) against end passengers (the actual users of the product)?
  5. Describe a time you shipped a product into a highly regulated or compliance-driven market. What was different about that process?
  6. How would you define and measure success for a new digital identity initiative targeting airport check-in?
  7. A legacy data format baked into most airline systems is blocking your roadmap. What is your approach to moving forward?
  8. How do you build a product roadmap when your customers are also your governance board and can vote on your priorities?
  9. Tell me about a time you influenced a cross-functional team or stakeholder who did not report to you.
  10. IATA serves carriers of very different sizes and geographies. How would you segment them to decide whose feedback shapes the roadmap first?
  11. How would you approach a go-to-market plan for a new airline sustainability reporting tool?
  12. How do you think about API products and developer experience when your end users are airline IT teams rather than consumers?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for behavioral questions. Three examples tailored to the kind of experience IATA looks for:

Q: Describe a time you shipped a product in a regulated environment.

*Situation:* At my previous company, we were building an e-invoicing module for logistics firms that had to comply with GST audit trail requirements.

*Task:* I had to ship a compliant feature without delaying the broader roadmap by more than one quarter.

*Action:* I brought the legal and finance teams into sprint planning early, mapped every compliance requirement to a specific user story, and created a 'compliance blocker' tag so nothing slipped through. I also ran a dry run with two pilot customers four weeks before launch to catch edge cases.

*Result:* We launched on time with zero compliance flags in the first audit cycle. The pilot approach became a standard practice for the team.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you handled a stakeholder who pushed back hard on your roadmap.

*Situation:* A senior sales leader wanted a feature that would benefit one large enterprise client but would pull engineers off a platform initiative for six weeks.

*Task:* My job was to either find a way to serve both needs or make a clear, data-backed case for the tradeoff.

*Action:* I ran a quick impact-effort scoring exercise, pulled retention and revenue data for the enterprise client versus the platform cohort, and presented both scenarios to the VP of Product and the sales leader together. I also proposed a lighter version of the feature that could ship in two weeks.

*Result:* The lighter version was accepted. The enterprise client was satisfied, the platform work stayed on track, and the sales leader became a stronger advocate for the roadmap process after seeing the tradeoff data.

---

Q: How have you worked with a product that had both B2B buyers and end users who were different people?

*Situation:* I managed a fleet tracking product sold to logistics companies (the buyer) but used daily by truck drivers and dispatchers (the actual users).

*Task:* Adoption was low because the UX was built for the buyer's reporting needs, not for the dispatcher's daily workflow.

*Action:* I ran contextual interviews with dispatchers at three client sites, mapped their actual workflow, and identified friction points that never appeared in support tickets. I then reframed the next quarter roadmap around dispatcher efficiency metrics, not just buyer-requested reports.

*Result:* Daily active usage climbed meaningfully within two quarters. Industry surveys suggest DAU growth in B2B SaaS is a strong leading indicator of renewal, and the buying team cited the usage improvement directly in the renewal conversation.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

A few frameworks that fit IATA-style PM interviews well:

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result): Use this for every behavioral question. IATA interviewers typically want concrete examples, not hypotheticals, for experience-based questions.

CIRCLES (or a simplified version): For 'design a product' questions, walk through who the user is, what their goals are, list possible solutions, prioritize, then summarize. Useful when asked about a new IATA digital tool.

RICE scoring (Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort): Good for prioritization questions. IATA products serve large, heterogeneous user bases, so explaining how you weight 'reach' across airlines of different sizes shows mature thinking.

Jobs-to-be-done: When asked about user needs, framing your answer as 'the airline needs to accomplish X workflow, not just use feature Y' tends to land well with IATA interviewers who think in terms of industry-wide processes.

Stakeholder mapping: For questions about standards adoption or cross-functional influence, sketching out who holds decision power, who is affected, and who can block will show the political awareness IATA PM roles require.

For metric questions, name a primary metric, one guardrail metric, and explain what you would NOT optimize for. This shows you understand tradeoffs, which matters in a membership-driven organization where optimizing for one airline group can hurt another.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates report from IATA PM processes, interviewers are typically looking for five things:

Aviation domain awareness. You do not need to be a pilot or an airline veteran. But you should know what NDC, BSP, IATA ONE Order, and IOSA mean, and why airlines care about each. Read IATA's publicly available product pages before your interview.

B2B product thinking. IATA products are used by organizations, not individual consumers. Interviewers want to see that you understand buying committees, implementation cycles, and enterprise adoption friction. Consumer PM instincts alone will not be enough.

Standards and compliance mindset. IATA literally writes industry standards. PMs here have to think about how a product change affects every airline that has built systems around the existing spec. Showing that you have shipped in regulated or standards-heavy environments is a real advantage.

Global stakeholder management. IATA's member airlines span every continent. Interviewers look for evidence that you can work across cultures, time zones, and competing organizational interests without losing momentum.

Data-driven but pragmatic. IATA works with legacy systems that predate modern analytics. Candidates who can talk about working with imperfect data, proxy metrics, and qualitative signals alongside quantitative ones tend to do better than those who only reference A/B tests and dashboards.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A four-week plan for IATA PM interviews:

Week 1: Know the company. Read IATA's publicly available annual report and product pages for NDC, ONE Order, and IATA NEXTT. Understand who the customers are (airlines, GDSs, travel agencies, airports) and what pain points each product solves. Watch any public IATA conference talks on YouTube to hear how insiders describe the problems.

Week 2: Build your STAR story bank. Write out six to eight stories from your own experience covering: shipping in a regulated market, influencing without authority, handling a major stakeholder conflict, prioritizing under resource constraints, and recovering from a product setback. Practice each story out loud until it lands in under three minutes.

Week 3: Practice product sense. Do mock exercises for B2B and enterprise contexts specifically. Practice RICE scoring with examples from your own work. Ask a peer to play a skeptical airline stakeholder and push back on your roadmap decisions.

Week 4: Sharpen and research. Revisit the job description, map each requirement to one of your stories, and close any gaps. Research recent IATA announcements (sustainability commitments, digital transformation news) to show current awareness. Prepare two to three thoughtful questions for your interviewers that reflect genuine curiosity about their product challenges.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Candidates who stumble in IATA PM interviews typically make one of these errors:

Treating it like a consumer PM role. Using examples from apps or consumer products without bridging to B2B or enterprise dynamics signals a mismatch. Reframe your examples to highlight organizational buyers, long implementation cycles, and system-wide implications.

Not knowing IATA's actual products. Saying you would learn more about what IATA does after joining is a red flag. Spend a few hours on IATA's public website before the interview. Mention NDC or BSP naturally in context, not as name-dropping.

Vague answers to stakeholder questions. IATA's PM role is heavily about aligning airlines with competing interests. Generic answers like 'I would get buy-in from stakeholders' without explaining how you actually did it tend to lose points.

Over-indexing on metrics without acknowledging data limits. IATA works with legacy systems and member airlines that do not all have modern data pipelines. Candidates who only talk about real-time dashboards and A/B testing can seem out of touch with the operational reality.

Not asking good questions. IATA's problems are genuinely complex, and interviewers notice when candidates ask shallow questions. Prepare questions about roadmap challenges, how member disagreements on standards get resolved, or how adoption is measured across airline tiers.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the IATA PM interview process typically have?

Candidates report anywhere from two to four rounds, though IATA does not publish a fixed structure. A typical pattern includes an initial HR screen, a hiring manager conversation, and one or two rounds covering product sense and behavioral questions. Some senior roles include a case study or take-home exercise.

Do I need an aviation background to get a PM role at IATA?

A formal aviation background is not required, but domain awareness is. Candidates who can speak to how airlines operate, what NDC or BSP means, and why airline IT teams face specific adoption challenges tend to perform better. You can build this knowledge through IATA's public materials and industry reports in a few days of focused reading.

What salary can I expect as a PM at IATA in India?

knok jobradar data shows Indian PM roles broadly ranging from 12-20 LPA at Associate level up to 55-90+ LPA at Group or Principal PM level. Actual IATA offers depend on the specific role, location, and your experience. For more precise benchmarks, check Glassdoor or levels.fyi for IATA-specific data points reported by current and former employees.

How important is it to know technical details like APIs or data standards?

You do not need to write code, but IATA's products are deeply tied to data standards and integrations. Understanding what an API product means for an airline IT team, or why migrating from one data format to another is a multi-year effort, will make your answers significantly more credible. Aim for technically fluent rather than technically deep.

What is the best way to prepare for a case study round?

Candidates report that IATA case studies typically focus on prioritization or go-to-market scenarios rather than consumer-style 'design an app' questions. Practice with B2B scenarios: how would you roll out a new standard to airlines with different IT maturity levels, or how would you prioritize a roadmap when your biggest member wants something smaller members cannot support. Use RICE or a simple impact-effort matrix to structure your answer.

How do I stand out as a candidate without direct airline industry experience?

Lead with transferable depth. Regulated industries like fintech, healthtech, logistics, or government software translate well to IATA's environment. Frame your experience around compliance, enterprise adoption, and multi-stakeholder alignment. Then show you have done your homework by mentioning a specific IATA product or initiative and connecting it to a problem you have solved before.
