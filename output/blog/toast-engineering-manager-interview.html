01 Overview

Overview

Toast builds the technology powering restaurants across the US and beyond, from point-of-sale hardware to payroll and online ordering. Engineering Managers at Toast own both the people side (hiring, coaching, performance management) and the delivery side (roadmap execution, technical quality, cross-functional coordination). The role sits at the intersection of a fast-scaling product company and a deeply operational industry where downtime has direct, real-world consequences for restaurant staff and customers.

Candidates typically report a multi-round process that includes a recruiter screen, a hiring manager conversation, a set of behavioral rounds with engineering and cross-functional leaders, and sometimes a case study or written exercise. The process leans heavily on leadership principles and real examples from your past, so preparation is about having your stories ready, not cramming for algorithms.

As of mid-2026, Toast has 375 open engineering and product roles tracked on knok's job radar. For Engineering Manager positions, salary bands publicly reported by candidates range from 35-60 LPA at the Manager level, 55-90 LPA at Senior Manager, and 90-150+ LPA at the Director level.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions reflect Toast's focus on leadership depth, reliability culture, and cross-functional collaboration in a high-stakes industry. Candidates report these themes coming up consistently across teams and levels.

  1. Tell me about a time you gave difficult feedback to a senior engineer. How did you approach it, and what happened?
  2. How do you balance technical debt with delivering new features when the business is pushing for speed?
  3. Toast's customers run their businesses on the platform during peak hours. Describe how you have built or maintained systems with strict reliability requirements.
  4. Walk me through how you have scaled an engineering team. What changed in how you operated as the team grew?
  5. Tell me about a project you led that failed or significantly missed its goals. What did you do, and what did you learn?
  6. How do you handle disagreement between your product manager and tech lead on the direction of a feature?
  7. Priorities at a high-growth company shift often. How do you keep your team focused and motivated through frequent roadmap changes?
  8. Walk me through your hiring process. What do you look for in an engineer beyond technical ability?
  9. How do you structure your one-on-ones to make them genuinely useful for both you and the engineer?
  10. Describe a situation where an engineer on your team was technically strong but struggling with communication or collaboration. How did you help them?
  11. Tell me about a time you had to advocate for your team's priorities with senior leadership.
  12. How do you maintain engineering quality standards such as code review, testing, and on-call practices without micromanaging your team?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a time you gave difficult feedback to a senior engineer.

*Situation:* A senior engineer on my team was producing excellent technical work but had become a bottleneck in code reviews, often going several days without responding and leaving junior engineers blocked.

*Task:* I needed to address this without demotivating someone who was technically strong and well-respected by the broader team.

*Action:* I came to our next one-on-one with specific instances and their downstream impact on team velocity. I opened by acknowledging the engineer's contributions, then described the pattern I was seeing and framed it around the team rather than personal criticism. I asked for their perspective first. It turned out they were carrying a parallel architecture task that was not visible to me. Together we agreed on a temporarily lighter review load and a clear expectation for response times going forward.

*Result:* Review turnaround improved within a couple of weeks, and the engineer later told me the conversation helped them feel more comfortable flagging overload early in the future.

---

Q: How do you handle it when your product manager and tech lead disagree on direction?

*Situation:* My PM wanted to ship a new reporting dashboard to restaurant operators quickly. My tech lead felt the underlying data pipeline needed a rebuild first to avoid accumulating serious technical debt.

*Task:* I needed to make a call that balanced business urgency with long-term system health, and bring both people along.

*Action:* I set up a structured conversation where each person laid out their reasoning. I asked the tech lead to describe the risk of proceeding without the rebuild in concrete terms: likely failure scenarios and ongoing maintenance cost. I asked the PM to explain what operators were losing by waiting. Once both sides heard each other clearly, I proposed a phased approach: ship a limited version using the existing pipeline, with a committed plan to complete the rebuild before the full rollout.

*Result:* Both agreed. The limited version shipped on time, received strong feedback from operators, and the rebuild was completed before the broader release.

---

Q: How have you developed an engineer who was technically strong but struggling with collaboration?

*Situation:* One of my most capable engineers was producing great work but came across as dismissive in design discussions, which was starting to affect team morale.

*Task:* My goal was to help this person grow without making them feel targeted or undervalued.

*Action:* In our one-on-ones I shared specific examples of what I had observed and the impact on the team, avoiding generalizations. We identified a concrete focus: leading with curiosity rather than opening with objections in group discussions. I also gave this engineer visibility on a high-profile cross-team project, pairing them with a senior colleague who naturally modeled the collaborative style we were working toward.

*Result:* Over the following months the team's perception shifted noticeably. This engineer became one of the more thoughtful voices in design reviews and eventually took on mentoring a newer hire.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioral questions. Every behavioral question at Toast is best answered with a Situation, Task, Action, Result structure. Keep the Situation and Task brief (two to three sentences combined) and spend most of your time on Action and Result. Candidates who spend too long on context and rush through what they actually did tend to score lower.

The impact ladder for results. When describing a result, connect it to something the business cared about: team velocity, system reliability, retention, or customer impact. 'The team shipped on time' is weaker than 'the team shipped on time and restaurant operators were able to close shifts faster, which reduced support tickets.'

Situation framing for cross-functional questions. Toast is a product-led company with strong opinions across engineering, product, and operations. When answering conflict or alignment questions, show that you understood all sides before describing your decision. Interviewers typically look for EMs who build alignment rather than impose decisions.

Technical credibility without deep-dive answers. EM rounds are not algorithm rounds, but you are expected to speak fluently about trade-offs, architecture decisions, and engineering quality. Practice framing past technical decisions in terms of context, options considered, and the reasoning behind your choice.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Toast interviewers typically look for a few qualities that reflect the company's context as a high-growth, operationally critical product company.

Reliability mindset. Restaurants run on Toast's platform during their busiest hours. Interviewers want to see that you have thought carefully about uptime, incident response, and engineering quality in past roles, not just delivery speed.

Ownership without hierarchy. Candidates report that the culture values people who drive outcomes without waiting for top-down direction. Examples that show you identified a problem, proposed a solution, and saw it through tend to resonate more than examples where you executed someone else's plan.

People development as a core skill. Strong EMs at Toast are described by candidates as coaches first. Be ready to talk about specific engineers you have developed, how you identified their growth areas, and what approaches actually worked.

Cross-functional fluency. The EM role requires close collaboration with product, design, data, and operations. Interviewers look for people who can navigate disagreement and build shared direction across functions, not just manage up and down within engineering.

Honest self-awareness. Questions about failures and difficult feedback are genuine probes. Candidates who gloss over mistakes or assign blame externally typically do not progress. Own the outcome, explain what you learned, and show how it changed how you work.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Map your key stories first. Write out several defining moments of your career as an EM: a team you scaled, a project that struggled, a person you developed, a conflict you navigated, a technical decision you made. Each story should be tight enough to deliver concisely using the STAR structure.

Learn Toast's operational context. Read about the restaurant technology space and Toast's product suite. Understand what reliability means for a restaurant operator and connect that context to your engineering leadership experience. Candidates report that interviewers appreciate genuine links between your background and Toast's domain, not generic EM answers.

Review the job description closely. Note the specific responsibilities and required experience listed. Prepare at least one concrete story that maps to each major responsibility. Candidates who mirror the language of the job description in their answers tend to get more follow-up questions, which is a positive signal.

Practice out loud, not just in your head. Reading your stories is not the same as saying them under mild pressure. Practice with a peer, record yourself, or use a mock interview service. Pay attention to whether your actions are specific enough and whether your results connect to real business impact.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Staying too high-level in answers. Many EM candidates describe what their team did rather than what they personally did. Toast interviewers typically probe for your specific contribution. Use 'I' more than 'we' when describing your actions.

Underselling failures. Questions about mistakes are not traps. Candidates who describe a real failure, take clear ownership, and explain a concrete change in their behavior tend to build more trust than those who reframe failures as hidden successes.

Skipping the 'so what' on results. A result without business context is less compelling. Connect your outcomes to something that mattered: customer impact, team retention, reduced incidents, or faster delivery.

Treating technical questions as optional. EM rounds at Toast are not purely behavioral. Candidates report questions about architecture trade-offs, technical debt strategy, and reliability practices. Be ready to discuss the technical reasoning behind past decisions.

Generic answers about Toast. Saying you are excited about 'a fast-growing tech company' lands flat. Candidates who connect their interest to the restaurant industry, the operational complexity of Toast's platform, or a specific product area make a noticeably stronger impression.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Toast Engineering Manager interview process typically have?

Candidates typically report a recruiter screen followed by a hiring manager conversation, then a set of behavioral rounds with engineering leaders and cross-functional partners. Some candidates also report a case study or written exercise, though this varies by team and level. The full process from first screen to offer typically spans a few weeks, depending on team availability and hiring urgency.

Does Toast ask coding or algorithm questions in EM interviews?

Candidates generally report that EM rounds at Toast do not include live coding or algorithm questions. The focus is on leadership, cross-functional collaboration, and technical judgment. You should be ready to discuss architecture trade-offs and engineering quality practices in depth, but writing code is typically not part of the EM interview process.

What salary can I expect as an Engineering Manager at Toast in India?

Based on publicly reported figures, Engineering Manager roles at Toast typically range from 35-60 LPA, Senior Manager roles from 55-90 LPA, and Director-level roles from 90-150+ LPA. These are broad ranges and actual offers depend on your level, team, experience, and negotiation. Glassdoor and levels.fyi carry candidate-reported data points that can help you build a more detailed and current picture.

How important is domain knowledge about restaurants or hospitality?

Prior restaurant industry experience is not required. However, candidates report that interviewers respond positively when you show genuine curiosity about the domain and can connect your engineering leadership experience to the operational realities Toast's customers face. Spending time before your interviews understanding what reliability and uptime mean for a busy restaurant is a simple and worthwhile step.

What is Toast's engineering culture like?

Candidates describe Toast's engineering culture as ownership-driven and fast-moving, with a strong emphasis on reliability given the critical nature of the platform for restaurants. Teams are reportedly cross-functional with close collaboration between engineering, product, and operations. Culture can vary across teams, so asking your interviewers directly about their team's working style is a good use of your question time at the end of each round.

How should I prepare for the 'tell me about a failure' question?

Pick a genuine failure where you had meaningful ownership of the outcome. Describe what happened clearly and without excessive hedging, explain your specific role, and focus most of your answer on what you learned and how it concretely changed how you work afterward. Interviewers at Toast, like most senior interviewers, are looking for self-awareness and a growth mindset. Reframing a failure as 'actually a success' typically backfires and signals low self-awareness.
