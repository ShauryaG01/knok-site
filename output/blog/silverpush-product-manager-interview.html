01 Overview

Overview

Silverpush is an AI-driven ad tech company best known for its contextual advertising platform, Parallels, and its cross-screen connected TV (CTV) solutions. The company works with global brands and agencies to deliver privacy-safe audience targeting, a capability that has become more valuable as the industry moves away from third-party cookies. As of mid-2026, Silverpush has 16 open roles on knok jobradar, reflecting active hiring across product and growth functions.

The PM interview at Silverpush typically runs across three to four rounds. Candidates report an initial HR or recruiter screen, a product case exercise (sometimes take-home, sometimes live), a domain and stakeholder discussion round, and a final leadership or culture conversation. The process tends to move faster than at larger tech companies.

Interviewers look for PMs who are comfortable with ad tech concepts, can define and interpret product metrics, and can communicate clearly across engineering, sales, and client-facing teams. Domain curiosity matters as much as process knowledge here.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How would you measure the success of a new ad format launched on connected TV?

*Situation:* At my previous company, we launched a pause-ad format on a streaming platform and had no shared success criteria defined before launch. Each team was tracking different numbers and calling the launch a success or failure based on whichever metric suited them.

*Task:* I was responsible for aligning business, sales, and engineering on a single measurement framework within the first sprint post-launch.

*Action:* I organised metrics into three layers. First, delivery health: fill rate, impressions served, and technical error rate. Second, engagement quality: view-through rate and completion rate, benchmarked against our existing pre-roll format. Third, business outcome: repeat bookings from advertisers in the first campaign cycle. I held a kickoff session to define 'success thresholds' with the sales team before we opened the format to all advertisers, so we had a shared definition of 'ready to scale.'

*Result:* Within two months, the pause-ad format showed stronger completion rates than pre-roll in our internal cohort data. Repeat bookings from pilot advertisers gave the sales team a clear signal to push broader rollout.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you disagreed with an engineer or designer. How did you resolve it?

*Situation:* We were building a reporting dashboard for media planners at an ad tech startup. Our lead engineer wanted to defer a campaign filtering feature, calling it 'nice to have.'

*Task:* I needed to make the case that this filter was blocking a key agency client from activating on the product, without derailing the sprint or creating friction with the team.

*Action:* Rather than pushing back in standup, I set up a focused session with the engineer and our customer success lead. I shared a recording of a user session showing the planner manually exporting data to work around the missing filter, adding several minutes to a daily workflow. I reframed the conversation: this was not a feature request, it was an activation blocker. I also helped scope a simplified version of the filter that fit within the current sprint without requiring full effort.

*Result:* The engineer agreed the scope was manageable and we shipped a working version that sprint. The agency client went live the following week.

---

Q: Describe a time you had to make a product decision without enough data. What did you do?

*Situation:* We were deciding whether to build a self-serve campaign optimisation feature or invest engineering time in improving reporting accuracy. User research was inconclusive and usage data was thin because the product was relatively new.

*Task:* I had to make a clear recommendation to the leadership team within a week, with limited quantitative evidence on either side.

*Action:* I ran a quick qualitative sprint: five short client calls where I asked users to walk me through their weekly workflow without prompting them on specific features. I also reviewed support tickets from the past quarter and categorised them by theme. Both sources pointed to reporting confusion as the more urgent pain, not missing optimisation controls. I presented my recommendation with explicit confidence levels: high confidence on the problem, lower confidence on the exact solution, and proposed a scoped experiment before full build.

*Result:* Leadership approved the reporting improvement. After launch, support tickets in that category dropped, validating the direction even without strong upfront data.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For Silverpush PM interviews, these frameworks come up frequently in candidate reports.

CIRCLES (for product design questions): Comprehend the situation, Identify the customer, Report customer needs, Cut through prioritisation, List solutions, Evaluate trade-offs, Summarise. This is particularly useful when asked to redesign a feature or propose a new ad format for a specific advertiser segment.

HEART + GSM (for metrics questions): Define goals using Happiness, Engagement, Adoption, Retention, and Task success. Pair each goal with a signal and a metric. For Silverpush's advertiser-facing products, both brand outcome metrics and performance delivery metrics typically need to be covered in your answer.

3C + P (for competitive questions): Company strengths, Customer needs, Competitor positioning, and your Product response. Use this when asked how Silverpush should respond to a new competitor or how to position a product change in the market.

Root cause tree (for problem diagnosis): When a client metric or product number is underperforming, start with: 'Is this a data issue, a product issue, or an external issue?' Then break each branch down systematically. Silverpush interviewers value structured diagnosis over instinct-based answers, especially in ad tech where many variables affect campaign performance at once.

Practice applying each framework out loud before your interview. Interviewers typically care more about your reasoning process than the specific conclusion you reach.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Candidates who have gone through the Silverpush PM interview report that interviewers look for three core signals.

Ad tech literacy. You do not need to be a programmatic advertising expert, but you should understand what a DSP, SSP, and contextual targeting mean in practice. You should also be able to explain why the deprecation of third-party cookies matters and how contextual solutions like Parallels address that shift. Candidates who cannot speak to this domain typically struggle in the technical discussion round.

Data comfort. Silverpush is a data-heavy platform where campaign performance and delivery metrics drive most product decisions. Interviewers typically probe your ability to define metrics from scratch, interpret a dashboard, and distinguish between correlation and causation in product data. Expect at least one question that involves diagnosing a hypothetical metric problem.

Cross-functional communication. Silverpush PMs work across engineering, sales, and international brand teams. Interviewers want evidence that you can adapt your communication style, influence without authority, and bring alignment across stakeholders who have different priorities.

Candidates also note that the team is lean and moves quickly. Interviewers look for people who are comfortable with ambiguity and can make reasonable decisions without waiting for perfect information.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: company and domain immersion. Read Silverpush's blog and recent press coverage to understand their core products: Parallels (contextual AI targeting), Mirrors (audience solutions), and their CTV advertising offering. Learn the basics of how programmatic advertising works, what DSPs and SSPs do, and why contextual targeting is growing as cookies phase out. Publicly reported industry commentary and Glassdoor reviews can give you colour on the culture and team environment.

Week 2: product case practice. Work through two to three product design cases using the CIRCLES framework. Focus on ad tech scenarios: improving a self-serve advertiser dashboard, designing an onboarding flow for a new client segment, or proposing a measurement feature for a new ad format. Time yourself to keep live answers under eight minutes.

Week 3: metrics and problem diagnosis. Build a personal template for metric questions: start with the goal, identify the north star metric, list supporting and guardrail metrics, then explain how you would monitor for problems. Practice diagnosing a hypothetical campaign performance drop using the root cause tree approach. These scenarios come up frequently in Silverpush interviews, candidates report.

Week 4: behavioural prep and mock interviews. Prepare six to eight STAR stories covering: influencing without authority, making a decision with limited data, handling a difficult stakeholder, and launching or iterating on a product. Practice with a peer or record yourself. Candidates report that Silverpush interviewers prefer concise, direct answers over lengthy narratives.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Being vague on ad tech concepts. Many candidates assume product thinking skills alone are enough. Silverpush interviewers specifically probe domain knowledge. If you cannot explain how contextual targeting works or what the cookie deprecation means for advertisers, it signals a preparation gap that is hard to recover from mid-interview.

Listing metrics without connecting them to outcomes. A common mistake is naming metrics (click-through rate, fill rate, daily active users) without explaining why each one matters to the business or the user. Always connect your metric choice back to a specific goal or behaviour you are trying to influence.

Jumping to solutions before clarifying the problem. When given a problem scenario, candidates who immediately propose features tend to score lower. Interviewers prefer candidates who ask clarifying questions, define success criteria, and consider whether a product change is actually the right solution versus a process or communication fix.

Missing the buyer-user distinction. Silverpush operates in a B2B model where the buyer (a brand or agency) and the end user (a campaign planner or media trader) are often different people with different needs. Missing this in product design answers is a common failure point, particularly when designing advertiser-facing features.

Over-preparing for system design. While Silverpush PMs work closely with engineering, candidates report that deep technical system design such as API schemas or infrastructure architecture is rarely expected in PM interviews. Focus your preparation on data interpretation and product reasoning instead.

How many rounds does the Silverpush PM interview typically have?

Candidates report three to four rounds typically: an HR screen, a product case exercise (take-home or live), a domain and stakeholder discussion, and a final leadership or culture round. Do I need an ad tech background to get a PM role at Silverpush?

A background in ad tech helps but is not always required. Candidates report that strong curiosity about the domain and a clear grasp of contextual advertising fundamentals can compensate for limited prior experience. You should at minimum understand what DSPs, SSPs, and cookie deprecation mean before your first technical round. Silverpush's own blog is a practical starting point for self-study.
