01 Overview

Overview

Roku is one of the largest streaming platforms in the world, connecting content providers with viewers across millions of devices. A Platform Engineer at Roku typically sits within infrastructure, developer experience, or reliability teams, building the internal systems that let product engineers ship faster and more confidently. The work spans CI/CD pipelines, container orchestration with Kubernetes, cloud infrastructure management, and observability at real streaming scale.

As of July 2026, knok's job radar shows Roku has 253 open roles, reflecting active growth. Platform Engineering at Roku means working on systems where a reliability gap directly affects viewer experience for a massive audience, so the bar for engineering judgement is high.

Candidates report the process typically spans 4-5 rounds: a recruiter screen, a technical phone screen on fundamentals, and then virtual onsite rounds covering systems design, coding (Python or Go are common), and a values conversation. Roku's interviewers favour questions grounded in real systems over abstract puzzles, so your strongest asset is your own infrastructure experience told clearly.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

  1. Walk me through designing a CI/CD pipeline for a microservices-based streaming platform from scratch.
  2. How have you used Kubernetes in production? Describe a scaling or stability challenge you actually solved.
  3. Describe your experience with infrastructure as code tools like Terraform or Pulumi. How do you manage state, drift, and module reuse?
  4. How do you design observability for a distributed system? What does a useful alerting strategy look like to you?
  5. Roku serves very large concurrent streaming audiences. How would you design for high availability and graceful degradation when a service fails?
  6. Tell me about a time you measurably improved developer velocity or reduced deployment time for your team.
  7. Walk me through your process during a production incident, from first alert to post-mortem.
  8. Describe your cloud infrastructure experience across AWS, GCP, or Azure. How have you decided between managed services and self-managed solutions?
  9. How do you handle secrets management and enforce least-privilege access in infrastructure pipelines?
  10. Have you ever migrated a legacy or monolithic system to a cloud-native architecture? What was the most difficult part?
  11. How would you approach capacity planning for a service with highly variable, spiky traffic patterns?
  12. Tell me about a time you partnered with product or application engineers to fix a reliability problem or unblock a release.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a time you measurably improved developer velocity or reduced deployment time.

*Situation:* At my previous company, the deployment pipeline for backend services was taking a very long time end-to-end, and engineers were losing focus context while waiting for builds.

*Task:* I was asked to investigate the bottleneck and propose a fix that would not sacrifice test coverage.

*Action:* I profiled the pipeline and found that integration tests were running fully sequentially with no caching of Docker layers between builds. I restructured the pipeline to parallelise test suites across agents, introduced a shared layer cache in the container registry, and moved slow smoke tests to an asynchronous post-deploy stage. I documented the change and ran it in parallel with the old pipeline for two weeks before switching over.

*Result:* Build time dropped by roughly two-thirds. Deployment frequency increased noticeably in the following quarter, and the team reported fewer context-switching complaints in the retrospective.

---

Q: Walk me through your process during a production incident.

*Situation:* Our video ingestion service showed a spike in error rates on a Saturday morning, affecting a portion of uploads for content partners.

*Task:* I was the on-call engineer and needed to diagnose, mitigate, and communicate clearly while keeping stakeholders updated.

*Action:* I checked dashboards first to narrow scope: latency was normal but error rates spiked only in one availability zone. I pulled logs and saw timeouts pointing to a downstream storage API, then confirmed via the vendor status page that there was a partial outage. I immediately rerouted traffic to the healthy availability zones using our load balancer config, sent regular status updates to stakeholders, and opened a vendor ticket. Once the vendor resolved their issue, I shifted traffic back gradually and monitored for stability.

*Result:* Full recovery happened within about an hour of my first alert. The post-mortem I wrote led us to add automatic availability zone failover to that service, reducing our exposure for similar incidents going forward.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you migrated a legacy system to a cloud-native architecture.

*Situation:* Our team inherited a monolithic deployment pipeline that ran on bare-metal servers with manual provisioning steps documented in a shared spreadsheet.

*Task:* I led the migration to a Kubernetes-based, fully automated infrastructure over six months, without disrupting ongoing product deployments.

*Action:* I started by mapping every service dependency before writing a single line of Terraform. I introduced infrastructure as code incrementally, standing up new environments in parallel and running shadow traffic through them before cutting over. I ran weekly syncs with product engineering leads so they could flag issues early. I kept the old pipeline frozen as a rollback option until we had three months of stable operation on the new stack.

*Result:* Provisioning a new environment went from a multi-day manual process to well under an hour. Infrastructure drift incidents dropped sharply, and new engineers could onboard without needing a senior walkthrough.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For systems design questions (high availability, capacity planning, CI/CD design), candidates who do well at Roku typically use a structured approach: start with clarifying questions about scale and constraints, walk through components from traffic ingestion to the data layer, call out trade-offs explicitly (cost versus reliability, managed versus self-managed), and close by discussing how you would monitor and iterate. Do not jump to a solution before you understand the requirements.

For behavioural questions, use the STAR structure: Situation (brief context), Task (your specific responsibility), Action (what you did and why, step by step), and Result (measurable outcome or what you learned). Keep Situation and Task short. Candidates report that Roku interviewers want to hear about your individual contribution, so use 'I' rather than 'we' when describing actions you personally took.

For trade-off questions (choosing between cloud services, tools, or approaches), a simple framework: state the options, list the key evaluation criteria for your context (scale, team size, cost, operational overhead), explain how you weighted them, and commit to a recommendation. Saying 'it depends' without following through is a common weak answer. Show your reasoning clearly.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates report from Roku Platform Engineer interviews, a few qualities consistently stand out.

Real experience at scale. Roku's own systems run at streaming scale, and interviewers can tell quickly if your answers come from production experience versus textbook knowledge. Ground your answers in specific systems you have owned and operated, not generalised theory.

Ownership and end-to-end thinking. Platform teams at Roku typically own infrastructure from design through operations. Interviewers want to see that you think about reliability, cost, and developer impact together, not just one dimension.

Clear communication of trade-offs. There is rarely one correct answer in platform engineering. Showing that you can weigh options and explain your reasoning clearly is valued more than picking the 'right' tool.

Cross-functional collaboration. Platform engineers work closely with product engineering teams at Roku. Candidates who can show they have successfully partnered with non-infrastructure engineers to solve delivery or reliability problems tend to stand out.

Curiosity about streaming-specific challenges. Even if your background is not in media, showing that you have thought about what makes streaming workloads distinct (spiky traffic, content delivery at scale, low-latency requirements) signals genuine interest in the domain.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know your own experience cold.
Pick your three strongest infrastructure projects. For each, prepare a two-minute verbal summary using the STAR structure. Identify specific outcomes (deployment time, error rates, recovery time) so your results are concrete. If you do not have exact figures, say 'roughly' and give an honest estimate.

Week 2: Systems design practice.
Practise designing two types of systems: a CI/CD pipeline for a microservices application, and a high-availability service that handles spiky traffic. For each, work through the components on paper, call out failure modes, and think through how you would monitor them. Review how Kubernetes handles node failures and how cloud load balancers route traffic across availability zones.

Week 3: Tool depth and Roku context.
Refresh your knowledge on the specific tools listed in your resume (Terraform, Helm, Prometheus, or others you have claimed) because interviewers typically go deeper on whatever you have listed. Read Roku's engineering blog and any publicly available engineering talks to understand how they think about platform problems.

Week 4: Mock interviews and logistics.
Do at least two timed mock interviews covering systems design and behavioural questions. For the virtual onsite, test your screen sharing, whiteboard tool, and camera ahead of time. Prepare two to three thoughtful questions to ask each interviewer about the team's current platform challenges.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping to solutions before clarifying scope. In systems design rounds, candidates who start drawing architecture before asking about scale, constraints, or team context often head in the wrong direction. Spend two to three minutes on requirements first.

Saying 'we' throughout behavioural answers. Interviewers want to understand your individual contribution. Describing everything as a team effort makes it hard to assess your specific skills. Be clear about what you personally decided, built, or drove.

Listing tools without showing depth. Saying you have used Kubernetes or Terraform is a basic expectation at this level. What stands out is describing a specific challenge you hit (a state management race condition, a node eviction issue) and exactly how you solved it.

Skipping the systemic fix in incident answers. Candidates who describe what went wrong but do not cover the permanent change they drove afterward come across as reactive. Always close the loop: what changed in the system as a result of the incident?

Not preparing questions to ask. Candidates report that Roku interviewers notice when someone has nothing to ask. One specific question about the team's current reliability challenges or platform roadmap shows genuine interest and preparation.

Underselling results. Platform engineering impact is often indirect: enabling faster deployments, reducing on-call burden, cutting manual toil. Do not skip the Result step of STAR just because the outcome felt intangible. Frame it in terms of what changed for the team.

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Roku Platform Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically runs 4-5 rounds. This usually starts with a recruiter screen and a technical phone screen, followed by a virtual onsite covering systems design, coding, and a values or behavioural conversation. The exact structure can vary by team, so confirm the format with your recruiter after the first call.

What programming languages does Roku expect Platform Engineers to know?

Candidates report Python and Go come up most frequently in technical screens, often for scripting, tooling, or infrastructure automation tasks. Roku does not typically require deep algorithmic coding, but you should be comfortable reading and writing production-quality code in at least one of these. Always check the specific job description you applied to for any stated language requirements.

Does Roku focus heavily on Kubernetes in Platform Engineer interviews?

Yes, candidates consistently report Kubernetes as a core interview topic, covering workload scaling, node management, and failure handling. Cloud infrastructure questions covering AWS or GCP also come up frequently, particularly around architectural trade-offs between managed and self-managed options. Preparing concrete examples of Kubernetes challenges you have solved in production will serve you well.

I come from a DevOps background, not streaming or media. Will that hurt my chances?

A strong DevOps or SRE background transfers well to Roku's Platform Engineer role, and candidates from non-media companies do get through. The key is showing that you understand what makes streaming workloads distinct: spiky concurrent traffic, content delivery requirements, and high availability across regions. Reading publicly available material on streaming infrastructure before your interview signals the curiosity about the domain that candidates report Roku values.
