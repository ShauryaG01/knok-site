02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions reflect common patterns at startups at Shuru's stage. Candidates report that technical rounds focus on depth of real experience and sound decision-making.

  1. Walk me through how you would set up a CI/CD pipeline from scratch for a microservices application.
  2. How do you manage secrets and credentials securely across dev, staging, and production environments?
  3. Describe your hands-on experience with Kubernetes. How have you handled cluster scaling, pod failures, or resource limits?
  4. What is your approach to monitoring and alerting in production? Which tools have you used, and why those tools?
  5. Tell me about a production incident you were personally responsible for resolving. What was your process?
  6. How have you implemented infrastructure as code? Do you prefer Terraform or another tool, and what drives that preference?
  7. How do you ensure zero-downtime deployments for a service with active users?
  8. Have you worked on reducing cloud infrastructure costs? How did you approach the trade-off between cost and reliability?
  9. How do you handle configuration drift across multiple environments?
  10. How would you design a disaster recovery plan for a cloud-native application with tight recovery time requirements?
  11. Describe a time you collaborated with a development team to debug a performance issue in a live environment.
  12. How do you evaluate whether a new DevOps tool or framework is worth adopting for your team?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR method (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for experience-based questions. Three examples below.

---

Q: Tell me about a production incident you resolved.

*Situation:* Our core API started returning errors for a subset of users late at night. Alerts fired and I was on call.

*Task:* Identify the root cause quickly and restore service without a full rollback if possible.

*Action:* I checked the Grafana dashboard first and noticed memory usage spiking on two pods. I cross-referenced the deployment history and found a new release had shipped a background job with a memory leak. I scaled up the replica count to absorb traffic while I investigated, then rolled back that specific deployment using Helm. I looped in the on-call developer with a message summarising what I found and what I had already done.

*Result:* Service was fully restored quickly. We added a memory threshold alert we had not had before, and I wrote a rollback runbook so future on-call engineers could move faster.

---

Q: How have you implemented infrastructure as code?

*Situation:* I joined a team that managed all infrastructure manually through the AWS console. Nothing was version-controlled.

*Task:* Migrate existing infrastructure to Terraform so changes were auditable, repeatable, and reviewable before being applied.

*Action:* I started by importing existing resources into Terraform state rather than recreating them, which minimised risk of downtime. I wrote modules for VPC, EC2, RDS, and S3. I set up remote state in S3 with DynamoDB locking, then introduced a PR-based workflow where every infrastructure change required a 'terraform plan' review before merging.

*Result:* Within a few months, all production infrastructure was in code. Deployment time dropped noticeably, and two misconfigurations were caught during PR review that would have caused outages if applied directly.

---

Q: How do you manage secrets securely in a cloud environment?

*Situation:* Our application was storing secrets as plain-text environment variables committed directly to the repository.

*Task:* Move to a secure, auditable secrets management solution without disrupting live services.

*Action:* I evaluated AWS Secrets Manager against HashiCorp Vault. Given our existing AWS footprint, Secrets Manager was the lower-overhead path. I rotated all existing secrets first, updated the application to fetch them at runtime via the SDK, restricted access using IAM roles with least-privilege policies, and enabled access logging through CloudTrail.

*Result:* No secrets remained in version control. Rotation became automated. The next security audit flagged zero issues with credential handling.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for experience and incident questions. When asked 'tell me about a time you did X', open with one sentence of context (Situation), one sentence on your specific responsibility (Task), two or three sentences on exactly what you did (Action), and close with a concrete outcome (Result). Keep the whole answer under two minutes. Rehearse out loud, not just in your head.

A four-step flow for system design questions. First, clarify requirements: ask what scale, what constraints, what matters most. Second, sketch a high-level architecture. Third, walk through trade-offs for the two or three most critical decisions. Fourth, mention what you would monitor in production. Startups like Shuru often care more about your reasoning process than the final design, so think out loud rather than jumping straight to a solution.

The 'why this tool' format. When asked why you chose a technology, always compare at least two options. Use this structure: 'I considered X and Y. I chose X because of A and B. The trade-off was C, which we accepted because of D.' This shows evaluative thinking rather than just tool familiarity.

Frame results in business terms. 'This reduced our monthly cloud bill' is stronger than 'this was more efficient.' At a startup, infrastructure decisions have direct business impact. Showing you are aware of that sets you apart from candidates who think purely in technical terms.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Shuru, like most startups at its stage, is looking for engineers who operate with high ownership and minimal hand-holding. Candidates report that panels at similar companies typically evaluate three things.

Hands-on depth. Can you actually do the work? Interviewers will probe for specifics: which flags you set, which errors you saw, how you debugged it. Saying 'I have experience with Kubernetes' is not enough. You need to follow it with a story that shows you have run it in production and dealt with real problems under pressure.

Speed and sound judgment. Startups move fast. The panel wants to see that you can make a reasonable infrastructure decision quickly, communicate it clearly, and course-correct if it turns out to be wrong. Do not over-engineer your answers or describe solutions that require a large team to implement.

Collaboration across teams. DevOps roles sit between dev, product, and ops. Expect at least one question about how you work with developers or communicate infrastructure constraints to non-technical stakeholders. Show that you can translate technical decisions into plain language without losing the important details.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Two weeks before. Focus on fundamentals. Refresh your knowledge of Kubernetes, Terraform, and CI/CD by building or rebuilding something small. Do not just read documentation: run experiments, break things, and fix them. Pull two or three incidents from your past work and structure them as STAR answers.

One week before. Go deeper on Shuru. Research the company, its product, and any publicly available information about its engineering approach. Think about the infrastructure challenges a startup at Shuru's stage typically faces: rapid feature deployment, multi-environment consistency, and cost control as the user base grows.

Three days before. Practice out loud with a timer. Answer each major question type (CI/CD setup, incident handling, IaC, monitoring, secrets management) in under two minutes. Record yourself or practice with a peer. Rambling or generic answers are a common rejection reason at startup interviews.

Day before. Prepare questions for the interviewer. Ask what the team's current tooling looks like, what a typical on-call rotation involves, and what the biggest infrastructure challenge is right now. Thoughtful questions signal genuine interest and help you assess whether the role is a fit.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Being too generic. Claiming you have experience with Docker, Kubernetes, or Terraform without backing it with a specific example is a red flag at startups. Every technical claim needs a story that shows you have done it in a real environment.

Misreading the startup context. DevOps at Shuru is not the same as at a large enterprise. Do not describe solutions that assume a large team, a multi-month delivery cycle, or an unlimited tooling budget. Shuru wants engineers who can do more with less and move quickly without introducing unnecessary complexity.

Skipping trade-off discussions. When you describe a tool choice or architecture decision, always mention what you gave up. Interviewers penalise candidates who present their choices as the only obvious answer. Trade-offs show engineering maturity.

Memorising commands instead of understanding concepts. Interviewers at technical startups often go off-script. If you have only prepared canned answers, you will struggle the moment they probe a level deeper. Focus on understanding why something works, not just what command to run.

Not asking questions. Leaving the interview without asking anything signals low interest or low curiosity. Prepare at least three genuine questions about the role, the team's current challenges, or the product direction.
