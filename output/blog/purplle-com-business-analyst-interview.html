01 Overview

Overview

Purplle.com is one of India's fastest-growing beauty and personal care e-commerce platforms, competing in the same space as Nykaa and Myntra Beauty. As of mid-2026, the company has 44 open roles, signalling active and broad hiring across functions. Business Analysts at Purplle typically sit at the intersection of product, growth, and operations, turning data into decisions on everything from campaign ROI to demand forecasting for new categories.

Candidates report a process that typically runs three to four rounds: an initial screening call, a case study or take-home assignment (usually involving Excel or SQL), a technical round with live problem-solving, and a final conversation with a senior stakeholder. The exact structure varies by team and seniority, so treat this as a general guide.

The role rewards people who combine sharp analytical thinking with clear communication to non-technical audiences. Purplle's business spans skincare, haircare, makeup, and wellness, so familiarity with D2C metrics, customer lifetime value, and funnel analysis gives you a real edge in both the interview and on the job.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Orders on the Purplle platform dropped this week. Walk me through your investigation.

*Situation:* At my previous company, we noticed a sharp, unexplained drop in daily orders over two consecutive days with no obvious external cause.

*Task:* I was asked to find the root cause and recommend immediate fixes before the weekly leadership review.

*Action:* I started by segmenting the drop to check whether it was across all categories or specific to one. It turned out to be concentrated in a single category. I then broke the funnel step by step, from landing page visits down to order confirmation, and found the sharpest drop at the payment stage. I cross-referenced analytics data with customer support tickets and found a spike in 'payment failed' complaints linked to a specific bank's net banking option. I brought in the engineering team immediately.

*Result:* The payment gateway issue was fixed within hours. I followed up by setting up a daily anomaly alert to catch similar problems faster in the future. The process became a template for incident response across the analytics team.

---

Q: How would you use cohort analysis to improve customer retention in the beauty category?

*Situation:* At a previous role, retention for customers who made their first beauty purchase was weaker than for other categories, and the team had no structured view of when customers were churning.

*Task:* I needed to identify at which point after the first purchase customers were dropping off, and what interventions could bring them back.

*Action:* I built weekly cohorts based on first-purchase date and tracked repeat purchase behaviour over the following weeks. I compared cohorts that received post-purchase communications with those that did not, and segmented by product sub-category since skincare buyers behaved differently from makeup buyers. I identified the specific week windows where drop-off was steepest and presented the findings to the CRM and category teams with a recommendation for targeted re-engagement nudges at those exact windows.

*Result:* The CRM team launched a targeted campaign using those timing windows. Industry surveys on D2C retention commonly cite meaningful lifts from well-timed re-engagement, and our internal results were in line with that pattern. The category team also adjusted their repeat-purchase bundle offers based on the cohort data.

---

Q: Describe a time you used data to change a decision your team was about to make.

*Situation:* My marketing team was planning a blanket discount on a popular product across all customer segments to hit a quarterly sales target.

*Task:* I was asked to model the financial impact before the decision was finalised.

*Action:* I segmented the customer base and analysed price sensitivity by segment. The data showed that high-value repeat customers bought at full price regardless of a promotion. Discounting them would reduce margin without adding volume. New and lapsed customers, however, responded strongly to promotions. I prepared a one-page summary comparing the margin impact of a blanket discount against a targeted one, and presented it to the marketing lead with a clear recommendation.

*Result:* The team shifted to a targeted discount strategy aimed only at new and lapsed buyers, protecting margin on the best customers. The marketing lead later told me this became the default approach for promotional planning across the team.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

North Star Metric: For any question about measuring success, anchor your answer to one primary metric that reflects the feature's core value, then list three to four supporting metrics. For a Purplle app feature, 'repeat purchase rate' might be the north star, supported by session depth, add-to-cart rate, and average order value.

Funnel Analysis: When asked to diagnose a problem (drop in orders, rising cart abandonment), always break the user journey into stages: awareness, visit, product view, add to cart, checkout, payment, and confirmation. Find the stage with the steepest drop-off and focus your analysis there first.

MECE (Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive): When structuring a case or prioritising features, group your factors so they do not overlap and together cover every relevant dimension. This signals structured thinking and is valued at product-first companies like Purplle.

RICE Scoring: For prioritisation questions, use a named framework. RICE scores ideas on Reach, Impact, Confidence, and Effort. Applying it, even briefly, shows you have a repeatable method rather than relying on gut feel.

The 'So What' Test: After every analysis insight, ask yourself 'so what does this mean for the business?' before presenting. Interviewers want a recommendation, not just an observation. End every answer with a clear point of view.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Purplle is a data-driven, fast-paced company. Based on what candidates report, interviewers typically look for four things.

Product sense: Can you think like a user and a business at the same time? Interviewers want analysts who understand why a metric moved, not just that it moved. Connecting data to customer behaviour in the beauty category is a strong positive signal.

SQL and Excel fluency: Candidates report at least one round involving hands-on data work. Being comfortable writing queries to segment users, calculate retention, or build a simple model is essential. Practice before you walk in, not the night before.

Structured thinking: Case questions are open-ended on purpose. Interviewers want to see you build a framework before jumping to an answer. State your assumptions, define your approach, then solve step by step.

Communication: You will present findings to non-technical stakeholders in this role. Interviewers look for candidates who translate numbers into clear business recommendations without jargon or unnecessary complexity.

Familiarity with the beauty and personal care industry is a plus but not a requirement. Showing that you have done basic homework on Purplle's categories, competitors, and recent news from 2024 to 2026 signals genuine interest and often tips close decisions in your favour.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know the company. Use the Purplle app and form your own opinions on the UX and product experience. Read recent media coverage, investor updates, and app store reviews. Understand the key categories, the private label strategy, and how Purplle positions itself against competitors. Come in with at least two or three specific observations about what is working and what could be improved.

Week 2: Sharpen your SQL. Practice queries involving joins, window functions, and cohort calculations. Common patterns in case rounds include computing retention by cohort, ranking products by revenue, and flagging anomalies in daily order data. Use publicly available datasets if you do not have a work environment to practice in.

Week 3: Case practice. Work through three to five product or growth cases using the funnel and MECE frameworks. Practice speaking your thinking out loud before arriving at an answer. The goal is to sound structured, not scripted.

Week 4: Behavioural prep. Write out three to four STAR stories from your experience. Cover: using data to change a decision, working with a difficult stakeholder, building something from scratch with limited data, and a time you were wrong and corrected course. Keep each story under two minutes when spoken aloud.

On the day: Ask clarifying questions before structuring your answer. State your assumptions explicitly. If you do not know something, say so and offer to reason through it. Ending with a clear recommendation is as important as the analysis that leads to it.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping to answers. The most common mistake candidates report is skipping structure and going straight to a solution. Interviewers at Purplle typically want to see your reasoning process. Take a moment to organise your thoughts, even for questions that seem simple.

Ignoring business context. Saying 'increase the sample size' or 'collect more data' without connecting it to a business outcome signals that you think like a tool user rather than a business partner. Always tie your analysis to a decision the team needs to make.

Over-engineering the framework. Listing several frameworks for a straightforward question can make you seem rehearsed but not sharp. Match the depth of your structure to the complexity of the question in front of you.

Not knowing your own work. Candidates who list advanced techniques like cohort analysis on their CV but cannot walk through one live have a hard time recovering. Only claim what you can defend in detail under questioning.

Skipping the recommendation. Ending your answer with 'and so we can see the trend' without saying 'therefore I recommend X because Y' leaves the interviewer without a conclusion. Every case and every analysis question should close with a clear point of view.
