02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions are commonly reported by candidates who have interviewed for QA roles at CashKaro. Expect a mix of core testing concepts, automation depth, and scenarios tied to the cashback and e-commerce domain.

  1. Walk us through how you would write a test plan for a new cashback credit feature.
  2. How do you test a coupon redemption flow, including edge cases like expired coupons, duplicate usage, and invalid codes?
  3. Describe your experience with automation frameworks. Which tools have you used and why did you choose them?
  4. How would you validate that affiliate tracking links and cashback attribution are working correctly for a newly onboarded brand partner?
  5. You have two days before a release and a full regression run takes one full day. How do you prioritize what gets tested?
  6. Walk us through testing a payment integration from the user side and at the API level.
  7. How do you handle a situation where a developer pushes a hotfix minutes before a scheduled release?
  8. What is your approach to writing test cases that stay maintainable as the product and codebase change?
  9. Have you done performance or load testing? How did you identify where the bottlenecks were?
  10. Describe a critical production bug you found or escalated. What was your process from discovery to resolution?
  11. How do you set up automation tests in a CI/CD pipeline, and what does a healthy pipeline look like to you?
  12. CashKaro works with hundreds of partner brands. How would you test a newly onboarded brand's cashback offer end-to-end?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format for behavioural and scenario questions: Situation, Task, Action, Result. The examples below show how to apply it to common CashKaro QA scenarios.

---

Q: How do you test a coupon redemption flow for edge cases?

*Situation:* At my previous company we launched a festive-season coupon campaign across a high-traffic checkout flow.

*Task:* I was responsible for complete test coverage of the coupon module before go-live, including boundary and negative scenarios.

*Action:* I mapped out every state the coupon could be in: valid, expired, already used, applied to an ineligible category, and applied alongside a conflicting offer. I wrote test cases for each state, ran them manually first, then automated the happy path and the top failure modes using Selenium. I also ran API-level checks to confirm the backend rejected invalid coupons before the UI even responded.

*Result:* We caught a bug where an expired coupon still applied a discount if the user session had started before midnight. The fix went in before launch and we had zero coupon-related escalations during the entire campaign.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you had to prioritize testing under a tight deadline.

*Situation:* Our team was releasing a new brand integration and the dev team delivered the build late, leaving only one day for QA.

*Task:* I had to decide which tests to run and communicate the risk of anything skipped to the product manager.

*Action:* I grouped test cases into three buckets: critical flows (cashback crediting, tracking pixel firing, payment), secondary flows (UI rendering, error messages), and cosmetic issues. I ran critical flows first with full coverage, flagged secondary items as things to verify in production monitoring, and documented every skipped case explicitly in the test report.

*Result:* The release went live on schedule. Two minor UI issues appeared post-launch, both non-blocking, and they were patched in the next sprint. The product manager appreciated the transparent risk communication upfront.

---

Q: Describe a critical bug you found and how you escalated it.

*Situation:* During regression testing for a new payment gateway integration, I noticed cashback was not being credited for a specific bank's net banking transactions.

*Task:* I needed to confirm the issue, assess its impact, and escalate quickly since the feature was only a few hours from going live.

*Action:* I reproduced the issue across multiple bank accounts to confirm it was consistent, captured API logs showing the transaction ID was not being passed to the cashback engine, and wrote a clear bug report with reproduction steps, logs, and a severity rating. I flagged it directly to the dev lead rather than waiting for the next standup.

*Result:* The bug was confirmed as critical and the release was held for a few hours while the fix was applied and re-verified. Catching it before launch protected both user trust and the affiliate revenue for that payment partner.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For 'how would you test X' questions

Start with scope: what is the feature doing, who uses it, and what does success look like. Then work through layers: UI, API, database, and any third-party integrations. Finish with non-functional concerns like performance and security. This shows systematic thinking rather than just a flat list of test cases.

For tool and automation questions

Name the tool, explain why you chose it over alternatives, and give a specific outcome it enabled. Avoid vague answers like 'I used Selenium because it is popular.' Instead say something like: 'I chose Selenium with TestNG because our CI was already on Jenkins and the integration was straightforward. We cut regression run time significantly.' Specificity is what separates a junior answer from a senior one.

For behavioral questions

Use STAR: Situation (brief context, one or two sentences), Task (what you were personally responsible for), Action (what you specifically did, not what the team did), Result (a concrete outcome). Spend the most time on Action and Result. A result like 'the team was happy' is weak; a result like 'we caught a payment bug before launch and avoided a rollback' is strong.

For conflict or deadline pressure questions

CashKaro teams are typically fast-moving. Interviewers want to see you advocate for quality without blocking releases. Lead with collaboration: 'I document the risk clearly and let the product owner make the call.' Avoid framing QA as a gatekeeper that holds up engineering.

For domain-specific questions (cashback, affiliate tracking)

If you have direct e-commerce QA experience, lead with that. If not, show you have done your homework: mention that cashback platforms depend on accurate tracking pixels, correct attribution of transactions to users, and reliable third-party partner APIs. These are the areas where bugs have direct revenue impact.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Strong fundamentals, not just tool names

CashKaro interviewers typically want to see that you understand why you structure tests a certain way, not just that you know Selenium or Postman. Be ready to explain trade-offs: when to automate versus test manually, when to block a release versus document a risk and ship.

E-commerce and fintech domain awareness

CashKaro's product involves money movement, affiliate partnerships, and high-traffic promotional events. Candidates who can speak to testing payment flows, tracking accuracy, and system behaviour under load stand out. You do not need prior cashback platform experience specifically, but you should understand what can go wrong in these flows and how to test for it.

Ownership and clear communication

In a fast-moving product environment, QA engineers are expected to raise issues proactively and communicate risk in plain language to non-technical stakeholders. Interviewers watch for whether you frame your past work in terms of decisions made and outcomes achieved, not just tasks completed.

Automation depth for mid and senior roles

At the 9-17 LPA range and above, knowing how to write a test is table stakes. Knowing how to build a maintainable, CI-integrated suite with clear reporting is what separates candidates. Expect detailed questions about framework choices and how you handle flaky tests and suite maintenance over time.

Precision in your own answers

QA is a precision role. Interviewers notice when candidates are vague about their own processes. Specific tool names, exact steps taken, and clear outcomes signal that you actually did the work you are describing.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Fundamentals and domain research

Revise core testing concepts: test planning, test design techniques (equivalence partitioning, boundary value analysis, decision tables), and the defect lifecycle. Spend time reading about how cashback platforms work, specifically how affiliate tracking, coupon codes, and cashback crediting operate end-to-end. This gives you concrete domain examples to use in your answers.

Week 2: Automation and tools

Pick one automation framework you are comfortable with (Selenium, Cypress, Appium, or similar) and practise writing tests for a sample e-commerce checkout flow. If you have not done API testing recently, work through some exercises with Postman or RestAssured against a public API. Review how to integrate tests into a CI tool like Jenkins or GitHub Actions.

Week 3: Practice and refinement

Write out STAR answers for the behavioral questions in this guide. Do at least one timed mock interview where you answer questions out loud, as many candidates who know the answers struggle to articulate them clearly under pressure. Have specific outcomes ready from your past projects: bugs caught before production, manual testing time reduced, releases protected from rollback.

Before the interview

Check CashKaro's current openings and note which team or product area the specific role covers. Tailor your examples to match. Candidates report that asking a specific question about the team's current QA challenges makes a strong impression in the final round.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

1. Listing tools without explaining outcomes

Saying 'I used Jira, Selenium, and Postman' is not an answer. Every candidate lists tools. What interviewers want to know is what those tools helped you achieve, and what would have gone wrong without them.

2. Ignoring the domain

Candidates who prepare only generic QA answers and show no understanding of cashback flows, affiliate tracking, or e-commerce testing often get filtered out early. Spend at least a few hours using the CashKaro product and thinking through how you would test its core features.

3. Framing QA as a blocking role

Answers like 'I would not allow the release until every bug is resolved' signal poor judgment at product companies. The right framing is: document the risk clearly, give stakeholders the information they need, and support the decision that gets made.

4. Vague STAR answers

Answers that end at 'we improved quality' or 'the team was happy' are weak. Push yourself to include a concrete result: a bug caught before production, a measurable reduction in manual testing time, or a release that went out without rollback.

5. Not preparing questions for the interviewer

Candidates who have no questions signal low interest or low preparation. Prepare a few genuine questions about the team's current test coverage, their CI/CD setup, or how QA collaborates with product managers on release decisions.

6. Treating the assignment as a formality

Many candidates underestimate the take-home assignment. Interviewers look at the quality of edge case coverage and how clearly you document your approach and trade-offs. A weak assignment can override an otherwise strong technical round.
