01 Overview

Overview

Zensar is a mid-size IT services and digital solutions company headquartered in Pune, with delivery centres across India. The Platform Engineer role sits within their cloud and infrastructure practice, where engineers help enterprise clients move to cloud-native platforms, modernise CI/CD pipelines, and manage containerised workloads at scale.

As of July 2026, knok's job radar shows 204 Platform Engineer openings across India, with Zensar alone carrying 255 open roles across all functions. Within the Platform Engineer category, openings span Bangalore (29), Delhi (12), and Pune (10), among other cities.

The interview process typically runs across multiple rounds. Candidates report a technical screening call, one or two deeper technical rounds covering infrastructure and DevOps, and a final HR or managerial conversation. The exact structure varies by project team and seniority, so confirm the format with your recruiter early.

Most Asked Questions

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk me through a CI/CD pipeline you designed from scratch.

*Situation:* At my previous organisation, the team was releasing software manually every few weeks, which led to frequent integration conflicts and slow feedback on bugs.

*Task:* I was asked to design and implement an automated CI/CD pipeline for a Java-based microservices application so the team could ship changes on demand.

*Action:* I set up GitHub Actions as the CI layer with stages for unit testing, static analysis via SonarQube, and Docker image builds. For continuous delivery, I used ArgoCD for GitOps-based deployments to an AWS EKS cluster. I introduced environment-specific Helm charts to separate staging and production config, and added automated rollback triggers tied to health-check failures.

*Result:* The team moved from fortnightly releases to deploying on demand. Rollbacks that previously took hours became automated and completed in minutes. Integration bugs were caught earlier in the cycle, reducing incidents after release.

---

Q: Describe a time a production deployment failed.

*Situation:* During a routine release at a fintech client, a configuration change caused the payment service to lose database connectivity, affecting transactions for active users.

*Task:* As the on-call platform engineer, I had to find the root cause quickly, restore service, and prevent the same issue from reaching production again.

*Action:* I used Datadog alerts and log traces to identify a misconfigured environment variable introduced in the release. I rolled back the deployment via ArgoCD, restoring service. I then added a config validation step to the pipeline that checks critical environment variables before any deployment proceeds. I also ran a post-mortem with the team to document findings and share learnings.

*Result:* Service was restored quickly with minimal disruption. The config validation step has since caught similar misconfigurations in staging before they could reach users.

---

Q: How do you keep cloud infrastructure costs in check while maintaining reliability?

*Situation:* At a previous client, the AWS bill had grown steadily over several months with no clear visibility into what was driving it.

*Task:* I was asked to investigate and propose optimisations without affecting production reliability.

*Action:* I set up AWS Cost Explorer with tagging by team and environment, which quickly revealed that dev and test environments were running full-size instances around the clock. I implemented auto-shutdown schedules for non-production environments using Lambda and EventBridge. I also right-sized instances based on CloudWatch utilisation data and moved stable workloads to reserved instances.

*Result:* Monthly cloud spend fell noticeably in the quarter following the changes. The team gained ongoing visibility to catch cost creep early, and production reliability was unaffected throughout the process.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Context first. Open by briefly naming the environment: the cloud provider, the type of application, and the rough scale. This helps the interviewer calibrate your experience before you get into the detail.

Tool choice with reasoning. Do not just name the tool. Explain why you picked it over an alternative. Zensar works across multiple cloud platforms and client environments, so showing you can weigh trade-offs matters more than naming a favourite stack.

Outcome with honest framing. Even without precise metrics from past projects, frame results in terms of what improved: deployment frequency, incident response time, or team confidence. Use qualifiers like 'significantly faster' or 'reduced from hours to minutes' rather than numbers you cannot verify. If you cite specific figures, make clear whether they come from publicly reported benchmarks or your own observed results.

For behavioural questions, use the STAR structure: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task brief. Spend most of your time on the Action, since that is where your thinking shows. Tie the Result to a business or team outcome rather than stopping at the technical solution.

Pacing. A clear, well-structured answer is stronger than an exhaustive technical monologue. Cover the key points without rushing, and invite the interviewer to probe deeper at the end rather than pre-empting every possible follow-up question.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on candidate reports, Zensar Platform Engineer interviewers consistently look for a few core qualities.

Hands-on depth, not just tool names. Listing Terraform, Kubernetes, and Jenkins is a starting point. Interviewers want to hear how you used those tools, what went wrong, and how you resolved it.

Client-facing mindset. Zensar is an IT services company, so platform engineers often work directly with client teams. The ability to explain infrastructure decisions in plain language to developers, architects, or project managers is valued alongside technical depth.

Cross-cloud comfort. Candidates report questions spanning AWS, Azure, and GCP. Deep expertise in one cloud combined with working knowledge of at least one other is typically sufficient. Being able to compare managed services or IAM models across providers is a useful signal.

Structured thinking under failure. Expect at least one question about how you handle production incidents. Interviewers look for a clear mental model: detect, contain, find the root cause, prevent recurrence.

Cost awareness. IT services clients are sensitive to cloud spend. Demonstrating that you factor cost into architecture and operational decisions, not just technical correctness, signals seniority.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Audit your own experience. List the CI/CD pipelines, cloud platforms, and IaC tools you have used in production. For each, recall a scenario where something went wrong and how you fixed it. These stories are the backbone of your interview.

Step 2: Refresh core concepts. Revisit Kubernetes networking, Helm chart structure, Terraform state management, and the differences between major cloud providers' managed services. Candidates report that Zensar interviews go deep on these areas.

Step 3: Practice out loud. Pick several questions from the list above and answer them out loud or in writing using the STAR format. Keep the Situation and Task brief, make the Action detailed, and tie the Result to a real outcome. A clear, well-paced answer is more effective than an exhaustive one.

Step 4: Research Zensar's context. Read about Zensar's service lines and recent client announcements. Think about which of your past projects are closest to enterprise IT services delivery, and frame your experience accordingly when you speak to the panel.

Step 5: Prepare your own questions. Have a few questions ready about the team's current platform stack, the types of clients you would support, and how the team handles on-call responsibilities. Good questions signal genuine interest.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Listing tools without a story. Saying 'I use Terraform and Kubernetes' without connecting those tools to a specific challenge and outcome does not differentiate you. Always anchor tool mentions to a real scenario.

Stopping at the technical solution. At a services company like Zensar, the client outcome matters as much as the technical approach. Finish every answer by explaining how your work helped the team, the client, or the business.

Overclaiming experience. If you have read about a tool but not used it in production, say so. Interviewers probe deeper on every tool you name, and overstating experience tends to surface quickly under follow-up questions.

Not asking questions. Candidates who leave the panel with no questions of their own can come across as disengaged. Prepare a few genuine questions about the team's platform, the client environment, and how success is measured in the role.

Ignoring follow-up after the interview. Candidates report that timelines at Zensar can vary by project and team. A brief, polite message to the recruiter if you have not heard back within a week is reasonable and shows continued interest.
