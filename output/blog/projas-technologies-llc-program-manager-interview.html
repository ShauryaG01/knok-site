Overview

At a services firm, you will often work across client-facing programmes, which means communication clarity and the ability to influence without direct authority carry extra weight.

Most Asked Questions

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use these as a template. Replace the specifics with your own experience. Interviewers value concrete details over polished-sounding generalisations.

Q: Describe a time when a project went significantly off track. What did you do?

*Situation:* A core integration between our payment service and a third-party gateway fell weeks behind because of undocumented API changes on the vendor's side. The go-live date was fixed due to a contractual commitment.

*Task:* I needed to either recover the schedule or negotiate a revised scope without breaching the contract or losing the client's confidence.

*Action:* I called an immediate cross-team sync with engineering, legal, and the account manager. We ran a focused two-day investigation to understand exactly which integration points were broken. I then documented three options for the client: full scope on a revised date, partial scope by the original date, or a short-term workaround with a follow-up release. I presented these with clear trade-offs rather than asking the client to choose blindly.

*Result:* The client chose the partial scope option. We shipped on the original date with the workaround in place and completed the remaining integration within the following month. The client retained the account for a follow-on project.

---

Q: How do you manage dependencies between teams that do not share the same reporting line?

*Situation:* Two product teams, each reporting to a different VP, had a shared dependency on a data pipeline that neither team owned outright. Both teams had hard external deadlines.

*Task:* I needed to get commitment from both teams on a shared delivery plan without having authority over either group.

*Action:* I set up a weekly dependency review with leads from both teams and the platform team that owned the pipeline. I maintained a shared tracker visible to all parties with clear owners, due dates, and active blockers. When one team started slipping, I escalated to both VPs together in a single message rather than separately, framing it as a shared programme risk rather than pointing at one team.

*Result:* Both teams shipped within the same sprint window. The shared tracker format was later adopted by the platform team as a standard template for future cross-team dependencies.

---

Q: How do you balance speed of delivery against technical debt concerns from engineering?

*Situation:* During a high-priority feature push, the engineering lead flagged that we were accumulating significant technical debt in the authentication module. The business wanted the feature live before a major product launch.

*Task:* I had to decide whether to slow down, ship as-is, or find a middle path, and then build alignment across engineering and product leadership.

*Action:* I asked the engineering lead to document the specific risks: what could break, at what probability, and what it would cost to fix later versus now. I then presented this to product and business stakeholders as a structured risk register, not a complaint from engineering. We agreed to ship with the debt but locked in a dedicated remediation sprint immediately after the launch, treated as a firm commitment rather than aspirational backlog.

*Result:* The feature launched on time. The remediation sprint happened as planned. The engineering lead later noted this was the first time technical debt had been handled as a formal delivery commitment rather than quietly deferred indefinitely.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions

Every 'tell me about a time' question calls for Situation, Task, Action, and Result. Keep Situation and Task brief, two to three sentences combined. Spend most of your time on Action, specifically on what you personally did rather than what the team did. Quantify the Result where you can. If you do not have a precise number, describe the qualitative outcome clearly.

RACI for stakeholder questions

When asked how you manage teams or stakeholders across boundaries, structure your thinking around who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed. This signals to interviewers that you think in systems, not just tasks. Even if you do not name the framework explicitly, answers structured this way come across as more rigorous.

Risk-first framing

For questions about programme health or project challenges, lead with the risk you identified before explaining what you did about it. Saying 'I noticed X was a risk three weeks before it became a blocker' is far stronger than 'we had a problem and I handled it.' This mirrors how experienced TPMs actually think and signals maturity.

Trade-off structure

For questions about prioritisation or speed versus quality, name the trade-off explicitly before explaining your decision. Saying 'I saw two paths: X and Y, and I chose X because...' is clearer and more impressive than just describing what you did without acknowledging the alternative you passed on.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

End-to-end ownership mindset

Interviewers want to see that you treat a programme as yours to deliver, not just yours to coordinate. The difference shows up in how you describe blockers: do you talk about escalating to someone else and waiting, or do you describe what you personally did to move the blocker before escalating?

Early and clear stakeholder communication

At a services firm, proactively surfacing bad news is a career differentiator. Interviewers look for candidates who communicate risk early rather than hoping things resolve on their own. If your answers show you waiting until a crisis to inform stakeholders, that reads as a red flag regardless of how you eventually resolved the situation.

Technical depth without technical execution

You are not expected to write code, but you are expected to understand what engineers are building well enough to ask the right questions and spot gaps in the plan. Interviewers test this through follow-up questions on the systems and technical decisions you describe. Know your past programmes deeply enough to explain them in plain language.

Influence without authority

Most TPM work involves persuading people who do not report to you. Interviewers want examples where you built alignment through data, clear framing, or relationship-building, not by relying on escalation as a first move. The best answers show you resolving friction at the working level before involving senior leadership.

Composure in the narration

How you tell a story about a past crisis matters as much as what you did. Candidates who describe chaotic situations with visible composure, and who focus on what they controlled, come across as significantly more credible than candidates whose answers make events sound like they happened to them rather than being managed by them.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Build your story bank first

Write out ten to twelve situations from your past work where you owned a programme outcome, managed a difficult stakeholder, handled a significant risk, or made a genuine trade-off. For each situation, note the context, your specific actions, and the outcome. These become the raw material for any behavioural question the interviewer asks.

Practice out loud, not just in your head

Reading answers and saying them are very different activities. Record yourself answering three to four questions from the list above. Listen for filler phrases, vague language like 'we kind of decided,' or answers that run longer than two to three minutes. Edit down and re-record.

Research Projas Technologies, LLC specifically

Look at their website, LinkedIn company page, and any recent news coverage. Note the types of clients they work with, the industries they focus on, and any technology partnerships or service lines they highlight. Weave specific observations into your questions for the interviewer. This signals genuine interest and preparation beyond the basics.

Prepare a clear 'why this company' answer

Interviewers at smaller technology firms often place more weight on motivation than those at large enterprises. Have a specific, honest answer ready that connects their work to your background or career direction. A generic answer about growth opportunities will not land well.

Prepare thoughtful questions to ask

Good questions include: how the TPM role connects to client delivery outcomes, how programme success is measured internally, and what the first three months typically look like for someone joining in this role. Avoid asking about salary or benefits in early conversations.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Saying 'we' when the interviewer means 'you'

The most common TPM interview mistake is using 'we' when describing what happened. The interviewer cannot evaluate a team; they are evaluating you specifically. Use 'I identified,' 'I escalated,' and 'I proposed' rather than 'we decided.' If the action genuinely was collaborative, explain your specific contribution within that collaboration.

Describing process without describing outcomes

Saying 'I set up weekly syncs and maintained a risk register' describes process, not impact. Always follow a process description with what it achieved: 'which helped us catch the dependency gap three weeks before it would have blocked the launch.' If the process did not lead to a clear outcome, pick a different example.

Avoiding technical depth

TPM candidates sometimes position themselves as pure delivery managers and avoid discussing the technical layer. At a technology services firm, this reads as a gap. Know the systems you have managed well enough to explain them clearly. You do not need to know how to build them, but you need to know what they do and what can go wrong.

Generic statements about stakeholder management

Phrases like 'I always kept stakeholders informed' are not believable without a specific story. Every answer should include at least one concrete detail: a role title, a decision point, a timeline, or a specific outcome. Vague answers signal that the experience may not be as deep as the candidate is suggesting.

Skipping your questions for the interviewer

Candidates who ask no questions, or only ask about benefits, signal low engagement. Asking a thoughtful question about how the TPM team is structured or what a current programme challenge looks like shows genuine interest and helps you evaluate whether this is the right role for you.
