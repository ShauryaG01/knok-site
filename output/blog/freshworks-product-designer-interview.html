01 Overview

Overview

Freshworks had 145 open Product Designer roles as of July 2026 (knok jobradar), making it one of the largest active employers for this role in India. The company builds SaaS products across customer support (Freshdesk), CRM (Freshsales), IT service management (Freshservice), and HR software, so designers work on real workflows used by support agents, sales reps, and IT teams every day.

That context shapes the interview. Panels want to see empathy for non-technical end users, comfort designing within a multi-product ecosystem, and a focus on measurable outcomes rather than visual craft alone. Candidates typically report a take-home design exercise, followed by a portfolio review call, and then a panel with design leads and cross-functional stakeholders. The exact sequence and number of rounds varies by team and level, so confirm the format with your recruiter before you begin.

Across India, 393 Product Designer roles were live at the time of this data (July 2026). Bangalore leads with 62 openings, Delhi has 33, and Mumbai has 13.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly, based on candidate reports for Freshworks design roles.

  1. Walk us through your strongest B2B or SaaS case study, from problem to shipped product.
  2. How do you balance user needs, business goals, and engineering constraints when they pull in different directions?
  3. Freshworks users are often non-technical, like call-centre agents or HR teams. How do you design for them specifically?
  4. Tell us about a time when research or data changed the direction of your design.
  5. How do you work with product managers and engineers during a fast-moving sprint?
  6. Describe a design that shipped and then needed major rework. What happened and what did you learn?
  7. How would you approach designing a feature that needs to work across multiple Freshworks products without fragmenting the experience?
  8. What is your process when a stakeholder asks you to add more elements to a screen that is already cluttered?
  9. How do you decide which usability problems to fix first when you have a long backlog?
  10. What metrics would you track to confirm that a redesigned feature is actually working for users?
  11. Tell us about a take-home design exercise you have completed. How did you approach the brief?
  12. How do you present and defend design decisions to someone who does not have a design background?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through your strongest B2B SaaS case study.

*Situation:* At my previous company, new users of our support tool (similar to Freshdesk) were dropping off during onboarding and never completing their first ticket workflow. The activation problem was flagged by the product team as a top priority.

*Task:* I owned the end-to-end redesign of the onboarding flow, from discovery through to final handoff to engineering.

*Action:* I started with user interviews, speaking with support agents to understand where they got confused. I found that the existing flow asked users to configure too many settings before they could do anything meaningful. I redesigned around a 'first win' principle: a new agent could close their first ticket using sensible defaults, without touching any settings. I validated a low-fidelity prototype with a small group of agents before moving to high fidelity.

*Result:* After the redesign shipped, the team saw a meaningful improvement in first-week activation, tracked on our OKR dashboard. The 'sensible defaults' principle became a standard pattern in our onboarding playbook.

---

Q: Describe a design that shipped and then needed major rework.

*Situation:* I shipped a bulk-action feature for a CRM table view. The design worked well on a large desktop screen, but the team had not tested it on the smaller laptops that most of our users actually worked on.

*Task:* After release, support tickets flagged that the action bar was being cut off on screen. I was asked to fix it quickly without breaking the rest of the table layout.

*Action:* I ran an audit of the affected screens across common screen sizes. I moved the bulk-action bar from a fixed position above the table to a floating bar that appeared only when rows were selected, which freed up vertical space and worked across all screen sizes. I paired closely with the front-end engineer to keep the fix scope tight.

*Result:* Support tickets for that feature dropped sharply after the update. The incident led our team to add a screen-size checklist to our design review process.

---

Q: How do you handle a stakeholder who wants to add more to an already cluttered screen?

*Situation:* A product manager wanted to add a promotional banner to our agent dashboard, which was already showing alerts, a task list, and a metrics panel.

*Task:* I needed to either find a way to accommodate the request or make a clear case for why it would hurt the user experience.

*Action:* I created a side-by-side comparison showing the current dashboard versus the proposed addition. I then pulled session recording data to show which existing elements agents actually used versus ignored. That gave us a shared basis for the conversation. I proposed collapsing the least-used panel to make room, with a user-controlled option to expand it.

*Result:* The PM agreed to the collapsed panel approach. We also agreed to track engagement on the new banner after launch to decide whether it was earning its space. The conversation shifted from 'yes or no' to 'how do we earn the space.'

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Start with the user, then the constraint. For any 'how do you design X' question, open by naming who the user is and what they are trying to accomplish. Freshworks interviewers want to see that you do not skip straight to the solution.

Use the STAR format for case studies. Situation: what was the problem and who was affected. Task: what was your specific role. Action: what you did and why, step by step. Result: what changed, and what you would do differently next time.

The 'five whys' for complexity questions. When asked how you simplify a complex workflow, walk through how you trace the root cause of the complexity before jumping to solutions. This signals systematic thinking rather than instinct.

Business value framing for stakeholder questions. When describing how you handle conflicting feedback, connect your final decision to a business or user outcome. Say 'we chose this because it reduced steps for a first-time user' rather than 'it looked cleaner.'

Metrics before and after. Even if you cannot share exact numbers, describe what you would measure to know if your design worked. Freshworks is a data-informed company and panels notice when designers think in outcomes rather than deliverables.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Empathy for non-technical users. Freshworks products serve call-centre agents, HR executives, and IT support staff, not developers. Candidates who speak about designing for users with low digital literacy, or who have real experience doing it, stand out.

Outcome orientation. Interviewers want to see that you connect your design decisions to measurable business or user outcomes. Portfolio presentations that show only visuals, without discussing impact, tend to score lower.

Collaboration instincts. Freshworks runs cross-functional squads. Expect questions about how you work with PMs and engineers, and prepare examples where disagreement was resolved through shared data rather than opinion or seniority.

System thinking. Because Freshworks has many products sharing a design system, panels look for candidates who think about consistency and reuse, not just the single screen in front of them.

Ownership through failure. Interviewers value designers who can describe a shipped product that did not work as expected, and who show they learned from it. A polished portfolio with no failures can feel rehearsed and unconvincing.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Know the product. Sign up for a free Freshdesk or Freshsales trial and use it as a real user for a few days. Take notes on flows that feel clunky or delightful. Pick one specific flow and think through how you would redesign it. This gives you a genuine, specific answer when interviewers ask about your familiarity with their product.

Step 2: Sharpen your case studies. Select two or three case studies from your portfolio that involve B2B, SaaS, or enterprise products. For each one, write out the STAR narrative: what was the problem, what was your specific role, what did you do step by step, and what changed. Be ready to go deep on follow-up questions on your strongest case study.

Step 3: Practice out loud. Record yourself presenting a case study without notes. Listen back for jargon, vague phrases like 'I worked with the team,' and moments where you skip the 'why.' Ask a peer to roleplay as a skeptical PM and push back on your design decisions.

Step 4: Prepare your questions. Review the job description and map each requirement to a specific story from your experience. Prepare a question for the interviewer about what the design team's biggest challenge is right now. That question signals you are thinking about impact, not just getting hired.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Showing process without showing decisions. A common mistake is walking through every step of your process (research, wireframes, prototypes) without explaining why you made the key decisions. Interviewers want to understand your thinking, not your timeline.

Using jargon without examples. Phrases like 'user-centric design' and 'design thinking methodology' without concrete examples signal that you know the vocabulary but not the practice.

Skipping the business context. Freshworks is a product company with real revenue goals. Candidates who talk only about user needs, without connecting them to business outcomes, miss what the company actually cares about.

Over-designing the take-home brief. Candidates sometimes spend too much time on polished visuals and too little on the written rationale. Interviewers care more about why you made each choice than how the final screen looks.

Treating 'tell me about a failure' as a trap. Interviewers ask this to learn how you think under pressure, not to catch you out. A candidate who describes a real failure and a clear lesson is far more credible than one who turns it into a disguised success story.

Not asking questions during the interview. Candidates who go through the entire process without asking anything often come across as passive. Asking what success looks like in the first few months shows initiative and genuine interest.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Freshworks Product Designer interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically includes a take-home design exercise, a portfolio review call, and one or two panel discussions with design leads and cross-functional stakeholders. The exact number of rounds varies by team and seniority level, so ask your recruiter to confirm the format upfront. Some teams add a brief screening call before the take-home exercise.

What kind of take-home brief should I expect?

Candidates typically report being asked to redesign a specific flow or solve a product problem within a set time window. The brief often involves a product type similar to what Freshworks builds, such as a support or CRM tool, rather than a consumer app. Focus your submission on the rationale behind each decision, not just the visual output. Interviewers consistently say they care more about how you think than how the mockups look.

What salary can a Product Designer expect at Freshworks?

Freshworks does not publish standard salary bands publicly. Based on industry surveys and publicly reported data, Product Designer compensation in India commonly falls in the range of 6-12 LPA for entry level (0-2 years experience), 14-24 LPA for mid level (3-5 years), 26-40 LPA for senior level (6-9 years), and 36-55+ LPA for lead or principal roles. These are market-level ranges, not Freshworks-specific figures. Check Glassdoor and levels.fyi for employee-reported numbers specific to Freshworks.

Do I need a design degree to get a Product Designer role at Freshworks?

Freshworks does not publicly specify a mandatory design degree for all Product Designer roles. Most job descriptions ask for a strong portfolio demonstrating UX thinking, not a specific qualification. Candidates with diplomas from design institutes, self-taught backgrounds, and engineering degrees with a design focus all report getting interviews. Your portfolio and case study quality matter far more than your formal credentials.

Is Freshworks a good company to grow as a Product Designer?

Freshworks is publicly listed and runs a large, multi-product portfolio, which gives designers exposure to complex systems and cross-functional product work. Candidates report that the design team has a shared design system and a reasonably mature design culture. As with any company, growth depends heavily on your team and manager, so asking about career paths and team structure during the interview itself is a smart move.
