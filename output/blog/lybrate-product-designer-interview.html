02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates who have gone through Lybrate's interview process typically report a mix of portfolio review, product thinking, and design execution questions. Here are the most commonly asked:

  1. Walk us through a project where you improved a health or consumer app experience end-to-end. What was your process?
  2. How would you redesign the Lybrate doctor search or booking flow to reduce drop-off?
  3. A user opens the app but does not complete a teleconsultation booking. How do you investigate and fix this?
  4. How do you design for users with low digital literacy, many of whom may be first-time app users?
  5. Describe a time you had to balance a business metric (conversions, retention) against the user's best interest.
  6. How do you decide what to cut from a design when engineering says the timeline is tight?
  7. Lybrate shows health content alongside its transactional features. How do you keep the UI from feeling cluttered?
  8. Tell us about a time you used research or data to push back on a stakeholder's direction.
  9. How would you approach designing notifications for a health app without causing anxiety or alert fatigue?
  10. Walk us through how you hand off designs to developers and what you do when the built product looks different from your spec.
  11. How do you test designs with users when budget or time is limited?
  12. Where do you see the biggest design opportunity for a platform like Lybrate through 2027?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time you improved a core user flow based on data or research.

*Situation:* I was working on a health-tracking feature in a consumer app. Analytics showed that a large share of new users never completed their first health profile setup.

*Task:* My goal was to increase profile completion without making the onboarding feel overwhelming.

*Action:* I ran several moderated usability sessions to find the friction points. Users were stopping at a form asking for many fields at once. I redesigned it as a progressive disclosure flow: three short steps with optional fields clearly marked, and contextual prompts explaining why each piece of information helped the user. I validated the prototype with another round of sessions before handoff.

*Result:* After launch, the team tracked a lift in profile completion (publicly reported in the product review). Users who completed setup showed stronger first-month retention.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you had to push back on a stakeholder.

*Situation:* A business stakeholder wanted to add a promotional banner on the home screen of a teleconsultation flow to upsell a premium membership plan.

*Task:* I was responsible for the UX of that screen and felt the banner would interrupt users who were already anxious about their health and just wanted to reach a doctor quickly.

*Action:* I put together a short document showing user-session recordings where people were scanning the screen for the 'consult now' button. I proposed a less intrusive placement: a contextual card that appeared only after a consultation was booked, when the user was in a positive state. I shared a side-by-side prototype so the stakeholder could see both options together.

*Result:* The team agreed to test my version first. It preserved the consult flow experience and the product lead noted it reduced misdirected taps on that screen.

---

Q: How do you approach design handoff?

*Situation:* On a previous project, a feature I designed shipped with several spacing and colour inconsistencies because the developer team was under time pressure and made judgement calls without checking back.

*Task:* I needed a more reliable handoff process for future sprints.

*Action:* I started annotating every Figma frame with explicit specs for states, spacing tokens, and interaction notes. I also introduced a brief 'design intent' walkthrough with the lead developer before each sprint: a short call to go through the screens and flag anything ambiguous. I created a shared 'known deviations' doc where engineers could log build constraints so I could decide whether to adapt the design or find a different solution.

*Result:* In the sprints after this process started, the team reported far fewer back-and-forth review comments, and the shipped product matched specs much more closely.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For design process questions, use a three-part structure: (1) how you defined the problem and who you talked to, (2) how you generated and narrowed down solutions, (3) how you measured or validated the outcome. Keep it concrete by naming the methods (usability testing, analytics, competitive audit) rather than saying 'I did research.'

For product thinking questions (like 'redesign the booking flow'), use a structured problem-breakdown approach. Start by clarifying goals and user segments. Then map the existing flow and name the biggest pain points. Propose two or three directional ideas, explain the trade-offs, and recommend one with a rationale. This shows you can think in product terms, not just visual terms.

For conflict or stakeholder questions, use STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) but make sure the 'Action' section is specific about how you persuaded, not just what you proposed. Interviewers want to see that you used evidence rather than opinion.

For 'how do you prioritise' questions, a simple 2x2 of user impact versus effort is always credible. Walk the interviewer through it with real trade-offs, not a textbook diagram.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Lybrate's interviews typically test for a few things beyond the portfolio itself.

Health-domain empathy. Designers working on a platform where users are dealing with illness, anxiety, or medical decisions need to show they understand the emotional weight of that context. Candidates who talk only about aesthetics and conversion often struggle here.

Product ownership. Lybrate typically looks for designers who have opinions about what to build, not just how to build it. Expect questions that probe whether you can read product metrics, challenge scope, and contribute to roadmap conversations.

Collaboration under constraints. Health-tech teams often move fast with small design teams. Candidates who can show they work well with engineers and PMs, and who have a clear process for managing tight timelines, are preferred.

Structured communication. Interviewers report that strong candidates narrate their thinking clearly. They say what they are optimising for, why they made a specific choice, and what they would do differently with more time.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Know the product deeply. Use the Lybrate app for at least a week before your interview. Book a mock consultation, browse doctor profiles, and note every friction point you encounter. Interviewers frequently ask candidates to critique or redesign a real flow.

Step 2: Prepare 3-4 portfolio stories. Select projects that show end-to-end work: discovery, ideation, execution, and outcome. At least one should show how you handled a difficult constraint (technical, timeline, or stakeholder related).

Step 3: Practise product thinking out loud. Pick a feature in any health or consumer app and redesign it verbally within a set time limit. Practise this with a friend or record yourself. Lybrate candidates typically report one design challenge round where you think out loud on a live problem.

Step 4: Brush up on health-tech context. Read about accessibility in health apps, patient privacy norms in India, and the design of prescription or teleconsultation flows. You do not need clinical knowledge, but you do need to show awareness of the stakes.

Step 5: Prepare thoughtful questions. Ask about design team size, how design and product collaborate on prioritisation, and what the biggest design challenge the team is currently facing. These signal genuine interest, not just job-seeking.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

  1. Treating the portfolio walkthrough as a gallery tour. Describe decisions and trade-offs, not just the final screens. Interviewers care more about how you think than how the finished design looks.
  1. Ignoring the health context. Generic design answers that could apply to an e-commerce app signal that you have not thought about Lybrate's specific user and their real-world needs.
  1. Vague research claims. Saying 'I did user research' without naming the method, the sample, or what you changed as a result is a red flag. Be specific even if the sample was small.
  1. Skipping constraints. Every real project has constraints. If your case study sounds like you had unlimited time, budget, and engineering bandwidth, interviewers will not find it credible.
  1. Not having questions ready. Candidates who say 'no, I think you covered everything' at the end of a round often leave a weaker impression. Prepare at least two thoughtful questions per round.
  1. Overselling visual craft at the cost of product thinking. For a platform like Lybrate, the ability to think about flows, metrics, and user behaviour is as important as visual execution.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does Lybrate typically have for Product Designers?

Candidates typically report 3-4 rounds. These commonly include an initial HR or recruiter screening, a portfolio review with a design lead, a product or design challenge round, and a final conversation with a senior stakeholder or people manager. Round structure can vary, so confirm the format with your recruiter before each stage.

Is a design assignment part of the process?

Many candidates report receiving a take-home or live design challenge at some stage. Typically it involves redesigning an existing feature or solving a new product problem within a set time. Focus on your process and reasoning, not just the final output, as interviewers often ask you to walk through your decisions in detail.

What should my portfolio include for a health-tech role at Lybrate?

Include at least one project where you worked on a complex user journey or information-dense interface, as health apps often involve both. Show your research process, how you iterated, and what changed as a result. If you have health-tech work, lead with it, but strong consumer or utility app work is also well received.

What salary can I expect at Lybrate as a Product Designer?

Based on publicly reported ranges for the broader market, entry-level designers (0-2 years) typically see 6-12 LPA, mid-level (3-5 years) 14-24 LPA, and senior (6-9 years) 26-40 LPA. Lybrate's offers typically sit within these bands depending on scope and team. Always negotiate based on your specific experience and the level of the role.

How do I stand out if I do not have health-tech experience?

Show that you understand the emotional and practical stakes of health decisions. Walk through how you have designed for anxious, time-pressured, or low-literacy users in other contexts. Demonstrating domain curiosity and a clear willingness to learn the health context quickly can be as compelling as prior health-tech work on your resume.

Does Lybrate hire for remote or hybrid roles?

Candidates report a mix of in-office and hybrid arrangements depending on the team. With 3 open roles currently active, it is worth confirming the location and work-mode requirement directly with your recruiter, as this can vary by team and seniority level.
