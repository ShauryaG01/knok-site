01 Overview

Overview

Technical Program Manager (TPM) is one of the most cross-functional roles in tech: you own the end-to-end delivery of large, multi-team programs without having a direct reporting line over the engineers doing the work. Knok's job radar counted 313 active TPM openings across India as of July 2026. Bangalore leads with 41 roles, followed by Delhi (14), Pune (13), Hyderabad (12), Chennai (5), and Mumbai (1).

TPM interviews are demanding because they test three things at once: your technical credibility with engineers, your ability to drive alignment across business and product stakeholders, and your structured thinking under pressure. Candidates typically face two to four rounds covering behavioral questions, program management scenarios, technical system design concepts, and sometimes a case study or presentation. This guide walks you through what to expect and how to prepare.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions appear frequently across TPM interviews at product companies and service firms. Candidates report seeing variations of most of these regardless of company size.

  1. Walk me through a large, multi-team program you owned from kickoff to launch.
  2. How do you identify and track risks before they become blockers?
  3. Tell me about a time engineering and product had conflicting priorities. How did you resolve it?
  4. How do you handle scope creep when a program is already mid-flight?
  5. Describe your approach to dependency management across multiple workstreams.
  6. How do you measure program health in real time?
  7. Give an example of a difficult conversation you had with senior leadership.
  8. How would you design a status reporting structure for a program with several workstreams running in parallel?
  9. Describe a technical trade-off you evaluated and drove a decision on.
  10. How do you keep engineering teams accountable without micromanaging?
  11. Tell me about a program that slipped or failed. What did you do differently afterward?
  12. How do you build credibility with senior engineers when you are not the technical owner?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk me through a large, multi-team program you owned from kickoff to launch.

*Situation:* At my previous company, four engineering teams were building separate components of a new payments platform. Each team had its own roadmap, and there was no shared view of dependencies.

*Task:* My role was to align timelines, surface blockers early, and make sure every team understood how their deliverable connected to the others.

*Action:* I set up a dependency map in the first week, created a single RACI document all teams signed off on, and ran a focused weekly sync. When one team slipped on an API contract, I escalated to the engineering director immediately rather than waiting for the next milestone review.

*Result:* We launched on schedule. The platform processed transactions cleanly in the first week without a major incident, and the team leads cited the dependency map as the single most useful artifact from the program.

---

Q: Tell me about a time engineering and product had conflicting priorities. How did you resolve it?

*Situation:* Product wanted to ship a new user-facing feature by end of quarter. Engineering had just flagged a critical infrastructure migration that would consume most of their capacity for the same period.

*Task:* I had to help both sides reach a decision the business could live with, without burning either team or damaging trust between them.

*Action:* I ran a structured trade-off session with both leads, framed the choices as three clear options with stated consequences for timeline, quality, and cost. I also brought in customer support data showing the user impact of delaying the infrastructure work.

*Result:* Leadership approved a one-sprint delay on the feature and carved out a small dedicated squad for the migration. Both teams felt heard. We also avoided a production incident that would have blocked the feature launch anyway.

---

Q: Tell me about a program that slipped or failed. What did you do differently afterward?

*Situation:* I was managing an integration between two newly merged product lines. The initial scope looked well-defined, and both engineering leads were aligned.

*Task:* I needed to deliver a working integration within the quarter while coordinating engineering, data, and compliance teams.

*Action:* I underestimated the compliance review cycle and did not flag it as a risk in the first planning sprint. When compliance came back with requirements that changed the data model, we lost several weeks rebuilding.

*Result:* The program slipped by one quarter. I learned to add a compliance checkpoint to my risk register from day one on any program touching user data. Every program I ran after that included a regulatory review in the initial planning phase, and none of them slipped for the same reason.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Three frameworks cover most TPM interview questions well.

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the default for behavioral questions. Keep your Situation and Task concise: a few sentences is enough. Spend most of your answer on Action (the specific things you did, not 'we' did) and close with a concrete Result. If the result was negative, name what you learned and changed.

DACI or RACI for cross-functional alignment questions. When asked how you drive decisions across teams, explain who is the Driver, Approver, Contributor, and Informed. Interviewers want to see that you think systematically about decision rights, not just 'I talked to everyone.'

Risk register framing for program design questions. When asked how you would plan or manage a program, structure your answer around: scope clarity, dependency mapping, risk identification with probability and impact, and cadence of review. This shows you treat program management as a discipline, not just meeting facilitation.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

TPM interviewers are typically looking for four things.

Structured thinking under ambiguity. TPMs deal with incomplete information constantly. Interviewers want to see you break problems into parts, name your assumptions, and make a reasoned call rather than waiting for perfect data.

Technical credibility without ownership. You will not write the code, but you need to hold a credible conversation with senior engineers about trade-offs, architecture constraints, and technical risk. Candidates who can explain why a distributed system has eventual consistency challenges, or why a schema migration is risky mid-release, earn trust with engineering quickly.

Influence without authority. Most of your stakeholders do not report to you. Interviewers probe for specific examples of how you got alignment when you had no direct control: pre-reads, structured trade-offs, data-backed recommendations, and escalation done thoughtfully rather than reactively.

Communication that scales. A TPM is often the single person who can explain the program status to an engineer, a product manager, and a VP in the same week, using different framing for each. Show that you adapt your communication to the audience without losing accuracy.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused two-week approach works well for most candidates.

Week one: Audit your own experience first. Write down the three or four most complex programs you have owned. For each one, map the stakeholders, the main risks, the dependencies, and what went wrong. Practice turning each into a STAR story you can tell in a few minutes. Review distributed systems concepts such as consistency, availability, partition tolerance, and caching at a level where you can discuss trade-offs with an engineering lead, not just recite definitions.

Week two: Research the company. Read recent engineering blog posts, look up the company's publicly reported product launches, and understand what scale they operate at. Prepare questions for each round that show you have done this. Mock interview with a peer or a TPM you know: ask them to push back on your answers and probe your reasoning. Record yourself and watch for vague language ('we basically kind of tried to...').

A day or two before the interview: review your risk register framework, re-read the job description, and note the two or three skills they emphasize most. Map your strongest stories to those skills.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Saying 'we' instead of 'I'. Interviewers are evaluating your contribution, not your team's. When you say 'we aligned everyone' or 'we shipped on time,' you make it impossible for the interviewer to assess what you specifically did. Use 'I' and be precise about your action.

Skipping the 'so what'. Many candidates end STAR answers without a concrete result. 'We launched the program' is not a result. 'We launched on schedule and the rollout had no major incidents in the first week' is a result. Always close with what actually changed because of your work.

Over-indexing on process, not outcomes. TPM candidates often describe what process they set up (weekly syncs, dashboards, risk registers) but forget to connect the process to the outcome. The interviewer wants to know whether the process worked. Always close with what the process enabled.

Treating technical questions as out of scope. Some TPM candidates deflect technical questions with 'I'm not the engineer, I rely on my team for that.' This signals low credibility with engineering stakeholders. You do not need to write the code. You do need to explain the trade-off.

Preparing stories that are too recent or too vague. A story from your current role about something that happened last month may not have a clear result yet. Use programs with resolved outcomes, even if they are a few years old.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

What is the difference between a TPM and a regular Project Manager?

A Project Manager typically manages timelines, budgets, and resources within a defined scope. A TPM is expected to have enough technical depth to evaluate engineering trade-offs, challenge architecture decisions, and hold credible conversations with senior engineers. In practice, TPMs at most Indian product companies own programs that span multiple engineering teams and require hands-on involvement in technical planning, not just scheduling.

Do I need a coding background to become a TPM?

Most TPM job descriptions in India list a computer science degree or equivalent engineering experience as a requirement. Candidates report that active coding ability is rarely tested, but you are expected to understand system design concepts, API contracts, data models, and release processes. If your background is non-technical, you will likely need to demonstrate technical credibility through past program work involving engineering teams.

How many interview rounds should I expect for a TPM role?

Candidates typically report two to four rounds at most companies. A common pattern includes a recruiter screen, a hiring manager round focused on your background and leadership style, a panel of cross-functional stakeholders (product, engineering, design), and sometimes a case study or presentation round. Some companies add a technical system design discussion. The exact structure varies and is usually shared by the recruiter before you start.

What salary can I expect as a TPM in India?

Salary data for TPM roles in India varies widely by company stage, city, and years of experience. Glassdoor and levels.fyi list ranges that differ significantly between startups and large product companies. The knok job radar dataset for this role currently does not include enough salary data points for a reliable band. Check Glassdoor or levels.fyi for company-specific figures before your negotiation conversation.

Which city has the most TPM jobs in India right now?

Based on knok's job radar data from July 2026, Bangalore has the highest concentration of active TPM openings with 41 roles. Delhi is next with 14, followed by Pune (13), Hyderabad (12), and Chennai (5). Mumbai had 1 opening at the time of the snapshot. Remote and hybrid roles are not broken out separately in this dataset, so the total number of accessible roles may be higher.

How should I prepare for the 'tell me about a program that failed' question?

Pick a real failure where you had meaningful ownership and where the outcome taught you something specific. Avoid failures that were entirely outside your control. The best answers name exactly what you did wrong, describe the concrete impact on the program, and close with a specific change you made to how you work that you have applied since. Interviewers are looking for self-awareness and growth, not perfection.
