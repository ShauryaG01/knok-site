03 Interview Process

Interview Process

Stage 1: Resume Screening
Your resume is reviewed by a recruiter or an applicant tracking system first. Profiles that closely match the job description keywords tend to move faster. Keep your resume clean, quantify your impact where possible, and make sure your skills section reflects the exact technologies listed in the posting.

Stage 2: Recruiter Call
If your profile looks like a fit, a recruiter typically reaches out for a short introductory call. Expect questions about your current role, your notice period, and your broad expectations. This is not a technical screen. Be clear about why you are interested in Zendev specifically, and have a sense of the kind of work you want to do.

Stage 3: Technical or Domain Assessment
Most technical roles at companies like Zendev include a take-home assignment or an online coding test at this stage. For non-technical roles, this might be a case study or a writing sample. The goal is to see whether you can do the actual work, not just talk about it. Block out focused time, read the instructions carefully, and submit clean, well-commented work.

Stage 4: Technical Interview Round(s)
This is the core of the process. Candidates typically face one or two rounds of technical interviews with the hiring manager or senior team members. Expect questions on core concepts, past projects, and real-world problem-solving. For software roles, live coding or system design discussions are common. For other domains, expect case-based or scenario-driven questions.

Stage 5: Cultural or Leadership Round
Many companies at this level run a final round focused on values, communication style, and team fit. You might speak with a senior leader or a cross-functional stakeholder. Questions here are behavioural. Think about how you handle disagreement, how you prioritise under pressure, or what kind of team environment brings out your best work. Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) to keep your answers focused.

Stage 6: Offer and Negotiation
If all rounds go well, HR will send a verbal or written offer. You do not need to accept on the spot. It is normal to ask for a day or two to review, and it is fine to negotiate if the offer does not match your expectations.

04 What They Look For

What They Look For

Technical depth where it counts. Zendev, like most product and services companies in this space, values candidates who can demonstrate real command of their core domain. Listing tools on your resume is not enough. Interviewers want to see that you understand how things work at a deeper level, and that you have used these skills to solve actual problems.

Clear communication. You will be working across teams and possibly with clients or external partners. The ability to explain your thinking simply and listen actively is something interviewers notice from the very first call. Candidates who give vague or rambling answers tend to score poorly even if their technical skills are strong.

Ownership mindset. Growing tech companies look for people who do not wait to be told what to do. If you have examples of taking initiative, flagging a problem early, or going beyond your formal role to make something better, bring those into your answers.

Collaboration. Solo brilliance matters less than how you make the team around you better. Interviewers will probe for how you handle conflict, how you give and receive feedback, and whether you share credit generously.

05 How To Prepare

How To Prepare

Research the company before every round. Read Zendev's website, look at their recent news, and understand what their current products or services do. Interviewers can tell instantly whether a candidate has done homework. Knowing even a few specific things about the company helps you stand out.

Tailor your resume to each role. With 18 open roles at Zendev right now, it is tempting to send one resume everywhere. Do not. Read each job description closely and adjust your bullet points to reflect the skills and outcomes that specific role cares about most.

Practise structured answers. For behavioural rounds, prepare several STAR stories that cover a range of situations: leading under pressure, handling failure, collaborating with difficult stakeholders, and driving a result with limited resources. These stories can be adapted to many different questions.

Sharpen your core technical skills. If you are applying for a software role, practise data structures, algorithms, and the specific languages or frameworks listed in the job description. If you are in a non-technical role, revisit domain fundamentals and be ready to walk through a past project in detail.

Prepare smart questions to ask. At the end of almost every round, you will be asked if you have questions. Have two or three ready that show genuine curiosity about the team, the product, or the challenge they are solving. Asking nothing leaves a weak impression.

Follow up after each round. A short, specific thank-you note to the recruiter or interviewer, referencing something concrete from the conversation, shows professionalism and keeps you top of mind while the team is deciding.

06 Culture And Values

Culture And Values

Growth-oriented environment. Companies in Zendev's space tend to attract people who want to build, ship, and learn fast. If you thrive in environments where things change quickly and you are expected to pick up new skills on the job, this kind of culture tends to suit you well.

Collaboration over hierarchy. Many technology companies at this stage run relatively flat teams where input is welcomed from all levels. This means you can have real influence on how work gets done, but it also means you need to be comfortable speaking up and defending your ideas clearly.

High bar for quality. The hiring process itself, with multiple rounds and a technical assessment, signals that the company cares about bringing in people who will raise the standard of the team. Once inside, that expectation continues.

Candid feedback culture. Based on what candidates and employees typically report at companies of this type, feedback tends to be direct and frequent. If you prefer clear, honest feedback over polite vagueness, this is likely a comfortable fit. If you are used to more formal or hierarchical feedback structures, expect an adjustment period.
