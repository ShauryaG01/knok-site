01 Overview

Overview

Synthesia is an AI video platform that lets teams create professional videos using AI avatars and voiceovers. No cameras, no studios needed. Enterprise clients in L&D, marketing, and corporate communications use it to scale video production without technical crews. A Product Designer here shapes the tools that make AI capability feel intuitive: the video editor, avatar selection flows, template libraries, and collaboration features.

The interview process typically spans multiple rounds. Candidates report a recruiter screen, a portfolio review with a design lead, a design exercise (take-home or live), and a cross-functional panel with PMs and engineers. Synthesia currently has 78 open roles globally, signalling active growth. Expect questions about designing for AI complexity, enterprise users, and how you balance shipping speed with design quality.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions are commonly reported by candidates who have interviewed for Product Designer roles at Synthesia. Prepare a specific story for each one.

  1. Walk us through a product or feature you took from discovery to launch. What decisions did you make, and what would you do differently?
  2. Synthesia serves solo marketers and large enterprise L&D teams. How do you design when your users are this varied?
  3. AI features like avatar customisation and voice cloning can feel opaque. How do you make something technically complex feel simple for a first-time user?
  4. Tell us about a time you disagreed with a PM or stakeholder on a design direction. How did you handle it?
  5. How do you decide when a design is ready to ship?
  6. Describe a design decision that did not work out as expected. What happened and what did you take from it?
  7. How would you redesign the onboarding flow for someone who has never made an AI video before?
  8. How do you measure whether a design is working after it goes live?
  9. Walk us through how you run user research when time or budget is limited.
  10. How do you communicate your design rationale to engineers or PMs who may not share your visual intuitions?
  11. Synthesia competes in a fast-moving space. How does competitive analysis fit into your design process?
  12. Trust is a real concern in AI-generated video. How would you design a feature that builds user confidence in AI content?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format for every behavioural question: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Aim for two to three minutes when spoken aloud. Spend the most time on Action and Result.

---

Q: Describe a design decision that did not work out as expected.

*Situation:* I was a product designer at a SaaS company, working on a bulk-upload feature for a content library.

*Task:* The goal was to reduce the time teams spent loading large batches of assets into the platform.

*Action:* I designed a drag-and-drop interface with inline progress indicators. Deadline pressure led me to skip a usability testing round and rely on my own judgment plus a quick PM review instead.

*Result:* After launch, internally tracked upload completion rates dropped. Users were abandoning mid-upload because they could not tell if the process had stalled or was still running. I ran five user interviews within a week, updated the progress UI with clearer state feedback, and completion rates recovered. The lesson: even one usability test would have caught this.

---

Q: How do you design when your users are very different from each other?

*Situation:* At a previous company, our product was used by individual freelancers and by enterprise procurement teams, sometimes within the same workflow.

*Task:* I was asked to redesign a permissions screen that both segments needed to use regularly.

*Action:* I ran separate research sessions with each user group, mapped their mental models side by side, and identified the overlap. I designed a single interface with sensible defaults for solo users and an expandable advanced panel for enterprise admins. Both groups tested the design before it reached engineering.

*Result:* Post-launch surveys showed both segments found the task straightforward. Internally tracked support tickets related to permissions fell in the following quarter.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you pushed back on a stakeholder request.

*Situation:* A senior marketing leader wanted a promotional banner added to the product dashboard to drive upsell.

*Task:* My responsibility was to advocate for the user experience while still taking the business goal seriously.

*Action:* I pulled session recordings and showed that the dashboard was the first surface users hit after login, exactly when they were trying to complete a task. A promotional interrupt there would create friction at the worst moment. I proposed a contextual upsell trigger shown after a user completed a video export, when they were in a positive mindset.

*Result:* The stakeholder agreed to the alternative placement. Internally tracked click-through outperformed the banner in earlier tests on other pages, and user satisfaction in that session flow held steady.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result): The core structure for behavioural questions. Spend the most time on Action (what you specifically did, not what 'the team' did) and Result (a specific, observable outcome, not just 'it went well').

'How Might We' for design prompts: When given an open-ended design challenge, reframe it as a 'How might we help [user] achieve [goal]?' before jumping to solutions. This signals that you start from user needs, not from features.

Decision log for portfolio walkthroughs: For every major design choice, prepare to answer: what options did you consider, why did you choose this one, and what did you rule out? Interviewers at product-led companies want to follow your thinking, not just admire your screens.

Constraint framing for design exercises: At the start of any live or take-home exercise, name the constraints out loud: who is the user, what is the business goal, what are the technical or time limits? This prevents designing in a vacuum and demonstrates structured thinking.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Synthesia builds AI tools used daily by non-technical people. Based on what candidates report, interviewers typically look for these qualities:

Comfort with AI complexity. Can you make a technically sophisticated feature feel obvious? Show portfolio work where you translated something hard into something simple for a non-expert.

Systems thinking. The Synthesia product spans many surfaces: the editor, templates, sharing, permissions, and integrations. Designers who think about how one decision affects adjacent flows tend to stand out.

Speed balanced with rigour. It is a scale-up pace. Show that you can ship and iterate, but also that you know when slowing down for research is the right call.

Cross-functional fluency. Prepare for questions about working with PMs and engineers. Show that you can communicate rationale clearly and that you have navigated disagreement professionally.

User empathy with business awareness. Strong candidates advocate for users and understand the trade-offs the business is making. Bring both perspectives into your answers.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused two-week approach based on what candidates typically report from Synthesia interviews:

Week 1: Research and portfolio polish

Spend real time using the Synthesia product as a user would. Note what feels smooth and where friction appears. Read publicly available product announcements and case studies from 2024-2026. Select two to three portfolio pieces that show: designing for AI or technical complexity, a B2B or enterprise context, and outcomes you can speak to. Write the STAR narrative for each piece before you even open Figma.

Week 2: Practice and exercise preparation

Practise talking through each portfolio piece aloud. Keep each story to five minutes. Time yourself. Run one timed design exercise using a prompt such as 'Redesign the template discovery experience for a new Synthesia user.' Present it back to yourself as if speaking to a cross-functional team. Prepare specific answers to each of the twelve questions listed above. Record yourself if it helps you catch vague language or filler words.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Showing screens instead of decisions. Clicking through a Figma prototype without explaining trade-offs leaves interviewers guessing. Lead with context and choices, then show the visuals.

Vague results. Saying 'users loved it' or 'the launch went well' is not enough. Use whatever specific signal you have: an internally tracked metric, a support-ticket reduction, a user research quote, or a stakeholder decision that validated your approach.

Ignoring business context. Synthesia is a commercial product with revenue goals. Design thinking that ignores constraints can read as inexperience at the product-led company level.

Skipping clarifying questions in a design exercise. Spend the first few minutes asking about users, constraints, and success criteria before touching the canvas. Jumping to solutions without discovery is a common signal of junior thinking.

Treating the portfolio review as a one-way presentation. It is a conversation. Pause, invite questions, and be ready to defend or revise your thinking when challenged. Candidates who freeze when pushed back on rarely advance.

Underestimating the cross-functional round. Candidates report that PMs and engineers also interview designers at Synthesia. Prepare for questions about design handoff, feedback loops, and how you handle disagreement, not just design craft.

Frequently asked

How many interview rounds does Synthesia typically have for a Product Designer?

Candidates typically report three to four rounds: a recruiter screen, a portfolio review with a design lead or hiring manager, a design exercise (take-home or live), and a final cross-functional panel. The exact sequence can vary by team and seniority level. Ask your recruiter at the outset to confirm the format for your specific role.

Is there a take-home design assignment?

Many candidates report receiving a design exercise, either as a take-home task or a timed session during the interview itself. Prompts often involve simplifying a complex flow or designing for a new user type. Treat it as a real project: define the problem, state your constraints, show your process, and present a clear recommendation with trade-offs acknowledged.

What salary can a Product Designer expect at Synthesia in India?

Synthesia is a UK-headquartered company with roles across geographies, so compensation varies by location and seniority. For India-based Product Designer roles, knok's job radar shows the broad market range is 14-24 LPA at mid-level (3-5 years experience) and 26-40 LPA at senior level (6-9 years). Specific Synthesia figures for India are not publicly confirmed; check Glassdoor or levels.fyi for reported ranges before entering a negotiation.

Does Synthesia care more about visual craft or product thinking?

Based on what candidates report, Synthesia values product thinking at least as much as visual craft. Expect interviewers to probe your decision-making, your research process, and how you handle trade-offs and ambiguity. Strong visual skills matter, but candidates who cannot explain why they made design choices tend not to progress past the portfolio review round.

How should I tailor my portfolio for Synthesia specifically?

Prioritise work that shows you have designed for complexity or for non-technical users. If you have B2B, SaaS, or AI-adjacent case studies, lead with those. For each piece, prepare to speak to: the user problem, the constraints you worked within, the options you considered, what you shipped, and what happened after launch. Synthesia builds tools for enterprise teams, so familiarity with that context is a genuine advantage.
