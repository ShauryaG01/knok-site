01 Overview

Overview

Asana is a work management platform used by teams at thousands of companies worldwide, and the Product Design team shapes how those users experience every feature. 02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Based on candidate reports and Asana's publicly stated values, these are the questions most likely to come up:

  1. Walk me through a project in your portfolio where you balanced user needs with business constraints.
  2. How do you approach designing for accessibility and inclusion in a B2B product used by diverse global teams?
  3. Describe a time you disagreed with a PM or engineer about a design direction. How did you resolve it?
  4. Asana's mission is to help humanity thrive by enabling teams to work effortlessly. How does your personal design philosophy connect to this?
  5. Tell me about a feature or design you shipped that did not perform as expected. What did you learn?
  6. How do you know when a design is ready to hand off to engineering?
  7. Walk me through how you would approach redesigning a core Asana workflow, such as task creation or the project timeline view.
  8. How do you use qualitative and quantitative research to validate design decisions at different stages of the process?
  9. Describe how you manage competing design priorities across multiple projects or product areas.
  10. How do you structure and give design critique? How do you receive it when you disagree with the feedback?
  11. Tell me about a time you simplified a genuinely complex UX problem. What was your process?
  12. How do you stay current with design trends without letting them drive your decisions?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk me through a project where you balanced user needs with business constraints.

*Situation:* I was the lead designer on a dashboard redesign at my previous company. User research showed that most users were overwhelmed by the number of widgets and were abandoning the customisation flow entirely.

*Task:* My job was to simplify the experience while ensuring the sales team could still demonstrate the product's depth to enterprise prospects. Removing features outright was not an option.

*Action:* I ran a card-sorting exercise with a group of users to understand which widgets felt essential versus 'nice to have.' I proposed a progressive disclosure model: a clean default view showing a handful of core metrics, and an 'expand' option for power users. I ran three rounds of usability testing with both new and experienced users, iterating on the toggle mechanism each time. I also presented the business case to the PM, showing that a simpler default would reduce onboarding drop-off without hiding advanced features.

*Result:* The redesign shipped in Q3. Onboarding completion improved by a publicly reported margin our PM shared with the team, and the enterprise demo flow remained intact. The sales team adopted the advanced view as a 'wow moment' in demos.

---

Q: Tell me about a feature you shipped that did not perform as expected.

*Situation:* I designed a new notification centre for a SaaS product. The goal was to reduce email overload by surfacing in-app alerts.

*Task:* I had to design an experience that was noticeable but not intrusive, and that would actually change user behaviour rather than be ignored.

*Action:* I used a red badge count on the bell icon, a pattern common across most apps. I ran a quick prototype test with a small group of users, all of whom said they understood it. We shipped it with confidence.

*Result:* Post-launch analytics showed click-through on the notification centre was lower than expected. A follow-up session with several users revealed that the badge felt like 'just another notification I can ignore.' I had tested comprehension but not motivation. I went back and added contextual nudges at the point of action, which improved engagement. The lesson I carried forward: validate behaviour, not just understanding.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you simplified a complex UX problem.

*Situation:* A project management tool I worked on had a permissions system that let admins set access at the workspace, project, and task level. Support tickets about permissions were among the top three issue categories every month.

*Task:* I needed to redesign the permissions UI so admins could confidently set up access without creating accidental data leaks or having to call support.

*Action:* I started by mapping every possible permissions state (there were dozens of combinations). I interviewed several admins and found that most operated in just three common patterns: full access for team members, view-only for clients, and restricted access for contractors. I proposed a 'role template' model that defaulted admins into one of three presets, with an 'advanced' escape hatch for custom configurations. I used plain-language labels ('Clients can view but not edit') instead of technical terms. I tested the new flow with a small group of admins who had never seen it before, and all of them completed setup correctly on the first try.

*Result:* After launch, permissions-related support tickets dropped significantly according to the product team's quarterly report. The advanced mode was used by a small fraction of admins, confirming that most only needed the simplified path.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions
Every behavioural question at Asana is best answered with a clear Situation, Task, Action, Result structure. Interviewers are listening for your specific contribution, not the team's. Use 'I' not 'we' when describing what you personally decided and did. If you say 'we decided,' follow it immediately with 'my specific role was...'

'Why, What, How' for design decisions
When explaining a design choice, lead with the user problem (Why), describe the solution (What), then explain the craft decisions (How). This shows you start from insight, not aesthetics. Asana interviewers consistently probe on the 'why' first, so lead with it rather than waiting to be asked.

User, Business, Constraints triangle for case questions
For redesign or whiteboard exercises, map all three dimensions first: who is the user and what is their goal, what does the business need from this feature, and what are the constraints (time, platform, accessibility, data). Candidates report that Asana interviewers look for designers who hold all three in mind before reaching for a solution, not those who jump straight to sketching screens.

Feedback framing
When asked about handling critique, use this structure: what you heard, what you considered, and what you changed or kept (and why). Showing that you can disagree without dismissing demonstrates the collaborative mindset Asana looks for in its design team.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Asana Product Designer interviewers typically look for four things, based on what candidates report from past rounds.

Systems thinking over pixel pushing. Asana is a complex, interconnected product. Interviewers want to see that you can think about how a change in one part of the product ripples into another, not just how a single screen looks. Bring examples where your design decisions accounted for edge cases or downstream effects on other workflows.

Clear articulation of trade-offs. You will be asked why you made specific choices. Saying 'it looked cleaner' is not enough. Candidates who can explain the user insight behind a decision, the alternative they considered, and the reason they chose one path over another consistently report positive signals from the panel.

Ownership and honesty about failure. Asana's culture values directness and personal accountability. If you cannot recall a project that did not go perfectly, interviewers typically probe harder. Owning a mistake and showing what changed because of it is more compelling than a string of perfect success stories.

Cross-functional collaboration instincts. Product Designers at Asana work closely with PMs and engineers from the start of a project, not just at handoff. Interviewers will probe whether you see collaboration as a nice-to-have or as a core part of your design process from day one.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Two to three weeks before
Start using Asana as a free user every day. Map the core workflows: task creation, project setup, inbox, timeline view. Identify at least one friction point you would genuinely improve and be ready to walk through your approach. Read Asana's publicly available product blog and announcements to understand their recent direction before your portfolio round.

One to two weeks before
Prepare three to four portfolio case studies that show your end-to-end process: discovery, problem definition, design iteration, and measurable outcome. Each story should take no more than ten minutes to tell. Practice saying them out loud, not reading from slides. If a story has no failure moment, go deeper until you find one.

The week before
Do a live mock interview with a friend or peer designer. Ask them to interrupt you with 'why did you choose that?' at least twice per story. This prepares you for the probe questions that typically follow portfolio walkthroughs at Asana. Prepare at least three specific questions for the panel, focused on how design and product share ownership of the roadmap, or how the team handles conflicting research signals.

On the day
For take-home exercises, candidates report that Asana values a clearly structured thinking process over a polished final design. Show your working. Annotate your screens. Be explicit about what you would explore next given more time. A rough wireframe with a well-argued rationale will typically score higher than a polished mockup with shallow reasoning.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping the 'why' in portfolio walkthroughs. The most common mistake candidates report is narrating what they designed without first explaining the user problem that made it necessary. Start every case study with the problem, not the solution.

Treating the design exercise as a visual test. Asana interviewers are evaluating your thinking process, not your visual execution. A rough wireframe with a well-argued rationale typically scores higher than a polished mockup with shallow reasoning. Show your assumptions and your working at every step.

Overusing 'we' in behavioural answers. Collaboration is valued, but interviewers need to understand your individual contribution. When you say 'we decided,' follow it immediately with 'my specific role was...' Otherwise your personal impact becomes invisible to the panel.

Not preparing questions for the panel. Arriving without thoughtful questions signals low engagement with the role. Generic questions like 'what does a typical day look like' are forgettable. Prepare at least three questions specific to Asana's product challenges or design team culture.

Underestimating the mission alignment question. Asana's values are not a formality in their interviews. Candidates who cannot connect their own design philosophy to Asana's mission in a genuine, specific way report receiving weaker feedback from the cultural rounds. Use the product before your interview and bring a real observation.

Presenting only successes. Asana interviewers actively look for evidence of learning from failure. If every project in your portfolio went perfectly, revisit your case studies and surface one honest setback and what changed because of it.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many interview rounds does Asana typically have for Product Designers?

Candidates report a process that typically includes a recruiter screen, a portfolio review with a hiring manager or senior designer, a design exercise (take-home or live), and one or two panel rounds with cross-functional partners. The number of rounds can vary by seniority and which team you are joining. Plan for the full process to span several weeks, and confirm the structure with your recruiter at the start so you can pace your preparation.

Does Asana give a take-home design exercise or a live whiteboard?

Many candidates report receiving a take-home brief, though some also report live exercises depending on the team and role level. When it is a take-home, the debrief conversation typically matters as much as the artefact itself. Focus on showing your thinking clearly: annotate your screens, state your assumptions, and be explicit about what you would explore next if you had more time.

What salary can I expect as a Product Designer at Asana in India?

Based on knok jobradar data, Product Designer roles in India range from 6-12 LPA at entry level (0-2 years), 14-24 LPA at mid level (3-5 years), 26-40 LPA at senior level (6-9 years), and 36-55+ LPA at Lead/Principal level. For Asana-specific offer numbers, Glassdoor and levels.fyi are useful references, though sample sizes for India-specific Asana data can be small, so treat those figures as directional.

How important is Figma proficiency for the Asana interview?

Asana's design team uses Figma as their primary design tool, and candidates report that proficiency is expected rather than optional. For take-home exercises, submitting work in Figma and keeping your file well-organised is itself a signal of professional practice. That said, interviewers are primarily evaluating your design thinking and decision-making, not your component library or motion polish.

How do I prepare for the Asana mission alignment question?

Read Asana's publicly stated mission and spend time using the product as an actual user before your interview, not just as a researcher. Identify a real workflow you found genuinely useful or a friction point you would genuinely want to improve. Candidates who bring specific, personal observations about the product consistently report stronger reactions from the panel than those who answer with generic statements about 'collaborative work.'

How long does the Asana Product Designer hiring process take?

Candidates report that the full process typically spans several weeks from the first recruiter call to an offer. Take-home exercises often have a one-week turnaround window. If you are managing competing offers, candidates report that Asana recruiters are generally responsive when you communicate a deadline directly and early in the process, though experiences vary by team and hiring manager.
