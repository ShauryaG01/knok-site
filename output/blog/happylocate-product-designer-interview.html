01 Overview

Overview

happylocate is a tech-enabled relocation and home-services platform that helps people move homes with less hassle. With 22 open Product Designer roles as of mid-2026, it is one of the more active design-hiring companies in the Indian startup space right now. Candidates typically go through a portfolio review, one or two design exercises, and a few rounds of interviews covering design thinking, cross-functional collaboration, and product sense.

The role sits at a meaningful intersection: users on happylocate are often anxious (moving is one of life's more stressful events), so the design bar is high for clarity, trust, and clear guidance at every step. If you are a mid-level or senior designer, expect questions that probe how you simplify complex multi-step service flows and how you make decisions when data and instinct point in different directions.

Across the broader market, knok data shows 393 Product Designer jobs live in India right now, with Bangalore leading at 62 openings. Salary bands run from 6-12 LPA at entry level (0-2 years) to 36-55+ LPA at Lead or Principal level, though actual offers depend on company stage and your specific skill set.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through your strongest portfolio project. What was the problem, what did you try first, and what did you ship?

*Situation:* I was the sole designer on a B2C insurance app where users were dropping off at the premium summary screen. Drop-off was high enough that the growth team flagged it as a top priority.

*Task:* My job was to find out why users were leaving and redesign that screen to improve conversions without hiding any information.

*Action:* I ran five user interviews and found that the pricing breakdown felt opaque and trust was low. I redesigned the screen to lead with a plain-language summary, moved the detailed breakdown into an expandable section, and added a short 'why this price' explainer. I then ran an A/B test with the engineering team.

*Result:* The redesigned screen improved form completion noticeably in our internal funnel data, and trust-related support tickets dropped in the following month. The key lesson was that users did not need less information. They needed it in a better order.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you disagreed with a stakeholder on a design direction. What happened?

*Situation:* A senior product manager at my previous company wanted to add a promotional banner on the checkout screen because marketing was pushing for it during a sale season.

*Task:* My concern was that the banner would distract users who were already in the act of paying and might increase cart abandonment.

*Action:* Instead of just saying no, I pulled together session recordings and heatmap data showing where users' attention was during checkout. I proposed a compromise: a slim, dismissable ribbon at the top rather than a full-width banner. I also suggested we A/B test both versions so the decision would be data-backed, not opinion-based.

*Result:* The PM agreed to test both. The slim ribbon version kept conversion stable while still surfacing the promotion. That experience taught me to always bring evidence to a design disagreement, not just a design instinct.

---

Q: Describe a time when user feedback caused you to completely change a design you had already committed to.

*Situation:* I had designed a multi-step onboarding flow for a home services app. The flow tested well in moderated sessions, so we shipped it.

*Task:* Two weeks after launch, analytics showed a steep drop at step three. I needed to understand what was happening and fix it quickly.

*Action:* I ran five quick unmoderated tests using a remote tool and found that step three asked for information (floor plan dimensions) that most users simply did not have on hand. I went back to the product manager, argued for cutting that step entirely and replacing it with an optional 'add later' path, and redesigned the flow in a week.

*Result:* Drop-off at that step fell noticeably after the fix shipped, based on our internal funnel data. The lesson was that lab testing does not always catch real-world context gaps, like users on a commute who do not have documents in front of them.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For portfolio and case study questions, use the Double Diamond structure: describe how you defined the problem (research, insights), then how you developed and narrowed solutions (ideation, testing, iteration). Interviewers at product companies respond well to hearing what you cut, not just what you shipped.

For behavioral questions ('tell us about a time...'), use STAR: Situation (brief context), Task (your specific responsibility), Action (what you actually did, in first person), Result (measurable or observable outcome). Keep Situation short. Spend most of your time on Action.

For product thinking questions ('how would you improve X'), use a simple Jobs-to-be-Done lens: start by naming the user and what they are trying to accomplish, identify where the current experience breaks down, propose one or two focused solutions, then say how you would measure success. Avoid redesigning the entire product when asked about one flow.

For 'how do you prioritize' questions, mention a lightweight framework: impact on user vs. effort to build, alignment with the current business goal, and what data exists to support the assumption. Showing that you can say no to low-impact work is as important as showing you can design fast.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates report and the nature of the product, happylocate interviewers typically look for a few specific qualities.

Empathy for high-stress users. Relocation is not a casual transaction. Designers who can articulate how to reduce cognitive load and build trust at key moments (pricing, confirmation, support access) stand out. If you have worked on fintech, healthcare, or any product where users feel vulnerable, draw those parallels clearly.

Clear design process, not just polished screens. Interviewers want to see how you think. A well-articulated case study that shows research, iteration, and tradeoffs beats a polished Figma file with no story behind it.

Collaboration and communication skills. happylocate is a growth-stage startup, which typically means small teams, fast sprints, and frequent scope changes. Show that you can work closely with PMs and engineers without friction, and that you can explain design rationale to non-designers.

Business awareness. Product Designers at startups are expected to understand conversion, retention, and unit economics at a basic level. You do not need to be a data analyst, but you should be comfortable asking 'what does success look like' before you start designing.

Low-tech user sensitivity. happylocate's user base spans multiple Indian cities and income levels. Candidates who have designed for users with limited digital experience, or who can speak to mobile-first and vernacular design considerations, tend to be valued.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

One week before the interview:

Spend time actually using the happylocate app or website as a new user. Map out the key flows: discovery, booking, tracking, and support. Note two or three specific moments where the experience could improve, and form a point of view on why. Interviewers often ask 'what would you change about our product' and a specific, evidence-backed answer beats a generic one.

Pick two or three portfolio case studies to prepare in depth. Choose projects that show research, iteration, and measurable outcomes. If you do not have metrics, prepare to speak to qualitative signals like 'user testing showed' or 'support tickets dropped.'

Three to five days before:

Practice walking through each case study in ten minutes. Record yourself if you can. The most common portfolio mistake is spending too long on background and running out of time to explain the impact.

Prepare STAR answers for the most common behavioral questions: a stakeholder disagreement, a time you used data to change a decision, and a time you simplified a complex flow.

Day before:

Review salary bands for your experience level on Glassdoor or levels.fyi before you go in. Know your number before the conversation reaches compensation.

Prepare two or three questions to ask the interviewers. Strong options include: 'What does the design team's relationship with product management look like today?' and 'What is the biggest unsolved design problem on the product right now?'

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Showing screens without showing process. A portfolio deck full of final UI with no research, no iteration, and no outcome data will not land well at a product-focused company. Always show what you started with, what changed, and why.

Vague answers to behavioral questions. Saying 'I always collaborate well with engineers' is not an answer. Interviewers want a specific story. Prepare concrete examples for each behavioral question type.

Redesigning the whole product when asked about one screen. When asked 'how would you improve our onboarding,' pick one problem, go deep, and propose one focused solution. Breadth-first answers suggest shallow thinking.

Not asking clarifying questions during a design exercise. In a live design exercise, jumping straight into solutions without asking about the user, the constraint, or the goal is a red flag. Take two minutes to ask questions first.

Ignoring mobile-first and low-bandwidth contexts. happylocate's users include people booking moves from smaller cities or on mid-range Android devices. Designs that assume a high-end device or fast internet will draw questions in the interview.

Under-preparing on metrics. Candidates who cannot answer 'how would you measure if this design worked' struggle at growth-stage startups. For every design in your portfolio, prepare a one-sentence answer on how success was or would be measured.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does happylocate typically have for a Product Designer role?

Candidates report a process that typically includes an initial screening call, a portfolio review round, one or more design exercises (sometimes a take-home, sometimes a live whiteboard session), and a final culture or leadership round. The exact number of rounds varies, so confirm the process with your recruiter at the start. Plan for three to four touchpoints in your schedule.

What salary can I expect as a Product Designer at happylocate?

Salary depends heavily on experience. Market data shows bands of 6-12 LPA for entry-level (0-2 years), 14-24 LPA for mid-level (3-5 years), 26-40 LPA for senior (6-9 years), and 36-55+ LPA for Lead or Principal designers. For a specific happylocate offer, check recent data on Glassdoor or levels.fyi, as startup compensation can shift with funding stage and role scope.

Does happylocate give a take-home design assignment?

Many candidates report receiving a take-home assignment at some point in the process, typically involving redesigning a flow or proposing a new feature. Such assignments are commonly cited as having a two-to-four-hour time box, so focus on demonstrating your process and reasoning rather than pixel-perfect execution. Bring the finished assignment to the next round and be ready to walk through your decisions out loud.

What tools should I know for a happylocate Product Designer interview?

Figma is the standard expectation at most Indian product startups right now. Familiarity with prototyping in Figma, basic usability testing tools, and an ability to present your work clearly matters more than knowing every tool in the market. Be ready to discuss how you use analytics tools like Mixpanel or Amplitude at a conceptual level, since data-informed design is a priority at growth-stage companies.

How important is domain knowledge in relocation or home services?

Domain knowledge helps but is not a hard requirement. Interviewers typically value your ability to quickly build empathy with unfamiliar users over prior category experience. If you have worked in any service-heavy or logistics-adjacent product (food delivery, healthcare, travel), draw those parallels clearly in your answers.

Is the happylocate design interview more portfolio-focused or more product thinking?

Candidates report it is a mix of both. You will almost certainly do a deep portfolio walkthrough, but expect product thinking questions alongside it: 'how would you improve X,' 'how would you measure Y,' and 'what would you build next.' Prepare for both modes rather than treating it as a purely visual or purely strategic interview.
