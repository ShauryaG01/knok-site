02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These are the questions candidates most commonly report from Pure Storage Data Analyst interviews. Expect a mix of technical, behavioural, and product-thinking questions across multiple rounds.

  1. Walk me through a complex analysis you did end-to-end: from raw data to a recommendation that got acted on.
  1. How do you handle messy or incomplete data? Give a specific example of a time you had to make a judgement call about data quality.
  1. Pure Storage products generate large volumes of performance telemetry. How would you approach designing a dashboard to help a customer success team spot at-risk accounts?
  1. Write a SQL query to find the top accounts by storage usage growth over recent quarters. How would you optimise it for a large table?
  1. Tell me about a time you disagreed with a stakeholder about what the data actually showed. How did you handle it?
  1. How would you measure whether a new product feature is successful? Walk me through the metrics you would choose and why.
  1. Describe a situation where you had to translate a complex finding into a simple message for a non-technical audience.
  1. How do you prioritise when multiple teams all want analysis from you at the same time?
  1. Pure Storage operates in a competitive enterprise storage market. If you had access to customer usage data, how would you identify early warning signals of churn?
  1. What is your experience with Python or R for analysis beyond what SQL can do? Give a concrete example.
  1. Tell me about a dashboard or report you built that people actually used regularly. What made it stick?
  1. How do you stay current with new tools and techniques in data analytics, and how do you decide when to adopt something new?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk me through a complex analysis you did end-to-end.

*Situation:* Our sales ops team was seeing inconsistent win rates across regions but had no clear explanation. The data lived in multiple separate CRM exports with different field definitions.

*Task:* I was asked to find out whether the variance was real or an artefact of how different teams logged deals, and if real, what was driving it.

*Action:* I cleaned and unified the source files in Python, standardising deal stage labels and normalising the time periods. I then built a cohort analysis in SQL comparing win rates by deal size, product line, sales rep tenure, and region. I found that the apparent regional gap nearly disappeared when controlling for deal size, but one region had a genuine lag in enterprise deals above a certain threshold.

*Result:* The sales director redirected enterprise-focused enablement to that region. The team adopted the analysis approach as a standard input to future regional planning, and my manager used it as a template for other market reviews.

---

Q: How would you design a dashboard for a customer success team to spot at-risk accounts?

*Situation:* At my previous company, customer success managers were reacting to churn after it happened rather than preventing it.

*Task:* I was asked to build a leading-indicator dashboard that gave them a weekly view of account health.

*Action:* I interviewed a group of CSMs to understand what signals they already used informally, then mapped those to available data: product login frequency, support ticket volume and severity, feature adoption trends, and days since last executive sponsor contact. I built a composite health score in our BI tool, weighted by what correlated most with past churn in our historical data, and added a colour-coded account list sorted by risk tier.

*Result:* CSMs reported the dashboard became their first stop every Monday. The team adopted it fully within the first month of launch, and account review conversations became more focused on specific signals rather than gut feel.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you disagreed with a stakeholder about what the data showed.

*Situation:* A product manager was convinced that a new onboarding flow had improved activation rates, based on a before-and-after comparison she had run herself.

*Task:* My job was to validate her analysis before it went into a leadership review.

*Action:* I noticed the comparison window overlapped with a marketing campaign that had brought in a higher-intent user cohort. I re-ran the analysis controlling for acquisition source and showed that for organic users, activation rates had not changed. I presented both cuts of the data side by side and walked her through the attribution issue without framing it as her being wrong.

*Result:* She appreciated the rigour, updated the deck to reflect the nuance, and we agreed to run a proper A/B test for the next iteration. The relationship stayed strong because I treated it as a shared problem, not a correction.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

The STAR structure (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the standard for behavioural questions and works well for Pure Storage interviews. Keep the Situation and Task brief, spend most of your time on Action (what you specifically did, not what 'we' did), and end with a concrete Result. If you do not have a measurable number, describe the decision or change your work enabled.

For technical and case questions, use a structured problem-solving approach:

  1. Restate the problem in your own words to confirm alignment.
  2. Ask a clarifying question or two about data availability and the business goal before diving in.
  3. Break the problem into components: data sources, metrics to define, analysis approach, and output format.
  4. Walk the interviewer through your thinking out loud as you go.

For SQL questions, write clean and readable code first, then discuss optimisation. Interviewers at data-driven companies like Pure Storage care whether you understand indexing, query plans, and the cost of joins on large tables, so proactively mentioning these shows depth.

For product and metrics questions, anchor on the business outcome first (what decision does this metric need to support?) before listing KPIs. Candidates who jump to a generic framework without tying it to the specific context often get pushed back.

On hedging your numbers: if you cannot recall an exact figure from a past role, say 'I do not recall the exact number but the direction was clear' rather than inventing a figure. Interviewers respect intellectual honesty.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates report from Pure Storage Data Analyst interviews, the hiring team is looking for a combination of technical depth, business sense, and clear communication.

Technical depth without being a black box. Pure Storage works with complex, high-volume data from storage systems and customer environments. Interviewers want to see that you can write efficient SQL, work with Python for analysis beyond basic queries, and think critically about data quality. But they also want you to explain your reasoning, not just produce an answer.

Business orientation. Candidates report that Pure Storage interviewers frequently ask 'so what?' after a technical answer. They want analysts who connect findings to decisions, not just analysts who can query a database. Being able to frame your work in terms of revenue impact, customer retention, or product improvement matters.

Structured thinking under ambiguity. Enterprise storage is a complex domain. Interviewers will often give you an open-ended problem with incomplete information to see whether you ask good clarifying questions and structure your approach before jumping to an answer.

Stakeholder credibility. The role involves working across sales, product, and engineering teams. Candidates who can show examples of influencing decisions without direct authority, navigating pushback on their findings, and communicating technical results to non-technical audiences tend to score well.

Curiosity about the domain. Showing genuine interest in how Pure Storage's products work, what problems their customers face, and how data fits into the company's strategy signals that you will ramp quickly and contribute beyond just completing tickets.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Understand Pure Storage's business. Read their investor materials, recent earnings commentary (publicly available), and product pages. Understand what all-flash storage means for enterprise customers, who their main competitors are, and what metrics matter in their market: customer retention, product adoption, and storage utilisation. This domain knowledge will make your case answers far more credible.

Step 2: Sharpen your SQL. Candidates report SQL tests covering window functions, CTEs, joins across multiple tables, and query optimisation. Practise writing queries from scratch without autocomplete. Focus on problems involving ranking, deduplication, and time-series aggregation, as these come up frequently in storage and SaaS analytics contexts.

Step 3: Prepare several strong stories. Map your past experience to the most common question themes: end-to-end analysis, data quality challenges, stakeholder disagreements, prioritisation under pressure, and dashboard or report design. Write out the STAR structure for each so you are not constructing it on the spot.

Step 4: Practise product and metrics thinking. Pick a Pure Storage product such as FlashArray or FlashBlade and think through what data you would collect, what metrics would indicate success, and how you would detect early warning signals of a problem. This preparation maps directly to questions candidates report receiving.

Step 5: Prepare smart questions for the interviewers. Ask about the data stack the team uses today, the biggest analytical challenges they are currently working on, and what success looks like in the first few months for someone joining this role. Good questions signal genuine interest and help you assess whether the team is a strong fit.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Being too vague in behavioural answers. Saying 'I worked on a project to improve data quality' without specifics leaves the interviewer with nothing to assess. Name the tool, describe the actual problem, and explain the specific action you took.

Jumping to SQL before understanding the business question. When given a case or technical question, candidates who immediately start writing queries often miss the point. Take a moment to confirm what decision the analysis is meant to support.

Overclaiming results. If you improved something, say by how much, or be honest that you do not have the exact figure. Inventing numbers or staying vague with 'it helped a lot' both hurt your credibility.

Ignoring data quality and edge cases. Strong data analysts proactively mention the assumptions they made and the edge cases they would check. Candidates who present an analysis without any caveats come across as inexperienced.

Not knowing Pure Storage's domain. Candidates who cannot speak at all to what all-flash storage is, or who are unclear on whether Pure Storage is primarily a hardware or software company, signal low motivation. A small amount of research has a disproportionately large impact on how you are perceived.

Weak questions at the end. Generic questions after a technical interview signal that you were not fully engaged. Prepare specific questions based on what you actually learned during the interview itself.

How many rounds does the Pure Storage Data Analyst interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically involves multiple rounds: a recruiter screen, one or more technical rounds covering SQL and analytical thinking, a case or take-home assignment, and a hiring manager conversation. The exact structure can vary by team and level. Confirm the full process with your recruiter after the first call so you can prepare the right way.

Does Pure Storage give a take-home SQL or analytics assignment?

Some candidates report receiving a take-home exercise involving SQL queries or a short analysis task, while others report the technical assessment is done live during a video call. It appears to vary by team and the seniority of the role. Practising SQL under time pressure and being able to explain your reasoning out loud will serve you well in either format.
