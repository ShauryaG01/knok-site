01 Overview

Overview

Vegapay builds credit card issuance and management infrastructure for banks and NBFCs in India. The platform handles card lifecycle management, transaction processing, and partner integrations, meaning engineers work on systems where reliability and regulatory compliance are as important as feature velocity.

With 26 open roles currently listed, vegapay is clearly in an active growth phase. An Engineering Manager here typically owns one or more squads working on API platforms, card processing pipelines, or partner integration layers. You are expected to bring both technical credibility and strong people management, since these teams handle real-money flows with strict uptime expectations from regulated partners.

Candidates report a process that typically spans 3-5 rounds, covering system design, behavioural leadership questions, people management scenarios, and a conversation with senior leadership. The salary band for Engineering Manager roles in fintech infrastructure sits at 35-60 LPA at this tier, with Senior Manager positions ranging from 55-90 LPA.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up frequently in vegapay EM interviews, based on what candidates report and the nature of their card infrastructure business:

  1. How have you managed a team delivering payment or financial infrastructure where an outage has direct monetary consequences for end users or partner banks?
  2. Vegapay's platform sits between banks, NBFCs, and end consumers. How do you handle engineering priorities when your customers are themselves regulated entities with their own SLA obligations?
  3. Describe a time a compliance requirement (PCI-DSS, RBI guidelines, or similar) conflicted with your team's delivery timeline. What did you do?
  4. How do you build and sustain a healthy on-call culture in a team handling real-time card transaction processing?
  5. Tell me about a time you scaled an engineering team during rapid hiring. What broke first, and how did you fix it?
  6. How do you balance technical debt reduction against new feature delivery when reliability is non-negotiable?
  7. How have you managed SLA commitments with external partners when your own upstream systems or vendors were unreliable?
  8. Walk me through how you hire engineers who can work with both speed and the caution that financial infrastructure demands.
  9. Describe your approach to incident post-mortems. How do you make them genuinely useful without creating a blame culture?
  10. How do you work with product managers when engineering constraints (security, compliance, or architecture limits) block features the business wants urgently?
  11. What is your framework for deciding when to build something in-house versus buying a vendor solution or using open-source?
  12. How do you keep a team motivated when a significant portion of the roadmap is maintenance, compliance updates, and reliability improvements rather than new product features?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time when a compliance requirement conflicted with your delivery timeline.

*Situation:* My team was mid-sprint on a new card onboarding flow when a security audit flagged that our token storage approach did not meet updated PCI-DSS requirements. The product deadline was three weeks out and the compliance fix needed at least two weeks of engineering time.

*Task:* I needed to restructure the sprint, communicate clearly to stakeholders, and ensure neither compliance nor the core delivery fell through.

*Action:* I immediately pulled the two engineers closest to the storage layer and created a focused sub-track for the compliance work. A quick impact assessment showed we could ship a limited version of the onboarding flow on time while deferring one non-critical feature. I presented the trade-off to the product manager and business stakeholder with clear options, not just a problem, and got alignment within a day.

*Result:* The compliance fix shipped before the deadline, the limited onboarding flow launched on schedule, and the deferred feature followed two weeks later. The audit passed cleanly.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you scaled an engineering team during rapid hiring.

*Situation:* Our team roughly doubled in size over about four months after a funding round. Informal processes that had worked well for a small group started breaking down: code reviews were bottlenecked, new engineers took too long to ramp up, and institutional knowledge sat with two or three senior people.

*Task:* I had to redesign onboarding and knowledge-sharing workflows without slowing down the existing team or disrupting a major integration we were mid-way through.

*Action:* I introduced structured onboarding buddies, moved code review ownership to domain leads instead of keeping it centralised with me, documented the three most critical system areas every engineer needed to understand, and set up fortnightly architecture walkthroughs open to the whole team.

*Result:* New hire ramp-up time shortened noticeably, senior engineers reported spending less time on repetitive review comments, and we shipped the major integration on schedule despite the team transition.

---

Q: How do you run post-mortems without creating a blame culture?

*Situation:* After a significant transaction processing delay that affected partner bank settlements, morale on my team was low. People felt they would be called out individually in the debrief.

*Task:* I needed to run a post-mortem that surfaced the real systemic causes, produced actionable follow-up items, and left the team feeling supported rather than exposed.

*Action:* I used a timeline-first format where we reconstructed exactly what happened, when, and why, before any discussion of who did what. I framed the session explicitly as 'what did our system and processes allow to happen' rather than 'who made a mistake'. Every action item was assigned to a process or system improvement, not to an individual.

*Result:* The team left with clear action items, a shared understanding of the failure mode, and no one feeling singled out. We ran the same format for the next two incidents and team members started volunteering to facilitate the sessions themselves.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For people and team questions, structure your answer around: context (team size, stage, what was at stake), the specific challenge, your deliberate choices, and the observable outcome. Be precise about what you personally did versus what the team did. Interviewers at this level want to hear your direct contribution, not a team story with no clear owner.

For technical and architecture questions, lead with your decision-making criteria before jumping to a solution. At vegapay, interviewers care more about how you evaluate trade-offs (reliability vs. velocity, build vs. buy, compliance vs. speed) than about which specific technology you chose.

For stakeholder and cross-functional questions, show that you understood the other party's constraints, not just your own. A strong answer names what the product manager, partner bank, or legal team actually needed, and explains how you found a workable path forward.

For prioritisation questions, use a concrete example rather than a generic framework. Describing a real situation where two important things competed, and explaining the criteria you used to decide, is far more convincing than reciting a prioritisation matrix.

The STAR structure works well for behavioural questions. Keep Situation and Task brief (two to three sentences each) and spend most of your time on Action and Result. For a senior role like EM, the Result should include an impact on the team or the business, not just the technical outcome.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Vegapay interviewers are looking for an Engineering Manager who can be trusted with infrastructure that real banks depend on. Based on what candidates report, these are the key signals they look for:

Technical credibility without micromanaging. You should engage meaningfully in system design and architecture discussions, but your answers should show that you amplify your team rather than doing the work yourself.

Comfort with regulated environments. Fintech EM roles differ meaningfully from product-startup EM roles. Interviewers want evidence that you have shipped in environments with compliance requirements, external audits, or partner SLAs. If you have PCI-DSS, RBI, or similar experience, make it visible early.

Clear communication under pressure. Payment infrastructure incidents are high-stakes. Interviewers probe for how you communicate with stakeholders during and after an outage, and whether you can stay structured when things are going wrong.

Hiring and team-building judgement. Vegapay is growing, and an EM who joins will almost certainly be involved in hiring. Expect questions about how you assess candidates and how you have built teams across different experience levels.

Product thinking. This is not a pure execution role. Interviewers want to see that you can challenge product decisions, translate business requirements into engineering constraints, and push back constructively when a feature request introduces unacceptable technical risk.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week one: understand the business and product.
Read everything public about vegapay's platform, their website, fintech press coverage, and analyst pieces on card-as-a-service in India. Understand what their platform promises to partner banks and NBFCs. Know the regulatory context: RBI guidelines on card issuance, PCI-DSS basics, and how co-branded card programs work in the Indian market.

Week two: prepare your story bank.
Identify five to seven strong stories from your career covering: a compliance or security challenge, a team-scaling experience, a production incident you led through, a difficult stakeholder conversation, and a significant technical trade-off decision. Map each story to the STAR structure and practise saying them aloud, timing yourself to stay concise.

Week three: sharpen the relevant technical areas.
Review distributed systems concepts relevant to payment processing: idempotency, retry logic, eventual consistency, and webhook reliability. Be ready to discuss how you would approach or review a card transaction processing system at a high level. You do not need to have built one, but you should be able to reason through the reliability trade-offs clearly.

Before each round:
Prepare two or three specific questions about vegapay's engineering culture, team structure, or current roadmap challenges. Candidates who ask sharp, informed questions leave a stronger impression than those who ask generic ones.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Talking about technical work as if you still own the code. Saying 'I wrote the solution' or 'I fixed the bug' raises questions about whether you delegate effectively at EM level. Reframe as 'my team shipped this' or 'I unblocked the engineer working on it'.

Generic answers that could apply to any company. Mentioning card transaction reliability, RBI compliance, partner bank SLAs, or PCI-DSS in your answers shows preparation. Candidates who give entirely generic EM answers are much harder to differentiate in a competitive process.

Underselling incident and reliability experience. If you have managed on-call rotations, handled production incidents, or driven reliability improvements, go into detail. This experience is directly relevant to vegapay and is often undersold by candidates who assume product feature stories are more impressive.

Skipping the Result in STAR answers. Many candidates tell a strong Situation and Action but trail off without a clear Result. Even if the outcome was partial or mixed, state what happened and what you learned. A clear Result shows you measure your own impact.

Not asking about team structure. Joining a growing fintech without understanding how many engineers you will manage, what the reporting lines look like, and what the team's current challenges are is a missed opportunity. Ask these questions early, ideally in the first round.
