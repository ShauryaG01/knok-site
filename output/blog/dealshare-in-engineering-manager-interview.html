01 Overview

Overview

DealShare.in is a social commerce company targeting value-driven shoppers in Tier 2 and Tier 3 Indian cities, built around a group-buying model powered by WhatsApp-based viral sharing. As of July 2026, knok jobradar shows 3 open Engineering Manager roles at DealShare, within a broader market of 975 EM openings across India, with Bangalore leading at 182 openings.

The Engineering Manager at DealShare typically owns an engineering squad, drives delivery for core commerce or growth-tech areas, and works closely with product, data, and operations teams. Candidates report a process of 4-5 rounds, typically covering an HR screen, a technical or system design discussion, a people-management round, and a leadership or cultural fit conversation, with some candidates reporting a final round with a senior leader.

Salary context: Based on knok jobradar data, EM-level roles in India fall in the 35-60 LPA range at the Manager level, 55-90 LPA at Senior Manager, and 90-150+ LPA at Director. DealShare is a growth-stage startup, so total compensation often includes ESOPs alongside base pay.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through how you built or scaled an engineering team during rapid growth.

*Situation:* At my previous company, we were onboarding several new engineers every month as we expanded our logistics product. Processes that worked for a small team were visibly breaking as we grew.

*Task:* I needed to restructure the team into autonomous squads, introduce lightweight engineering rituals, and ensure quality did not drop during the hiring surge.

*Action:* I split the team into two squads aligned to product domains, each with a designated tech lead. I introduced a weekly cross-squad sync, a shared on-call rotation, and a written RFC process for any decision affecting more than one squad. I personally ran structured onboarding sessions for every new hire during their first month.

*Result:* Deployment frequency improved, cross-team escalations dropped noticeably, and new engineers were contributing independently within their first four weeks. The squad model was later adopted by two other engineering teams in the company.

---

Q: Tell us about a major production incident you managed.

*Situation:* During a promotional campaign, our payment gateway integration timed out, causing orders to fail silently. Customers were being charged but not receiving confirmation.

*Task:* I had to coordinate a fast technical fix, communicate clearly with the business team, and prevent a repeat.

*Action:* I opened a dedicated incident channel immediately, assigned a backend engineer to the fix, and personally drafted status updates to the CTO and business head every 20 minutes. Once the immediate issue was patched, I led a blameless post-mortem and drove implementation of circuit breakers and better alerting.

*Result:* The incident was resolved within a couple of hours. The post-mortem produced several action items, all completed within two weeks. We had zero incidents of that class in the following 6 months.

---

Q: How do you decide when to pay down technical debt versus shipping new features?

*Situation:* At my last company, a legacy monolith was slowing down feature delivery, but the product team had a full roadmap for the quarter.

*Task:* I needed to create space for refactoring without blocking planned releases.

*Action:* I proposed reserving a fixed portion of each sprint for debt reduction, tied to specific reliability or velocity outcomes. I also started quantifying debt in terms of developer hours lost per sprint, which made the business case concrete for leadership.

*Result:* Within two quarters, build times dropped noticeably and the team reported higher confidence in deployments. The product team noticed fewer last-minute blockers during feature work.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For people-management questions, use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) and make sure your Result names something concrete: retention trends, deployment frequency, incident counts, or engineer ramp-up time. Vague endings like 'things improved' weaken an otherwise strong story.

For conflict or decision questions, briefly map the stakeholders before diving into STAR. Name who was involved and what each party wanted. This shows you understand organisational dynamics, not just technical ones.

For system design questions (such as the flash sale backend), use a structured approach: clarify requirements, estimate scale, propose a high-level architecture, identify the top failure points, and explain your tradeoffs. DealShare operates in burst-traffic scenarios, so mentioning async queues, caching layers, and graceful degradation shows genuine platform thinking.

For prioritisation questions, lead with a framework (impact vs effort, or reliability vs velocity) before giving your example. This shows you have a repeatable method, not just instinct.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

DealShare's engineering interviews typically probe for four qualities:

Technical credibility without being hands-off. Interviewers want EMs who can review a system design, spot scalability risks, and have meaningful technical debates with senior engineers, not just manage timelines from a spreadsheet.

Startup operating rhythm. DealShare has scaled rapidly through resource constraints. Interviewers look for candidates who have made decisions with incomplete information, handled ambiguity, and moved quickly without waiting for a perfect plan.

People development focus. Expect questions about how you grow engineers, handle underperformance, and build psychological safety. They specifically want evidence that you retain good people, not just hire them.

Customer empathy for Bharat users. DealShare's product is built for first-time internet shoppers in small towns. Candidates who show awareness of low-bandwidth constraints, regional language considerations, and price sensitivity tend to stand out.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Company and product depth
Read everything publicly available about DealShare's business model, their group-buying mechanics, and their engineering approach. Press interviews with their technical leaders are a good starting point. Map how engineering likely supports the core buying flow, logistics, and the social sharing layer.

Week 2: People-management story bank
Write out several stories from your career using STAR format, covering team scaling, conflict resolution, underperformance handling, incident management, and a decision you made under pressure. Practice saying each one aloud in under 3 minutes.

Week 3: System design practice
Practise designing high-throughput systems relevant to social commerce: flash sales, notification pipelines, order management under load, and real-time inventory. Focus on tradeoffs and failure modes, not just the happy path.

Week 4: Questions to ask
Prepare a few thoughtful questions for each round. Good topics: how product and engineering collaborate on roadmap decisions, what the biggest technical challenge is right now, and what success looks like for an EM in the first 90 days.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Sounding like a project manager, not an engineering leader. Candidates who only talk about standups, timelines, and delivery tracking rarely get through. DealShare wants EMs who can go deep on architecture when needed.

Generic answers with no supporting detail. Saying 'I improved team performance' without context (team size, what changed, over what period) signals you are narrating rather than reflecting. Use the specifics you have, and be honest about thin data.

Over-engineering every system design. Proposing a full microservices architecture for every problem misses the point. For a company like DealShare that has had to move fast, showing you know when NOT to over-engineer is as important as knowing how to scale.

Ignoring the Bharat context. Candidates who design only for high-bandwidth, urban users miss DealShare's core customer. Mentioning offline-first patterns, low-data modes, or regional language support shows you understand the product.

Using 'we' when interviewers want 'I'. Saying 'we shipped the feature' is not an EM answer. Interviewers want your specific contribution: the decision you made, the call you took, the person you coached.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the DealShare Engineering Manager interview typically have?

Candidates report a process of 4-5 rounds, though this can vary by team and hiring manager. Rounds typically cover an HR screen, a technical or system design discussion, a people-management conversation, and a leadership or cultural fit round. Some candidates report a final conversation with a CXO or VP of Engineering. Confirm the exact structure with your recruiter at the start so you can prepare accordingly.

Does DealShare ask live coding questions in Engineering Manager interviews?

Candidates typically report that live coding is rare for EM roles at DealShare. The focus is on system design, architectural judgment, and people-management scenarios. That said, be prepared to discuss code at a conceptual level, walk through a design decision, or review an architecture. Technical credibility is tested, but usually not through a timed coding exercise.

What salary should I expect for an Engineering Manager role at DealShare?

Based on knok jobradar data, Manager-level EM roles in India fall in the 35-60 LPA range, while Senior Manager roles are in the 55-90 LPA range. DealShare is a growth-stage startup, so the split between base salary and ESOPs matters a lot. Ask for a clear breakdown of fixed, variable, and stock components before comparing this offer with others.

How important is startup or e-commerce experience for this role?

DealShare's model requires moving fast, handling ambiguity, and working with leaner teams than a large product company. Candidates with startup or consumer e-commerce backgrounds tend to align naturally with the environment. Candidates from larger companies can also do well if they can show examples of moving quickly, making decisions with incomplete data, and working outside rigid processes.

What is DealShare's engineering culture like?

Publicly available interviews and employee reviews describe DealShare's engineering culture as fast-paced, with a strong focus on impact for users in smaller cities and towns. Teams are typically lean, which means EMs often stay close to the technical work. The company has gone through significant growth phases, so candidates should expect some evolving processes and shifting priorities as the norm.

How should I prepare for the system design round specifically?

Focus on designs relevant to social commerce: viral sharing flows, flash sale backends, order management under high load, and notification systems. DealShare's users are often on low-bandwidth mobile connections, so designing for performance and resilience at the edge is a strong differentiator. Practice explaining your tradeoffs clearly, since interviewers are typically as interested in your reasoning as in the final architecture you arrive at.
