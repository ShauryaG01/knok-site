01 Overview

Overview

G-P (Globalization Partners) is a B2B SaaS platform that provides Employer of Record (EOR) services, helping companies hire people in other countries without needing to set up a local legal entity. A Data Scientist at G-P typically works on customer churn prediction, product usage analytics, revenue forecasting, and workforce trend analysis across global markets.

The role sits at the intersection of product, sales, and operations, so expect questions that mix core data science skills with business impact. G-P's platform serves HR and finance leaders, which means stakeholder communication matters as much as model accuracy.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions reflect what candidates report encountering in G-P Data Scientist interviews. They test both technical ability and your capacity to apply data science thinking to a global SaaS business.

  1. Walk me through a predictive model you built end to end, from framing the problem to delivering results.
  2. How would you measure and improve customer retention for a B2B SaaS platform operating across multiple countries?
  3. G-P's data spans many currencies, time zones, and legal jurisdictions. How do you handle data quality issues that come from this kind of diversity?
  4. Describe a time you had to simplify a complex analysis for a non-technical audience, such as an HR leader or a CFO.
  5. How would you design a dashboard to track the health of G-P's customer base across regions?
  6. Tell me about a time your analysis led to a decision that turned out to be wrong. What did you learn?
  7. How do you decide which features matter most when building a classification model?
  8. What is your approach to A/B testing on a B2B product where user numbers are small and experiment cycles are long?
  9. How would you define a 'healthy customer' metric for G-P, and how would you track it over time?
  10. Describe your experience with SQL and any data pipeline or orchestration tools you have used.
  11. Have you worked with NLP or unstructured text data? How might that apply to a global hiring platform?
  12. How do you prioritise competing data requests from product, sales, and operations teams when you cannot do everything at once?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for behavioural questions. Keep each answer to about two minutes when spoken aloud.

---

Q: Walk me through a predictive model you built end to end.

*Situation:* At my previous company, the customer success team was reacting to churn after it had already happened, which meant we were always too late to save accounts.

*Task:* I was asked to build a model that would flag at-risk accounts well ahead of their renewal date so the team could intervene early.

*Action:* I started by aligning with the CS team on exactly what 'churn' meant in our context, since different people defined it differently. I then pulled together product usage logs, support ticket history, and contract data from three separate systems, cleaned and joined them, and explored which signals correlated with eventual churn. Early feature adoption turned out to be a stronger predictor than recent login activity. I built a gradient boosting classifier, validated it on a holdout set, and built a simple dashboard showing a risk score per account that the CS managers could review each week.

*Result:* The team adopted it within one quarter. Accounts flagged as high-risk that received early outreach showed a noticeably higher renewal rate compared to the prior period. The model became a standard part of the CS weekly workflow.

---

Q: Describe a time you had to explain a complex finding to a non-technical audience.

*Situation:* I ran an analysis showing that a pricing segment which looked profitable on the surface was actually generating negative margin once support and onboarding costs were factored in.

*Task:* I had to present this to a VP of Sales who was not technical and was protective of that customer segment.

*Action:* Instead of showing model outputs or correlation plots, I built a one-page visual comparing revenue to total cost per customer tier in plain terms. I anticipated the pushback, so I included a simple sensitivity table showing the margin stayed negative even under optimistic cost assumptions. I framed the finding as a revenue opportunity rather than a critique of the team's existing work.

*Result:* The VP engaged thoughtfully, accepted the finding, and we worked together to adjust the pricing structure. That conversation also earned me more trust with the sales team for future analytical work.

---

Q: Tell me about a time your analysis was wrong.

*Situation:* I built a demand forecasting model for a product team. The model predicted a significant usage spike that did not materialise.

*Task:* I had to understand why the forecast failed and rebuild the team's confidence in data-driven decisions.

*Action:* In my post-mortem I found that I had trained on a historical period that included a one-time marketing campaign, which inflated the baseline. I had not flagged this as a data quality risk before presenting the forecast. I rebuilt the model excluding that anomalous period, documented the assumption explicitly, and added a note in the dashboard explaining which external events could cause the forecast to break.

*Result:* The revised forecast tracked actual usage much more closely. More importantly, the product team started proactively flagging upcoming campaigns to me so I could adjust model inputs in advance.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For technical questions: Lead with your approach, not your tools. State the business problem, explain your reasoning for the method you chose, and mention the trade-offs you considered. Interviewers want to see how you think before they care which library you used.

For business impact questions: Use a simple structure: problem, metric, action, outcome. If you do not have a clean success story, be honest about what you learned. G-P values intellectual honesty because data decisions affect real employment contracts across countries.

For stakeholder questions: Show that you can hold rigour and accessibility at the same time. Name who your audience was, what they cared about, and how you adapted your output to serve them rather than to showcase your method.

For case or product questions: Think out loud. A good structure is: clarify the goal, define the metric you would track, identify the data you would need, describe the analysis approach, and name the risks or caveats. Interviewers are evaluating your reasoning, not just the final answer.

For 'failure' questions: Do not pivot too quickly to what you learned. Spend time describing what actually went wrong before moving to the lesson. Interviewers find it more credible when you sit with the mistake honestly rather than rushing past it.

For prioritisation questions: Use a business impact versus effort framework. Show that you can push back on low-value requests without being dismissive, and that you involve stakeholders in the prioritisation conversation rather than deciding unilaterally.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

G-P is a remote-first, globally distributed company, so the bar for clear communication is high. Interviewers typically look for four things.

Strong fundamentals. You should be comfortable with SQL, Python or R, and core statistical and ML concepts. Feature selection, cross-validation, class imbalance, and metric choice should not catch you off guard.

Business thinking. G-P's data scientists are expected to define problems, not just solve assigned ones. If you have worked in a product analytics or growth context, lean into that. Connect your data outputs to business decisions rather than stopping at model metrics.

Cross-functional collaboration. The role involves working with product managers, engineers, and customer success teams who may not understand data science deeply. Candidates who communicate findings in plain language consistently stand out.

Comfort with messy data. Global EOR data is inherently complex. Employment rules differ by country, currencies fluctuate, and data is rarely cleanly labelled. Interviewers want to see that you approach incomplete or inconsistent data calmly and with a clear methodology.

Candidates also report that G-P interviewers appreciate genuine curiosity about the product itself. Understanding what an Employer of Record does, who uses it, and what problems it solves gives your answers far more context and credibility.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Understand the company and the domain.
Read G-P's public website and available material on how EOR services work. Think through the data problems that exist in a global hiring business: customer health tracking, cross-border compliance risk, revenue forecasting, and workforce trend analysis. Come in knowing what G-P actually does at a product level.

Week 2: Refresh your technical skills.
Practise SQL with window functions, CTEs, and multi-table aggregations. Review machine learning concepts most relevant to a SaaS product: churn modelling, customer segmentation, and time series basics. Be ready to explain your model choices and their trade-offs without jargon.

Week 3: Build your story bank.
Map three to four strong past projects to the question types in this guide. For each story, note the business context, the data challenge, the method you chose, and the outcome. Versatile stories are ones you can adapt to multiple different questions.

Before each round:
Ask your recruiter what to expect so you can adjust your focus. Candidates report that early rounds at G-P typically emphasise technical and analytical skills, while later rounds lean more toward business thinking and stakeholder scenarios.

For live practice, run your STAR answers out loud or record yourself. Two minutes per answer is the right target for most questions.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping to tools before framing the problem. Saying 'I would use XGBoost' before explaining what you are predicting is a red flag. Always state the business goal and the metric you are optimising for before describing your method.

Treating stakeholder questions as soft. Questions about communicating with non-technical teams are scored just as seriously as SQL or modelling questions at companies like G-P. Prepare real examples with outcomes, not vague claims about 'translating data for business.'

Glossing over failure. If you describe a project as a success but probing reveals it was abandoned or inconclusive, that is more damaging than owning the failure clearly. Be direct about what went wrong before explaining what you learned.

Ignoring data quality in your answers. In a global data context, interviewers assume the data is messy. If you describe a past project without mentioning how you handled missing values, inconsistencies, or biased samples, you signal limited real-world experience.

Arriving unprepared for the case round. Technical prep is important, but candidates often neglect business case practice. Run through at least a few structured case exercises so you are comfortable thinking out loud under time pressure.

Not asking questions. At the end of each round, have two or three thoughtful questions ready. Asking about how the data team is structured, how projects get prioritised, or what success looks like in the first few months signals genuine interest and good professional instincts.
