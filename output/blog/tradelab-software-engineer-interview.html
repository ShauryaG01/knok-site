02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

The following questions come up repeatedly for Software Engineer candidates at Tradelab, based on what candidates report across forums and interview-sharing platforms.

  1. Walk us through how a real-time bidding (RTB) system works end to end. Where are the biggest latency risks?
  2. How would you design a distributed frequency-capping service that limits how often a user sees the same ad within a rolling time window?
  3. You have a data pipeline processing millions of events per hour. How do you ensure exactly-once delivery and handle late-arriving data?
  4. Explain the CAP theorem. In a trading or bidding system, which tradeoffs would you accept and why?

  6. A production service starts throwing high-latency alerts at peak traffic. Walk me through your incident response process.
  7. How do you design a user-profile store that must serve lookups under tight latency constraints at very high concurrency?

  11. How do you ensure a data pipeline respects user privacy requirements such as consent flags and data-retention policies?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use these as inspiration. Adapt the context to your own experience before your interview.

Q: Tell me about a time you made a significant performance improvement.

*Situation:* At my previous company we had a reporting service that aggregated campaign metrics for clients. As data volume grew, queries were timing out and the frontend dashboard was showing stale numbers.

*Task:* I was assigned to reduce query latency and make the dashboard reliably up to date without requiring a full engineering rewrite.

*Action:* I profiled the slowest queries and found three main culprits: missing composite indexes, a correlated subquery running inside a loop, and no caching layer between the API and the database. I added targeted indexes, rewrote the subquery as a JOIN, and introduced a Redis-backed cache with a short TTL tied to our data-ingestion cadence.

*Result:* Dashboard refresh time dropped substantially, clients stopped raising support tickets about stale data, and database CPU utilization fell enough that we avoided an expensive instance upgrade.

---

Q: How would you design a distributed frequency-capping service?

*Situation:* An ad-tech product I worked on needed to cap how many times each user saw a given ad creative within a rolling window, across multiple data-center regions.

*Task:* I had to design a solution that was accurate enough to prevent overexposure but fast enough not to add meaningful latency to the bid path.

*Action:* I proposed a two-tier approach: a local in-process counter with an approximate sliding-window structure as the first check, backed by a Redis cluster for cross-region reconciliation on cache misses. I accepted slight overcounting at region boundaries as a deliberate tradeoff to stay within the latency budget. I documented the accuracy-vs-latency tradeoff explicitly so the product team could sign off.

*Result:* The solution passed load testing and the product team accepted the small overcounting margin as commercially acceptable. We shipped without needing a heavier consensus-based approach.

---

Q: Walk me through a production incident you handled.

*Situation:* A bid-processing service started returning high-latency responses during a peak traffic window, causing downstream timeout errors.

*Task:* As the on-call engineer I needed to identify the root cause quickly, mitigate user impact, and prevent recurrence.

*Action:* I started with dashboards to narrow the spike to one service, then correlated it with a recent deployment that had changed a batch-size config. I rolled back the config change immediately to restore normal latency, confirmed the issue resolved in metrics, then opened a detailed post-mortem to analyse why the config had not been load-tested before rollout.

*Result:* Service recovered within minutes of the config rollback. The post-mortem led to a new checklist item requiring load-test sign-off for any config touching batch sizes or concurrency limits.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use STAR for every behavioural question. Situation sets context (one or two sentences), Task clarifies what you were responsible for, Action covers what you personally did (use 'I', not 'we'), Result gives a concrete outcome. Candidates who skip Result leave interviewers guessing whether the work actually mattered.

For system-design questions, use a structured walkthrough. Start by clarifying requirements and scale assumptions. Then sketch the high-level components, pick your data stores with explicit reasoning, address the hardest constraint (usually latency or consistency), and finish with how you would monitor and operate the system. Thinking out loud is expected: interviewers at companies like Tradelab typically care more about your reasoning than about arriving at one 'correct' answer.

For debugging and incident questions, show a methodical process. State what signal you would look at first (metrics, logs, traces), how you would isolate the fault, and what mitigation you would apply before pursuing a full fix. Demonstrating that you do not panic and that you document your steps signals operational maturity.

Quantify wherever your own data allows. You do not need industry benchmarks. Saying 'query time dropped enough to eliminate client support tickets and avoid an infrastructure upgrade' is concrete and honest without inventing numbers.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates report and the nature of Tradelab's product, interviewers are typically looking for a few key signals.

Deep comfort with distributed systems tradeoffs. Ad-tech and trading platforms live and die on latency and throughput. Expect to defend your design choices, especially around consistency vs. availability.

Real production experience. Candidates who have operated systems under load, responded to incidents, and run post-mortems stand out. Theoretical knowledge is a starting point, not a finish line.

Data awareness. Tradelab's systems are data-heavy. Comfort with stream processing, event-driven architecture, and analytics pipelines signals you can contribute quickly.

Clear communication. Interviewers want to follow your thought process in real time. Candidates who think silently and then dump a final answer tend to fare worse than those who reason aloud and iterate.

Ownership mindset. Tradelab is a mid-sized product company. Engineers typically own features end to end. Demonstrating that you care about outcomes, not just code correctness, resonates well.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Foundations
Revise data structures and algorithms with a focus on problems involving queues, heaps, hash maps, and sliding windows. These patterns appear frequently in ad-tech and trading system contexts. Practice on a competitive coding platform until you can solve medium-difficulty problems comfortably within a typical phone-screen time window.

Week 2: System Design
Study distributed systems fundamentals: consistent hashing, write-ahead logs, Kafka consumer groups, Redis data structures, and rate-limiting algorithms. Practice designing at least two systems relevant to Tradelab: a real-time bidding pipeline and a high-concurrency key-value store. Write down your designs and review the tradeoffs you made.

Week 3: Domain Knowledge
Read up on how programmatic advertising works: DSPs, SSPs, bid requests, impression tracking, and conversion attribution. You do not need to be an ad-tech expert, but showing you have done basic research signals genuine interest in the company.

Week 4: Behavioural and Mock Rounds
Prepare five to six STAR stories covering: a performance improvement, a production incident, a disagreement with a teammate, a time you took ownership beyond your scope, and a project you are proud of. Do at least two timed mock interviews with a friend or peer.

Ongoing
Track the Tradelab careers page. With 10 open Software Engineer roles as of mid-2026, hiring is active. Apply early in the cycle when interviewers have more bandwidth.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping requirements clarification in system design. Jumping straight into drawing boxes without asking about scale, consistency requirements, or read-write ratios is one of the most common reasons candidates stall. Spend the first few minutes asking questions.

Using 'we' instead of 'I' in behavioural answers. Interviewers are assessing your individual contribution. Saying 'we built' or 'our team fixed' makes it impossible for them to calibrate your personal impact.

Giving textbook definitions without application. If asked about the CAP theorem, explaining the letters is not enough. Connect it to a real system you have built or a tradeoff you have made.

Ignoring operational concerns in design questions. Candidates who design a system but say nothing about monitoring, alerting, or failure modes come across as engineers who have not shipped to production.

Underestimating the domain curiosity signal. At a product company like Tradelab, knowing nothing about programmatic advertising when you apply for a core engineering role reads as low motivation. A small amount of preparation here goes a long way.

Rushing to code without a plan. In coding rounds, candidates who type immediately and then hit a wall mid-function lose more time than those who spend a couple of minutes sketching an approach first.
