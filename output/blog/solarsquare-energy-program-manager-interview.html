01 Overview

Overview

SolarSquare Energy is one of India's fastest-growing rooftop solar companies, helping homes and businesses go solar with end-to-end installation and financing. A Technical Program Manager (TPM) at SolarSquare sits at the intersection of software, hardware, field operations, and business teams, owning program delivery from scoping to post-launch review.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up most often, based on what candidates report for TPM interviews at growth-stage energy-tech companies like SolarSquare:

  1. Walk us through a complex technical program you owned from scoping to delivery. What was your co-ordination structure?
  2. SolarSquare works across hardware, software, and field installation. How do you manage dependencies between teams that work on very different cadences?
  3. Tell me about a time a program went off-track. How did you detect it early and what did you do?
  4. How do you handle scope creep when a business stakeholder keeps adding requirements mid-sprint?
  5. Describe how you set up milestones for a program with unclear success metrics.
  6. SolarSquare's growth is tied to government policy and grid regulations. How would you factor external dependencies like these into your program timeline?
  7. How do you prioritise competing programs when engineering bandwidth is limited?

  9. How have you worked with data or analytics teams to measure program impact?
  10. Give an example of a time you influenced a technical decision without direct authority.
  11. How would you onboard yourself to SolarSquare's tech stack and ongoing programs in your first few weeks?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a complex technical program you owned from scoping to delivery.

*Situation:* At a B2B SaaS company, we needed to migrate a legacy monolith to microservices while keeping the production system live for paying customers.

*Task:* I was asked to own the program end-to-end: scope, timeline, stakeholder alignment, and risk management across four engineering squads.

*Action:* I ran a two-week discovery sprint to map dependencies before writing a single line of the project charter. I set up a weekly risk review, built a clear RACI document, and introduced a feature-flag approach so we could ship incrementally without a big-bang cutover. I flagged two blockers to leadership three weeks before they would have become critical.

*Result:* We completed the migration on schedule. The platform team reported significantly fewer incidents in the quarter after launch, and the approach became the internal playbook for future migrations.

---

Q: How do you manage dependencies between hardware, software, and field teams?

*Situation:* At a previous role in an IoT company, I managed a program where firmware updates had to be co-ordinated with device shipments and a customer-facing app release.

*Task:* Three teams worked on very different cycles: hardware on a quarterly cadence, firmware on two-week sprints, and the app team on a monthly release. My job was to align these without slowing any team down.

*Action:* I built a dependency matrix that everyone could see and update. I ran a short cross-team sync every fortnight, kept it tight, and used a shared 'red list' of blockers. I negotiated one joint integration window per quarter where all three teams would freeze features and do full system testing together.

*Result:* The first quarterly release under this model had zero last-minute rollbacks, which had been a recurring problem before. Stakeholders called out the improved predictability in the retrospective.

---

Q: Describe how you handled a situation where a program went significantly off-track.

*Situation:* I was managing a data platform rollout at a fintech. Midway through, we discovered a key third-party API had a rate limit that would break our ingestion pipeline at scale.

*Task:* I had to re-plan delivery without losing the core business deadline, while keeping leadership informed and the team motivated.

*Action:* I called a focused war-room session with the three people who could actually solve the problem, not a general status meeting. We identified two parallel options: a caching layer or a batch ingestion fallback. I presented leadership with a revised timeline showing both options, the trade-offs, and a clear recommendation. I was transparent that we had missed an assumption in discovery.

*Result:* Leadership appreciated the honesty and chose the caching approach. We shipped two weeks later than originally planned but within a buffer we had flagged early. The incident led to us adding third-party API audits to our program-intake checklist.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the baseline for every behavioural question. Keep the Situation brief (two or three sentences), spend most of your time on Action, and end with a concrete Result.

Structured problem-solving works well for 'how would you approach X' questions. Clarify the goal, identify constraints, lay out options, recommend one, and walk through the trade-offs.

Dependency mapping is a pattern that resonates strongly in TPM panels. When asked about cross-functional programs, name the teams involved, describe how you mapped their interdependencies, and explain what you did when a dependency slipped.

The risk-radar framing is useful for questions about program health. Describe how you spot risks early using leading indicators rather than status colours, how you escalate, and how you close the loop after resolution.

Influence without authority is a theme SolarSquare panels probe, candidates report. Have a story ready where you changed a technical decision using data or relationships rather than rank. Name the stakeholder, the disagreement, and what you did to align them.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

SolarSquare is scaling fast in a sector that mixes hardware, software, government regulation, and consumer trust. Interviewers are typically looking for these qualities:

Comfort with ambiguity. Energy-tech programs often have shifting specs because policy, grid infrastructure, or component supply can change. Panels want to see that you build buffers and re-plan without panic.

Cross-functional credibility. You will work with installation engineers, firmware developers, and customer-success teams. Panels look for someone who can speak each team's language without pretending to be a domain expert.

Data-driven prioritisation. Candidates report that interviewers ask how you decide what to cut when timelines compress. Have a clear framework ready, not just 'I talk to stakeholders'.

Ownership mindset. TPMs at growth-stage companies wear many hats. Panels want evidence that you have stepped into gaps, flagged risks proactively, and owned outcomes even when things went wrong.

Mission alignment. SolarSquare's product is directly tied to India's energy transition. Showing genuine interest in the clean-energy space, without being over the top, tends to land well in final-round conversations.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know the company and the role.
Read SolarSquare's public materials: their website, any press coverage, and LinkedIn posts from their engineering or product team. Understand their product lines (rooftop solar for homes vs. commercial), their customer app, and their financing model. Map this to the TPM job description and write down three specific programs you could imagine owning there.

Week 2: Build your story bank.
Write out six to eight stories from your past experience using the STAR format. Cover: a cross-functional program you owned, a program that went off-track, a technical decision you influenced, a time you managed competing priorities, and a time you delivered with incomplete information. Practise saying each story out loud in under three minutes.

Also prepare two or three questions to ask the panel. Good examples: 'What does the first program a new TPM typically owns look like here?' or 'How does the engineering team and the field operations team co-ordinate on a new product rollout?'

Week 3: Mock interviews and logistics.
Do at least two timed mock interviews with a peer or mentor. Focus on trimming filler and making your Results concrete. Check your internet connection and background if the rounds are virtual. Confirm round formats with the recruiter so you know whether to expect case questions, system-design discussions, or purely behavioural panels.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Vague results. Saying 'the project was successful' without any detail is the most common miss. Even if you cannot share exact figures, say something like 'the team shipped on time for the first time in three quarters' or 'leadership approved the next phase of funding based on this delivery'.

Over-explaining the situation, under-explaining the action. Panels want to understand what YOU did. Cut the backstory and spend more time on your specific decisions and why you made them.

Treating every question as purely technical. TPM interviews at companies like SolarSquare test your people and process skills as much as your technical depth. If you only talk about architecture decisions, you may miss what the panel is actually evaluating.

Not knowing SolarSquare's context. Candidates who give generic answers about 'agile programs' without connecting to the energy sector, hardware-software integration, or field deployment realities tend to receive lower ratings.

Forgetting to ask questions. Not asking anything signals low curiosity or low preparation. Have two thoughtful questions ready for each round.

Underselling cross-functional influence. Many candidates describe themselves as 'co-ordinators' when they actually drove decisions. Use active verbs: you 'aligned', 'proposed', 'negotiated', 'blocked', or 'escalated'.
