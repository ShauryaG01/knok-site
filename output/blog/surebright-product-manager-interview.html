01 Overview

Overview

SureBright is an embedded insurance and warranty platform, connecting e-commerce merchants with product protection plans at the moment of purchase. As a PM here, you sit at the crossroads of insurance, checkout UX, and merchant analytics, building for two distinct user groups: the merchant who wants higher attach rates, and the consumer who files claims when something goes wrong.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a time data changed your product thinking.

*Situation:* I was PM for a checkout add-on feature at a B2C platform. We had built a warranty upsell card that appeared just above the 'Place Order' button.

*Task:* The feature launched to decent initial attach rates but performance dropped after a few weeks. I needed to find out why and decide whether to iterate or sunset it.

*Action:* I pulled session recordings and funnel data. The data showed users were clicking the warranty card, reading the fine print modal, then abandoning checkout entirely. I spoke with several customers who had abandoned. They said the fine print felt 'scary' and they did not understand what was covered. I proposed replacing the modal with plain-language bullet points and a single 'What is covered?' expand link, then A/B tested both versions.

*Result:* The simplified version won the A/B test clearly. Abandon rate at that step dropped and attach rate recovered. The lesson I carried forward: trust signals matter more than completeness of information at the point of purchase.

---

Q: How would you reduce consumer drop-off at the protection plan upsell step?

*Situation:* At my previous role, our embedded protection product had a checkout upsell converting below what the merchant expected.

*Task:* I owned the upsell widget and was asked to improve its conversion without changing the plan price.

*Action:* I ran a structured discovery sprint. I mapped the upsell UI against several competing checkout experiences, ran a usability test with users unfamiliar with the product, and reviewed heatmaps to see where attention landed. I found the price was shown in a confusing per-month framing, the claim process was not explained, and the CTA button was below the fold on mobile. I prioritized the mobile fold fix first (zero development cost), then rewrote the price copy to show a single total, and added a one-line claim promise.

*Result:* Attach rate on mobile improved in the following sprint cycle. The claim promise copy became a standard element across the product line, and merchant satisfaction scores for the widget improved in the next quarterly review.

---

Q: How do you manage conflicting requirements from the insurance underwriting team versus a merchant's integration needs?

*Situation:* I was leading a new product protection category launch. The underwriting team required specific eligibility questions to be answered before a plan could be issued. The merchant's dev team said adding those fields to checkout would hurt conversion.

*Task:* My job was to launch on schedule without breaking underwriting requirements or the merchant's checkout performance.

*Action:* I facilitated a joint session with both teams and asked the underwriting team to rank which eligibility fields were legally required versus 'nice to have.' Several fields turned out to be optional for launch. For the required ones, I proposed collecting them post-purchase via a confirmation email flow, within a short agreed window before plan activation. I documented this as a formal exception, got legal sign-off, and built a fallback: if the consumer did not respond in time, the plan defaulted to the most restrictive eligibility tier.

*Result:* We launched on schedule. The post-purchase collection flow had a completion rate that satisfied the underwriting team. The merchant reported no measurable change in checkout conversion, and this approach was later adopted as a pattern for additional category launches.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For product design and improvement questions, use a Problem-Solution-Metrics flow. Start by naming the user (merchant or consumer), state the core friction they face, propose solutions ranked by impact versus effort, then close with the metrics you would track to confirm it worked. SureBright's dual-sided platform means you should always clarify which user you are designing for before going further.

For metrics and analytical questions, lead with a North Star metric (attach rate, claim satisfaction, merchant retention), then break it into input metrics (traffic, click rate, conversion, claim frequency). For SureBright specifically, metrics that connect merchant revenue to consumer protection outcomes tend to land well with interviewers, candidates report.

For prioritization questions, use a lightweight impact-confidence-effort (ICE) framing. State your assumptions out loud, acknowledge the constraints (insurance regulatory requirements, merchant integration costs), and show you can make a call even with incomplete data. Interviewers at startups want to see decisiveness, not just structured thinking.

For behavioral questions, use STAR structure: Situation (one or two sentences), Task (what you personally owned), Action (what you specifically did, not the team), Result (a measurable outcome or clear learning). Keep Situation and Task short. Spend the bulk of your time on Action and Result, because that is where interviewers assess your judgment.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

SureBright PMs are expected to be comfortable operating in a regulated, two-sided market. Interviewers typically look for four things.

Deep user empathy, for both sides. Can you articulate the merchant's commercial goal (higher attach rate, smoother integration) AND the consumer's emotional state at the moment they decide to buy or skip a protection plan? Candidates who only talk about one side often get probed harder on the other.

Comfort with data and ambiguity. You will likely face a scenario with incomplete numbers. Interviewers want to see you structure the problem, state your assumptions clearly, and propose a measurement approach. You do not need the exact answer; you need to show how you would find it.

Domain awareness. You do not need to be an insurance expert, but knowing basics like how claim ratios affect underwriter pricing, or what 'attach rate' means to a merchant, signals preparation. Candidates who reference relevant industry examples, even briefly, typically fare better than those giving purely generic product answers.

Ownership and decision-making. SureBright is a startup. Interviewers want evidence that you have driven something end-to-end, handled trade-offs without waiting for full consensus, and can communicate clearly with non-technical stakeholders like insurance partners or merchant account teams.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Understand the product and domain. Use SureBright's product as a customer if possible. Look at how their protection plans appear at partner checkouts. Read about embedded insurance basics: how attach rates work, what makes a claim experience good or bad, and how insurtech startups structure merchant partnerships. You do not need to memorize insurance regulations; just know enough to sound fluent in the domain.

Week 2: Practice product cases. Pick several scenarios from the most-asked questions above and write out full answers. Practice saying your answers out loud, not just thinking them through. Cases involving SureBright's dual-sided model (merchant AND consumer) are especially worth rehearsing, since they test a skill most generic PM prep misses.

Week 3: Sharpen your behavioral stories. Identify a set of career experiences that cover: using data to change a decision, handling cross-functional conflict, launching something from scratch, and recovering from a product failure or near-miss. Map each story to STAR format and practice keeping the Situation brief.

Before the interview. Review the job description closely and note any specific domain words they use. Prepare a few questions for the interviewer that show genuine curiosity about SureBright's roadmap or business model, not generic 'what is the culture like' questions. Thoughtful questions signal high interest.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating it like a generic PM interview. SureBright's product is domain-specific. Candidates who give textbook answers without connecting to embedded insurance, merchant use cases, or two-sided platform dynamics tend to score lower. Show that you have thought about their actual business.

Skipping the 'which user' clarification. SureBright serves merchants and consumers. Not clarifying which user you are solving for at the start of a design question is a common and costly slip. Always establish the user before diving into solutions.

Over-indexing on features, under-indexing on outcomes. Listing many features you would build is less impressive than explaining one feature well: why it matters to the user and how you would measure whether it worked. Interviewers typically remember the candidate who talked clearly about outcomes, not the one who had the longest roadmap.

Vague behavioral answers. Saying 'we improved the product' without a specific action you took or a result you can describe is a red flag in any PM interview. Even if you cannot share exact numbers, describe what changed and what you personally did to drive that change.

Asking no questions. Candidates who ask nothing at the end of any round often signal low interest. Prepare at least a couple of genuine questions about SureBright's roadmap, current challenges, or team structure. A good question at the end of a round can leave a strong last impression.

How many rounds does the SureBright PM interview typically have?

Candidates report a process that typically includes a recruiter or HR screening call, one or two product case or take-home rounds, and a final discussion with senior leadership. Do I need prior insurance or fintech experience to get a PM role at SureBright?

Prior experience in insurance or fintech is helpful but not always required, candidates report. SureBright typically looks for strong product fundamentals, comfort with two-sided marketplace dynamics, and the ability to learn a regulated domain quickly. If you do not have direct insurance experience, showing that you have researched how embedded insurance works and can speak confidently to attach rates, claim flows, and merchant economics will help you stand out from candidates with purely generic PM backgrounds.

What is the best way to prepare for a SureBright product case?

Practice cases that involve both sides of SureBright's platform: the merchant who wants better attach rates and the consumer who needs a trustworthy claims experience. Use the Problem-Solution-Metrics framework and always clarify which user you are designing for before diving into solutions. Spending time understanding how a real embedded protection plan actually appears at checkout on any merchant partner's site will give you concrete, specific examples to reference during your case rather than speaking in abstractions.

Is there a take-home assignment in the SureBright PM interview process?

Some candidates report receiving a take-home product case or analytical exercise, though this varies by role and hiring cycle. If you receive one, focus on demonstrating clear thinking over exhaustive research: state your assumptions, show your reasoning, and tie your recommendations to measurable outcomes. Keep your submission focused and concise rather than trying to cover every possible angle, since evaluators are assessing your judgment about what matters most.
