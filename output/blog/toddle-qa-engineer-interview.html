03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk me through how you would test a new feature in toddle's lesson planning module.

*Situation:* At my previous company, we launched a new content scheduling feature in an e-learning app, similar in complexity to a lesson planning workflow.

*Task:* I was responsible for end-to-end test coverage before the release, with no formal spec document available.

*Action:* I started by sitting with the product manager to understand the user journey, then mapped out the happy path, edge cases, and failure scenarios. I tested across different user roles (teacher, admin), browser types, and screen sizes. I also ran exploratory sessions to find gaps the spec had missed, and wrote automated smoke tests for the critical save-and-publish flow.

*Result:* We caught a data-loss bug where unsaved changes were silently dropped on session timeout. The fix went in before release, and post-launch support tickets for that feature were notably lower compared to previous launches.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you disagreed with a developer about whether something was a bug.

*Situation:* During regression testing at my last job, I flagged that a form field accepted negative values for a quantity input. The developer said it was 'working as designed' since the backend handled it.

*Task:* I needed to make a clear case without creating conflict, while ensuring the user experience was not harmed.

*Action:* I pulled up three similar competitor products and showed how they all blocked negative values at the UI layer. I also framed it as a user story: 'a teacher enters minus two worksheets by accident.' I escalated it as a UX defect rather than a functional bug, which shifted the conversation.

*Result:* The team agreed to add client-side validation. It merged within two days, and the feature shipped cleanly. The developer later thanked me for framing it as a UX issue rather than a technical argument.

---

Q: How do you prioritise test cases when you have limited time before a release?

*Situation:* At a previous role, a release date was brought forward by a full week due to a client commitment, leaving us with roughly half the planned testing time.

*Task:* I had to decide what to cut and what to protect, with no option to delay the release.

*Action:* I ranked test cases by three factors: user impact if it failed, how frequently the flow is used, and whether the area changed in this release. I focused all manual effort on changed code paths and high-traffic flows. I deprioritised cosmetic checks and low-traffic admin settings, and flagged the cut scope to the PM and dev lead so they could make an informed go/no-go call.

*Result:* We shipped on the new date with zero critical bugs in the first week post-launch. Two minor cosmetic issues were raised by users and fixed in the next patch. The prioritisation framework I documented became the team standard for time-boxed releases.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For 'how would you test X' questions: Start with users and use cases before jumping to test cases. Name the user roles involved (teacher, admin, student in toddle's context), list the core flows, then layer in edge cases and negative scenarios. Finish by mentioning what you would automate versus keep manual, and why.

For behavioural questions (tell me about a time...): Use the STAR format: Situation (one or two sentences of context), Task (what you were responsible for), Action (the specific steps you took, using 'I' not 'we'), Result (a concrete outcome). Candidates often spend too long on Situation and too little on Action. Flip that ratio.

For 'how do you prioritise' questions: Show a framework, not just instinct. A simple one: impact if it fails, frequency of use, and whether the code changed in this release. Naming a framework signals that you work systematically rather than ad hoc.

For product-thinking questions: Toddle is used by teachers who are not technical. If you ground your answers in 'what a teacher would experience' rather than 'what the system does,' you will stand out. Show empathy for non-technical end users.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Toddle builds for a specific user: international school teachers who are not technical. Interviewers typically look for QA candidates who genuinely care about end user experience, not just test coverage numbers.

User empathy first. Candidates who talk about 'what a teacher would feel if this broke' tend to do better than those who only reference pass/fail rates.

Clear communication. QA engineers at toddle work closely with product managers and developers. Expect questions that test whether you can explain a bug or a risk clearly, in plain language that a non-engineer can act on.

Structured thinking. Whether it is a test plan, a bug report, or a prioritisation call, interviewers want to see a repeatable process, not just experience.

Comfort with ambiguity. EdTech products evolve fast. Candidates report that toddle values people who can test features with incomplete specs and flag gaps proactively rather than waiting for full documentation.

Ownership mindset. QA at a startup like toddle is not just about executing test cases someone else wrote. Interviewers look for people who treat quality as their responsibility end to end.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Build your foundation

Start by signing up for toddle's free teacher account and using the product yourself. Map the main user flows: creating a unit plan, adding resources, sharing with colleagues. These are the features most likely to appear in a take-home or live assignment.

Next, refresh your core QA fundamentals. Equivalence partitioning, boundary value analysis, and exploratory testing techniques are all fair game. If you have API testing experience, practice with a public REST API using Postman or a similar tool.

To close out the first week, write a short sample test plan for one toddle feature (the lesson planning screen is a good choice). This doubles as interview prep and a portfolio piece you can reference during the interview.

Week 2: Sharpen and simulate

Early in the second week, practice your STAR answers out loud for the behavioural questions listed above. Record yourself if possible. The goal is to keep each answer under two minutes without rushing.

Then research toddle's recent product updates and blog posts. Knowing what features shipped recently signals genuine interest and gives you fresh examples to raise in the interview.

In the final days of your prep, run at least one full mock interview with a peer or mentor. Cover a 'how would you test this' question and one behavioural question. Ask for feedback on clarity and structure, not just content.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping to test cases before understanding users. When asked 'how would you test X,' many candidates immediately list test cases. Interviewers at product-first companies like toddle want to hear you think about who uses the feature and why before you describe how you would test it.

Vague STAR answers. Saying 'I improved test coverage' is not a result. Describe what changed, what was prevented, or what the team was able to do differently. If you do not have a specific number, describe the outcome in concrete terms.

Over-automating in your answers. Candidates often say 'I would automate everything.' Experienced interviewers know that is not realistic. Show that you understand what is worth automating (stable, high-frequency flows) and what is better left to exploratory or manual testing (new features, UX-heavy interactions).

Not asking about the stack. Toddle is a web-first product. If you do not ask about the tech stack, browsers supported, or device coverage, you signal that you are not used to scoping test environments carefully.

Ignoring the EdTech context. Generic QA answers that could apply to any product miss the point. A teacher losing unsaved curriculum work before a class is a very different severity from a button being slightly off-colour. Show that you understand stakes from the user's perspective.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the toddle QA interview typically have?

Candidates report typically going through two to three rounds. This commonly includes an initial HR or recruiter call, a technical or take-home assignment focused on testing a specific feature or bug scenario, and a final round with the hiring manager or team. The exact structure can change based on role level, so confirm the process with your recruiter at the start.

Does toddle ask coding questions in QA interviews?

Most candidates report that toddle QA interviews focus on test design, bug analysis, and product thinking rather than heavy coding. However, if you are applying for an SDET or automation-heavy role, expect questions on scripting in Python or JavaScript and working with frameworks like Selenium or Playwright. Check the job description carefully to understand the automation expectation for your specific opening.

What is the salary range for a QA Engineer at toddle?

Toddle does not publicly disclose salary bands. Based on India-wide QA Engineer market data from knok jobradar, mid-level professionals (3-5 years) typically see offers in the 9-17 LPA range, and senior professionals (6-9 years) in the 17-30 LPA range. For more community-reported numbers, check Glassdoor and levels.fyi, which tend to have more recent data points.

Do I need EdTech experience to get hired at toddle?

EdTech experience helps but is not a strict requirement. What matters more is showing genuine curiosity about how teachers use the product and the ability to think from a non-technical user's perspective. Candidates from fintech, healthtech, or consumer apps who frame their answers around user empathy and high-stakes data tend to do well. Using toddle's free product before the interview is the easiest way to bridge any domain gap.

How do I prepare for a toddle QA take-home assignment?

Take-home assignments at product companies like toddle typically ask you to review a feature, write a test plan, or find bugs in a staging environment. Structure your submission clearly: start with the user story or use case, list your test scenarios by priority, and include exploratory observations beyond the obvious flows. Show that you thought about edge cases a real teacher might hit, not just the happy path.

Is there a lot of manual testing at toddle or is it mostly automation?

Based on what candidates report, toddle QA roles involve a mix of both. The product ships features frequently, so exploratory and regression testing by hand remains important for new and complex flows. Automation is valued for stable, high-frequency features. If you can articulate clearly when to use each approach rather than insisting on one over the other, you will come across as a more practical hire.
