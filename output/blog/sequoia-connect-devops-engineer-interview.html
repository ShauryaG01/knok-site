02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions are based on what DevOps candidates typically report from interviews at companies of similar scale and stage. They span CI/CD design, cloud platforms, incident handling, and cross-team collaboration.

  1. Walk us through a CI/CD pipeline you designed end to end at a previous company. What drove your tool choices?
  2. How would you design a zero-downtime deployment strategy for a microservices-based application?
  3. Describe your experience with Kubernetes, including cluster scaling, resource limits, and troubleshooting pod failures.
  4. How do you approach infrastructure as code? Which tools have you used, and what trade-offs did you navigate?
  5. Tell us about a production incident you led the response to. How did you communicate during the outage, and what changed after it?
  6. How do you manage secrets and credentials safely across multiple environments and services?
  7. What steps have you taken to reduce cloud infrastructure costs without hurting reliability or performance?
  8. If you were onboarding a brand-new service, how would you set up its monitoring, alerting, and on-call runbooks from scratch?
  9. How do you think about the tension between shipping fast and maintaining security or compliance guardrails?
  10. Describe a time you had to convince developers to change the way they work, for example adopting a new deployment tool or process.
  11. How would you design a disaster recovery plan for a business-critical production database?
  12. What is your experience with multi-cloud or hybrid-cloud environments, and what trade-offs did you have to make?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR method for every behavioural and situational question: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Here are three worked examples.

---

Q: Tell us about a production incident you led.

*Situation:* Our payment service dropped on a Friday evening, affecting live customer transactions across the platform.

*Task:* As the on-call DevOps engineer, I was responsible for coordinating the response, keeping stakeholders informed, and restoring service as quickly as possible.

*Action:* I pulled recent deployment and config logs and spotted a config change that had gone out shortly before the drop. I rolled it back using our feature flag system, verified the fix in a shadow environment, then gradually shifted traffic back via load balancer weights. I kept a running Slack thread with status updates at regular intervals so the product and support teams had a single source of truth throughout.

*Result:* Service was restored within the hour. The incident led directly to a new mandatory review gate for production config changes, which the team shipped the following week.

---

Q: How have you reduced cloud infrastructure costs?

*Situation:* Our AWS bill had grown steadily over a couple of years without a formal review process in place.

*Task:* I was asked to audit spend and propose reductions without affecting uptime SLAs or application performance.

*Action:* I used AWS Cost Explorer to identify the highest-spend categories. I found a number of oversized EC2 instances running at low utilization, forgotten RDS snapshots, and S3 buckets with no lifecycle policies. I right-sized the instances after running load tests in staging, set up S3 lifecycle rules to automatically move old objects to cheaper storage tiers, and introduced a monthly cost review ritual with engineering leads.

*Result:* The team saw meaningful savings within one quarter. I cannot cite a precise figure here, but the finance team confirmed the impact was material and the practices were adopted as standing policy across all our cloud accounts.

---

Q: Describe a time you convinced a dev team to adopt a new practice.

*Situation:* The backend team at my previous company was deploying to production manually via SSH, causing inconsistent builds and environment drift between staging and prod.

*Task:* I needed to move them to a fully automated CI/CD pipeline without disrupting their sprint delivery schedule.

*Action:* Instead of mandating the change top-down, I ran a short demo showing the team how the new pipeline would cut their manual steps. I paired with two developers to migrate the first service together, documented every step in a shared runbook, and made the pipeline opt-in for the first two weeks so teams could trial it without pressure. I stayed available on Slack for questions throughout the rollout.

*Result:* All backend services moved onto the pipeline within six weeks, and manual SSH access to production was fully decommissioned. Developers later said they felt part of the process rather than subjected to it.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For technical design questions (pipelines, DR plans, monitoring setups): structure your answer as requirements first, then constraints, then your chosen approach, then trade-offs you considered. Interviewers want to see how you think, not just the solution you land on.

For incident and debugging questions: lead with how you identified the problem, then how you communicated during it, then what permanent fix followed. Companies hiring senior DevOps engineers typically weight communication skills as highly as technical resolution speed.

For 'why this tool' questions: frame your answer as: problem first, options considered, reason for the choice, outcome. Avoid the trap of saying 'everyone uses Terraform' as a justification. Show a deliberate decision based on your specific context.

For cross-team and influence questions: use STAR and make your action steps concrete. 'I collaborated with the team' is weak. 'I ran a two-hour workshop, created a shared runbook template, and set a fortnightly review checkpoint' is strong.

A useful self-check before every answer: can you tell the full story in ninety seconds? If not, trim the situation and expand the action and result instead.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates typically report from companies hiring at this scale and stage, DevOps interviewers are generally weighing four things.

Depth on one or two core areas, breadth across the stack. You do not need to be an expert in every cloud and every tool. But you should be able to go deep on your primary platform and speak knowledgeably about the wider ecosystem around it.

Incident ownership, not just incident participation. There is a clear difference between 'I was on the on-call rotation' and 'I led the post-mortem, wrote the action items, and tracked them to closure.' Interviewers at this level typically probe for the second kind of ownership.

Cross-functional communication. DevOps roles at growing companies require constant coordination with product, backend, and security teams. Interviewers look for candidates who can translate technical constraints into plain language that non-engineers can act on.

A blameless, learning mindset. When describing past incidents or failures, frame your answers around what the system lacked, what process improved, and what you personally took away. Avoid placing blame on teammates or management, even when that instinct is understandable.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week one: audit your own experience. Write down every pipeline, cloud setup, and incident you have touched in the last three years. For each, note the tools used, the problem being solved, and the measurable outcome. This becomes your personal answer bank going into the interview.

Week two: review core concepts. Brush up on Kubernetes internals (scheduling, networking, RBAC), CI/CD deployment patterns (blue-green, canary, feature flags), IaC best practices (Terraform state management, module structure), and observability foundations (the four golden signals: latency, traffic, errors, saturation).

Week three: practise out loud. Record yourself answering five questions from the list above. Play it back and check for vague actions, skipped results, or filler phrases. Timed practice is the single most effective way to tighten STAR answers before the real thing.

Before the interview: look up any publicly available engineering content from Sequoia Connect, such as blog posts or job descriptions, to understand what their platform does and what scale challenges that implies. Prepare two or three questions to ask at the end of the interview, focused on their current infrastructure pain points or how the DevOps team is structured.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Listing tools instead of solving problems. Saying 'I have used Helm, ArgoCD, and Datadog' is a list, not an answer. Always lead with the problem the tool was solving before you name the tool.

Skipping the result in STAR answers. Many candidates end at the action step. Interviewers need to hear what changed as a result of your work, even if the outcome is qualitative rather than a specific number.

Overcomplicating design answers. When asked to design a pipeline or DR strategy, candidates sometimes jump to the most complex architecture possible. Start simple, explain trade-offs, then offer to go deeper. This demonstrates engineering judgement, not just knowledge.

Avoiding 'I do not know.' If a question covers a tool or concept you have not worked with, say so clearly, then explain how you would approach learning it or what transferable experience you bring. Interviewers respect honesty and a learning mindset far more than a bluffed answer.

Talking only about individual contributions. In a DevOps role, your impact is often measured through the teams you enable. Include examples where you helped developers ship faster, helped a security team close a gap, or helped a product manager understand a technical constraint.

How many rounds does the Sequoia Connect DevOps interview typically have?

Candidates typically report three to four stages: a recruiter or HR screening call, one or two technical rounds covering systems design and hands-on tooling, and a final conversation with engineering leadership or a hiring manager. Round structures vary and no official process has been published, so treat any account as a rough guide based on candidate experiences.

What salary can I expect for a DevOps Engineer role at Sequoia Connect?

Sequoia Connect has not published specific salary bands publicly. Based on knok job radar data for DevOps roles across India, mid-level candidates (3-5 years) typically see offers in the 15-28 LPA range and senior candidates (6-9 years) in the 30-50 LPA range. For company-specific data points, Glassdoor and levels.fyi are the most useful sources to check alongside any offer you receive.

Is there a coding test or LeetCode-style round in the DevOps interview?

Candidates at DevOps interviews typically report that pure data-structures-and-algorithms rounds are rare compared to software engineering roles. More common are practical tasks: writing or reviewing a Dockerfile, debugging a broken pipeline config, or scripting a deployment task in Bash or Python. Focus your preparation on hands-on tooling rather than competitive programming problems.

What cloud platform should I focus on for my preparation?

Sequoia Connect has not publicly confirmed which cloud platform their engineering team primarily uses. In your interview, focus on the cloud platform you know deepest and be ready to explain your architectural reasoning clearly. You can also ask the interviewer directly which environment the team uses, as it is a natural and well-received question at this stage.

How long does the hiring process take from application to offer?

Candidates report that timelines vary considerably depending on team availability and how many roles are being filled at once. Typically, an active pipeline at a growing company runs two to four weeks from first contact to offer, though this can stretch if scheduling is difficult or additional rounds are added. Following up politely with your recruiter after each stage is standard practice and generally well received.
