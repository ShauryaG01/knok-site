01 Overview

Overview

Tiqets is an Amsterdam-headquartered online marketplace connecting visitors with tickets to museums, cultural attractions, and experiences across major cities worldwide. Their product teams focus on the full booking funnel: visitor-side discovery and checkout, venue-side inventory and redemption tools, and the personalisation layer that surfaces the right experience to the right visitor. As of mid-2026, knok jobradar tracked 9 open roles at Tiqets across product and related functions.

The interview process candidates report typically runs three to five rounds. Expect an initial recruiter screen, a hiring manager conversation focused on your background and motivation, one or two product case or design rounds, and a final stakeholder or panel discussion. Some candidates also report a written take-home exercise or a live product critique session. Because Tiqets operates a two-sided marketplace in travel and experiences, interviewers consistently probe your comfort with supply-demand tradeoffs, conversion optimisation, and global product thinking.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions reflect patterns candidates report for Tiqets PM interviews, shaped by the company's marketplace model, travel-and-experiences focus, and global user base.

  1. How would you improve Tiqets' discovery experience for a first-time visitor who opens the app in an unfamiliar city?
  2. Tiqets serves two distinct customers: venues (supply) and visitors (demand). How do you decide to prioritise a feature that clearly helps venues but may reduce visitor conversion?
  3. Walk us through a time you used data to reverse or significantly change a product decision you had already shipped.
  4. How would you design a self-serve inventory management tool for small independent museums that have limited digital experience?
  5. A key venue partner reports a sharp spike in ticket redemption failures this week. How do you triage this, and what do you communicate to the partner?
  6. How would you define and measure success for a new 'last-minute deals' feature on the Tiqets mobile app?
  7. Tiqets is entering a new geography with lower smartphone penetration than its current markets. What does your launch strategy look like?
  8. Describe a time you aligned engineering and business stakeholders who had conflicting views on feature scope.
  9. How would you reduce checkout abandonment on Tiqets without changing the underlying payment infrastructure?
  10. How do you approach accessibility and localisation when building a product that serves international tourists from many different countries?
  11. What is the single biggest threat to Tiqets' marketplace model over the next couple of years, and how would you respond to it as a PM?
  12. Name a product in the travel or experiences space you genuinely admire. What is one idea from it you would bring to Tiqets, and how would you validate it with data?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use these three STAR-format answers as a template. Before your interview, swap in your own real experience.

Q: Walk us through a time you used data to reverse a product decision you had already shipped.

*Situation:* At a B2C travel platform, my team simplified the booking flow by removing the seat-selection step. Our hypothesis was that fewer steps would reduce drop-off.

*Task:* Three weeks after launch, support tickets about incorrect seating spiked and satisfaction scores from business travellers declined noticeably. I needed to diagnose the cause and recommend a course of action.

*Action:* I pulled a cohort analysis segmenting leisure and business travellers. Business travellers were a smaller share of total bookings but accounted for a much larger share of revenue, and their post-launch churn was significantly higher. I framed this as a revenue risk to the team and proposed a segmented experience: leisure travellers would keep the simplified flow, while business travellers would see seat selection restored. I scoped a focused A/B test to validate before a full rollout.

*Result:* The segmented flow shipped in the following sprint. Support tickets returned to baseline and business traveller retention recovered within about six weeks. The lesson I carry forward: define success metrics by segment before shipping, not just by overall funnel conversion.

---

Q: How would you improve Tiqets' discovery experience for a first-time visitor?

*Situation:* In a product case exercise, the interviewer framed discovery as a core problem because most new visitors arrive on Tiqets without a specific experience in mind.

*Task:* My task was to identify the biggest friction in the zero-to-booked journey and propose one high-impact improvement with a clear rationale.

*Action:* I mapped the typical journey: land on a city page, scroll through a generic category grid, feel overwhelmed by choice, leave without booking. I proposed a lightweight 'trip intent' onboarding card at entry, three questions asked once (trip length, travel style, group type) that personalise the homepage ranking and surface a curated 'Start Here' bundle. I argued for this over search improvements because intent capture happens before search behaviour does.

*Result:* This was a hypothetical case, but I backed it with a reasoning model: if the onboarding card improves click-through on featured cards by even a modest margin, the effect compounds through the funnel into meaningfully more completed bookings. The interviewer followed up by asking how I would A/B test the onboarding step without hurting new user activation, which opened a strong discussion about holdout design.

---

Q: Describe a time you aligned engineering and business stakeholders who disagreed on scope.

*Situation:* At a previous company, the business team wanted a loyalty programme shipped before a major travel industry event. Engineering said the full scope, including points redemption, could not be delivered in time.

*Task:* As the PM, I needed a path that met the business deadline without shipping something broken or burning out the team.

*Action:* I ran a structured scope negotiation where I asked both sides to rank features by what customers would actually experience on day one versus day thirty. We agreed to ship earn-only loyalty at the event date (fully visible and demoable) and commit redemption to a specific date in the next sprint, not a vague 'later'. I documented this as a written agreement both teams signed off on.

*Result:* We hit the event date, the business team had a live demo, and redemption shipped on the agreed date three weeks later. Both teams felt heard, and the written commitment removed the ambiguity that typically causes friction at the next planning cycle.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For product improvement and design questions (Questions 1, 4, 9, 12): Start by clarifying the goal and the user segment before proposing anything. Map the current experience step by step and identify where users face the most friction. Propose one to two focused improvements, rank them by impact and effort, and explain how you would validate them. Avoid listing every possible feature. Interviewers want to see prioritisation thinking, not open-ended brainstorming.

For prioritisation and tradeoff questions (Questions 2, 11): Name the framework you are using (RICE, ICE, north star alignment) and apply it visibly. For marketplace tradeoffs, explicitly model both sides: how does the decision affect supply-side health (venue retention, inventory quality) and demand-side conversion (visitor booking rate)? A strong answer acknowledges that short-term and long-term incentives can point in different directions and explains how you resolve that tension.

For metrics and measurement questions (Question 6): Use a layered structure. Start with the north star metric for the feature (the outcome you ultimately care about), then name the leading indicators that tell you it is on track, and finally note the guardrail metrics that would signal harm elsewhere. For a 'last-minute deals' feature, the north star might be incremental bookings from undecided visitors, with click-through rate and booking completion rate as leading indicators, and cancellation rate as a guardrail.

For stakeholder alignment questions (Questions 3, 8): Lead with the constraint each side was optimising for. Show that you listened before proposing a solution. A strong answer includes how you documented the decision and who owned each follow-through item.

For strategy and market questions (Questions 7, 11): Use a simple structure: market context, the specific challenge or opportunity, your recommended approach, and the first concrete steps you would take. Ground your strategy in one or two specific product bets rather than staying at a theoretical level.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on candidate reports, Tiqets interviewers typically look for four things across all rounds.

Marketplace fluency. Candidates who understand that Tiqets has two very different customers and that optimising for one can hurt the other consistently perform better. Show that you think about venue health and inventory quality, not just visitor conversion.

Structured thinking under ambiguity. Product cases at Tiqets are often deliberately open-ended. Interviewers want to see how you frame a problem before you solve it. Candidates who jump to feature lists without first defining the user, the goal, and the constraint tend to score poorly.

Data comfort paired with judgment. Tiqets is described as a data-informed company, and interviewers expect you to speak about metrics naturally. But they also value judgment: knowing when to act on qualitative signals or when a dataset is too thin to draw conclusions is as important as knowing how to design an A/B test.

Cross-functional empathy. Tiqets teams work across engineering, venue partnerships, design, and marketing. Candidates who describe working with non-PM stakeholders as partners rather than placing themselves at the centre of every story tend to stand out.

A global, curious mindset. Tiqets serves customers across many countries and cultures. Candidates who show genuine curiosity about different user contexts and can think beyond their home market tend to resonate well in later rounds.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Weeks one and two: understand the product directly. Use the Tiqets app as a visitor browsing at least two different cities. Map the full discovery-to-checkout flow yourself. Note where the experience feels smooth and where it creates friction. This direct observation gives you specific, credible material for product case questions that generic PM prep cannot replicate.

Weeks one and two continued: study the marketplace model. Understand how Tiqets earns revenue (commissions from venues) and why venue retention matters as much as visitor acquisition. Read about two-sided marketplace dynamics broadly. This will ground your supply-demand tradeoff answers in a way that impresses interviewers who hear generic responses all day.

Week two: practise cases out loud. Pick two or three of the twelve questions in the section above and answer them out loud, targeting about ten to twelve minutes each. Record yourself if possible. The goal is to hear whether your structure is audible and your reasoning is clear without sounding memorised.

Week two continued: prepare your STAR stories. Have at least four ready: a time you used data to make a decision, a time you prioritised under pressure, a time you managed a difficult stakeholder, and a time a product you worked on fell short of expectations and what you learned. Tailor each story to surface a skill Tiqets would value in that specific role.

Before each round: Review the specific job description for your role. Tiqets had 9 open PM roles as of mid-2026, covering different parts of the product. Understand which area your role owns and prepare one or two specific observations about that area. Interviewers notice role-specific research immediately.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Ignoring the supply side. Many candidates prepare for consumer product interviews and focus entirely on visitor experience. At a two-sided marketplace, overlooking what venues need signals that you have not understood the business model. Bring up venue health unprompted in at least one answer.

Jumping to solutions too early. When given an open-ended case, candidates who immediately list features without first defining the problem, the user segment, and the success metric tend to score poorly. Spend the first two to three minutes structuring before proposing anything.

Vague metrics. Saying 'I would track engagement' without specifying what the metric is, how it is measured, and what a good result looks like reads as shallow. Always name a specific metric (for example: 'percentage of sessions resulting in a completed booking within the same visit').

Generic marketplace answers. Saying 'I would balance supply and demand' without explaining how you would do that for Tiqets specifically is a missed opportunity. Refer to the venue partnership model, the types of attractions on the platform, or the advance-purchase versus day-of booking dynamic.

Softening failure stories. Candidates sometimes reframe setbacks as disguised successes to avoid sounding negative. Tiqets interviewers typically value honest self-assessment. A clear 'here is what went wrong, here is why, and here is what I changed' answer builds more trust than a polished spin.

Arriving with no questions. Candidates who have nothing to ask signal low interest. Prepare two to three questions about the product roadmap, the biggest challenge the PM team is currently solving, or how success is measured for this specific role.

How many rounds does the Tiqets PM interview typically involve?

Candidates report the process typically runs three to five rounds. This usually includes a recruiter screen, a hiring manager conversation, one or two product case or design rounds, and a final panel or leadership discussion. Some roles also include a take-home exercise. The exact structure varies by level and team, so it is worth asking the recruiter upfront what to expect for your specific role.

Does Tiqets give take-home case studies, or are all cases done live?

Based on candidate reports, both formats appear depending on the role and the hiring team. Some candidates describe a written take-home case with a day or two to complete it, while others report all cases were conducted live in a structured interview. Ask your recruiter which format applies to your role so you can prepare the right way.
