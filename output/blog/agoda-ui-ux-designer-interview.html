02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up consistently in Agoda UI/UX Designer interviews, based on candidate reports:

  1. Walk me through a project in your portfolio where you had to simplify a complex user flow.
  2. How do you use data and analytics to validate a design decision? Give a specific example.
  3. Describe a time you ran or contributed to an A/B test. What did you learn from it?
  4. How do you design for users from different cultural backgrounds or who speak different languages?
  5. Tell me about a time a stakeholder disagreed with your design direction. How did you handle it?
  6. How do you balance shipping fast with maintaining design quality?
  7. Walk us through how you would redesign the hotel search results page for mobile users.
  8. How do you approach designing for accessibility on mobile devices?
  9. Describe your process when you receive vague or conflicting requirements from a product manager.
  10. Tell me about a time your design did not perform as expected after launch. What did you do next?
  11. How do you build and maintain design systems for large-scale products used across multiple markets?
  12. How do you prioritise which user pain points to address when time and resources are limited?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk me through a project where you simplified a complex user flow.

*Situation:* At my previous company, our mobile checkout flow had a significant drop-off rate that the product team flagged as a priority problem worth solving before the next release.

*Task:* I was asked to redesign the experience without removing any required input fields and without a major engineering overhaul.

*Action:* I ran usability sessions with a small group of participants and found that users felt overwhelmed when they saw all fields on a single screen. I restructured the flow into three sequential steps with a progress indicator, and worked with the engineering team to enable auto-fill for stored user preferences. I kept the PM and dev lead updated throughout so there were no surprises at handoff.

*Result:* After launch, the product team tracked a meaningful improvement in checkout completion. The redesign became the template for two other flows on the app, and the PM cited it in a team review as an example of collaborative, evidence-based design.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you ran an A/B test and what you learned.

*Situation:* On a travel booking product I worked on, there were two competing ideas for displaying pricing in search results: showing the total cost upfront versus showing a nightly rate first.

*Task:* My job was to design both variants and help the product manager define the right success metric before the experiment launched.

*Action:* I created both variants in Figma and documented the hypothesis clearly before any testing began. The hypothesis was: users who see the total price first will complete bookings at a higher rate. I collaborated with the data team to set up the experiment and made sure both variants passed our accessibility checks before handoff.

*Result:* The total-price variant performed better. More importantly, writing the hypothesis first made the post-test analysis faster and the learnings sharper. I now do this as a habit on every experiment I am involved in.

---

Q: Describe a time a stakeholder disagreed with your design direction.

*Situation:* A product manager wanted a promotional banner placed at the top of our homepage. My concern was that it would push the primary search bar below the fold on smaller screens.

*Task:* I needed to either find a workable compromise or make a clear case for keeping the current layout.

*Action:* Instead of simply pushing back, I pulled up heatmap data showing that the search bar was the most interacted element on the page. I then designed two alternate banner placements that kept the search bar visible and presented both options with a brief rationale for each.

*Result:* The PM chose one of the alternate placements. The banner launched, search interaction held steady, and the PM later mentioned that coming with options rather than objections had built trust for future design discussions.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions. Every 'tell me about a time' question expects a clear Situation, Task, Action, and Result. Keep the Situation brief (one or two sentences), spend most of your time on Action (the specific decisions and steps you took), and close with a concrete Result, even if it is qualitative.

Structured design process for case questions. When asked to redesign something (like the hotel search page), follow this order: clarify goals and constraints, identify the user and their core need, map current pain points, sketch a few directions, explain your chosen direction, then describe how you would validate it. Jumping straight to a solution is the most common case interview mistake.

Data anchoring. Agoda values evidence. When you make any design claim, attach it to a user research finding, a usability observation, or a metric. Saying 'users told us during testing' or 'the drop in completion rate showed us' carries far more weight than 'I felt this was cleaner.'

Explicit trade-off framing. Interviewers want to see that you think in trade-offs. When you explain a design decision, name what you gave up and why that was the right call given the constraints. This signals maturity and real-world product experience.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Data fluency. Agoda's design culture is built around experimentation. Interviewers want to see that you are comfortable with metrics, A/B testing concepts, and using research findings to justify decisions, not just personal taste.

Cross-functional collaboration. Agoda teams move quickly across product, engineering, and data. They look for designers who communicate clearly with non-designers and who involve developers early rather than handing off polished files at the last moment.

Global and cross-cultural thinking. Agoda serves users from many countries with different languages and mental models. Being able to describe how you have thought about localisation, right-to-left layouts, or culturally different user behaviours is a meaningful differentiator in interviews.

Ownership and initiative. Candidates who show they proactively spotted a problem and drove the solution, rather than waiting to be assigned work, consistently stand out based on publicly reported interview feedback.

Portfolio depth over breadth. Bring two or three well-developed case studies rather than many shallow ones. Interviewers want to go deep on your thinking, your trade-offs, and what actually happened after launch.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Prepare your portfolio case studies. Choose two or three projects that show your full design process: the problem, your research approach, the key decisions with rationale, and the outcomes. At least one should involve a mobile product, and at least one should show how you used data or user testing to shape a decision.

Step 2: Research Agoda's product hands-on. Spend time using the Agoda app as a traveller would. Notice specific flows that feel smooth and ones that feel clunky. Be ready to discuss particular screens with specific observations, not generic praise.

Step 3: Prepare your STAR stories in advance. Write out answers to the questions listed above using STAR format before your interview date. Practise saying them aloud so they feel natural rather than memorised.

Step 4: Brush up on A/B testing concepts. You do not need to be a data scientist. You should be able to explain what a hypothesis is, what control and variant mean, and how you would define a success metric for a design change you care about.

Step 5: Prepare thoughtful questions for your interviewer. Ask how design decisions get prioritised, what collaboration between design and data teams looks like, and what a typical sprint looks like for the design team. Specific questions signal genuine interest.

Step 6: Read the job description closely. Match your portfolio examples to the skills the specific role lists. If the JD mentions design systems or a particular platform, lead with examples from that area.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping to solutions in case interviews. Many candidates start wireframing before they understand the user or the goal. Interviewers notice this immediately. Ask clarifying questions first, even if it feels like slowing down.

Showing portfolio work without outcomes. Beautiful screens without explaining what happened post-launch miss the point at Agoda. Even if results were mixed or you are still measuring, talk about what you tracked and what you learned.

Avoiding data entirely. Saying 'I chose this design because it felt more intuitive' without supporting evidence is a red flag at a company that runs frequent design experiments. Tie decisions to research, testing, or metrics, even when the sample was small.

Trying to solve every edge case in a timed design exercise. Candidates who attempt to cover every scenario run out of time and lose focus on the core user problem. Solve the primary flow well first, then mention what you would tackle next if time allowed.

Not asking questions during the exercise. At Agoda, asking smart questions about constraints, users, and goals is itself a design skill being evaluated. Staying silent and just building is read as a lack of curiosity.

Generic portfolio walkthroughs. Saying 'I redesigned the homepage and users responded well' tells the interviewer very little. Be specific about the problem, what you tried first, what failed, and what ultimately worked.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Agoda UI/UX Designer interview typically have?

Candidates typically report a process that includes a portfolio review call, a design exercise or take-home assignment, and one or two interview rounds covering behavioural and design thinking questions. The exact number of rounds can vary by team and seniority level. Always confirm the full process with your recruiter at the start so you can prepare each stage properly.

What should my portfolio include for an Agoda UI/UX Designer application?

Include two or three detailed case studies that show your end-to-end design process: the problem, your research approach, the decisions you made and why, and the outcome after launch. At least one case study should cover a mobile product. Agoda values evidence-based design, so highlight any A/B tests, usability sessions, or metrics tied to your work.

Does Agoda give a design assignment, and how should I approach it?

Candidates commonly report receiving a take-home or live design exercise as part of the process. Treat it like a real product problem: ask clarifying questions first, define the user and their core need, and walk through your thinking step by step. Show trade-offs and explain what you would validate before shipping. A clear, well-reasoned process is valued more than a pixel-perfect output without rationale.

What salary range can I expect for a UI/UX Designer role at Agoda in India?

Agoda has not published official salary bands for India-based design roles. Glassdoor and levels.fyi list a broad range depending on seniority, city, and experience. Research current figures on those platforms for the most up-to-date publicly reported numbers, and factor in total compensation including any performance or stock components when comparing offers.

How important is design systems experience for this role?

Design systems experience is commonly cited as a strong differentiator for product design roles at large tech companies like Agoda. Being able to show that you have contributed to, maintained, or scaled a component library will strengthen your application. If your direct experience is limited, focus on examples where you worked within structured design constraints and maintained consistency across a product.
