Most Asked Questions

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use STAR for every behavioural question: Situation (brief context), Task (what was expected of you), Action (what you personally did), Result (a concrete, ideally tracked outcome). Here are three examples.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you redesigned something because the first version did not meet user needs.

*Situation:* I was working on the onboarding flow for a mobile fintech product. After launch, we saw a high drop-off at the document upload step.

*Task:* I was asked to understand why users abandoned at that point and improve the completion rate.

*Action:* I ran five moderated usability sessions with target users, mostly first-time app users from Tier-2 cities. I found that error messages were too technical and that the camera permission prompt appeared before users understood why it was needed. I redesigned the flow to show a checklist of required items upfront, moved the permission request to the moment it was actually needed, and rewrote every error message in plain language.

*Result:* The redesigned flow showed a meaningful improvement in completion rates in the next test cycle, per the product team's internal dashboard. The team shipped the updated version within two weeks.

---

Q: How do you handle a stakeholder who pushes back on your design recommendations?

*Situation:* At a previous role, a marketing lead wanted a landing page with several CTAs because they wanted to 'cover all bases.' My research suggested this would reduce conversion.

*Task:* I needed alignment on a single primary CTA without making the stakeholder feel their input was dismissed.

*Action:* Instead of debating opinions, I shared competitor examples and publicly reported conversion data from industry surveys showing what focused CTAs typically achieve. I proposed a compromise: one primary CTA above the fold, with secondary options further down, and a two-week A/B test to let the data decide.

*Result:* The stakeholder agreed to the test. The focused version performed better, and the marketing team became more receptive to design rationale in future reviews.

---

Q: Describe your process for user research when the timeline is very tight.

*Situation:* We had two days to validate a checkout redesign before the development sprint kicked off.

*Task:* I had to get real user signal without a full research cycle.

*Action:* I ran quick sessions with five colleagues from non-product teams using a low-fidelity prototype, then followed up with three remote calls with target users. I focused only on the questions whose answers would actually change the design direction. I delivered a one-page brief instead of a full research report.

*Result:* We caught a critical navigation confusion that would have caused a dev rework mid-sprint. The engineering lead estimated it saved roughly a week of back-and-forth, though that was an informal assessment.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

The STAR method is the most reliable structure for behavioural questions: Situation (one or two sentences of context), Task (what was expected of you specifically), Action (what you personally did, step by step), Result (a concrete outcome, ideally one that was tracked or measured by the team).

Portfolio walkthrough: Open with the business problem, not the visual. Walk through your process: discovery, define, design, test. Show at least one moment where the design changed because of what users told you. Close with the outcome and what you would approach differently today.

Design rationale: When asked 'why did you design it this way,' structure your answer as: user need, plus the constraint, plus the tradeoff. Example: 'The user needed to complete this task on a low-end Android with limited data. Given that constraint, I chose a skeleton screen over a spinner because publicly reported research on loading UX shows it reduces perceived wait time.'

Stakeholder disagreement: Acknowledge their goal, share your evidence, and offer a testable middle path. Avoid framing the story as 'I was right and they were wrong.' Career Maker Consultant Bpc places designers into client environments, so interviewers want to see that you can work constructively with people who are not designers.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Process over polish. Recruiters screening for consultancy placements need to be confident your work will hold up in front of a client. They want to hear you explain why you made each decision, not just see the final interface.

Communication that lands with business audiences. Since Career Maker Consultant Bpc places candidates across industries, they look for designers who can translate design decisions into business outcomes. Saying 'I reduced cognitive load' is weaker than saying 'users completed the task faster and support tickets for that feature dropped, based on the product team's tracking.'

Adaptability across contexts. A consultancy places you in varied environments. Show examples from more than one product type or industry if you have them. If you do not, explain clearly how your skills transfer to a new domain.

Self-awareness. Interviewers respond well to candidates who say 'I do not have deep experience in X, but here is how I approached a similar challenge.' Overconfidence without examples is a consistent red flag.

Individual ownership. Use 'I' for your specific contributions, even when the project was a team effort. Interviewers are evaluating you, not your team.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Three to five days before the interview:

Choose two or three portfolio projects that together show: a complete design process including research, a moment of stakeholder pushback you navigated, and a design that changed because of user feedback. Prepare a five-minute spoken walkthrough for each.

Write STAR answers for the twelve questions in this guide. Practice them out loud, not just in your head. Notice where you use vague words like 'we' or 'good' and replace them with specifics.

Look up Career Maker Consultant Bpc on LinkedIn. Check which client sectors appear most in their active listings and adjust your examples to match where possible.

One to two days before:

Do a practice run with a friend or record yourself on your phone. Watch for answers that run over two minutes, long pauses, and filler words.

Prepare your salary expectation answer. Current bands are 6-12 LPA at entry level, 14-24 LPA at mid-level, and 26-40 LPA at senior level. Decide on your number and say it clearly when asked.

Prepare two to three questions to ask the interviewer: about the client profile they are hiring for, the design team size, and what success looks like in the first three months.

Day of:

Test your portfolio link or Figma file before the call. Share only the relevant project tab, not your full desktop. Keep a cheat sheet of your STAR bullet points nearby.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Showing only final designs. Interviewers at consultancies want to see early-stage work too: sketches, rejected directions, and research notes. A polished portfolio with no visible process signals you cannot explain your thinking under pressure.

Vague outcomes. 'The product improved' is weaker than 'the team tracked a drop in support tickets for that flow after the redesign.' If you cannot share numbers, describe what was measured and by whom.

Over-crediting the team. Using 'we' throughout makes it impossible for the interviewer to assess your individual contribution. Use 'I' for your actions, even in collaborative projects.

Skipping the adaptability question. Career Maker Consultant Bpc places designers across many sectors. If you only discuss one domain, you may seem like a poor fit for varied client work. Highlight how your skills transfer even if your direct experience in a given sector is limited.

Not asking about the placement. Failing to ask what kind of client you would be placed with, or what the team structure looks like, reads as low interest. Interviewers consistently notice when a candidate asks nothing.

Going in blind on salary. Know your number before the call. Publicly reported data on Glassdoor and industry surveys commonly cite mid-level Product Designer figures consistent with the 14-24 LPA band. Have a specific figure ready rather than saying 'whatever is the market rate.'

Q Questions

Frequently asked

Is the Career Maker Consultant Bpc interview a screening round or the final step?

Career Maker Consultant Bpc is a recruitment consultancy, so their interview is typically a screening round before your profile is forwarded to the client company. Candidates report that this round covers portfolio fit, communication style, and salary alignment. A separate process with the client usually follows, which may include a design task or a panel interview.

How many rounds does the full Product Designer hiring process typically involve?

Candidates typically report one to two touchpoints with Career Maker Consultant Bpc directly: an initial screening call and sometimes a portfolio review. After that, the client company runs its own rounds. The total number depends on the client and the seniority of the role, so ask the recruiter to walk you through the full pipeline after your first call.

Do I need to prepare a take-home design task in advance?

For the consultancy screening itself, a strong portfolio is usually sufficient. If the role progresses to a client interview, a design task is common practice across the industry. Prepare two to three case studies you can walk through verbally, and have your Figma files or a PDF portfolio ready to share on screen without fumbling for the link.

What salary should I quote and how do I negotiate?

Current market bands run from 6-12 LPA at entry level (0-2 years), 14-24 LPA at mid-level (3-5 years), and 26-40 LPA at senior level (6-9 years). Lead and Principal roles start at 36 LPA and go higher, with publicly reported figures from levels.fyi and industry surveys commonly cited in the 36-55+ LPA range. State a specific number rather than a vague range, and be ready to back it up with your years of experience and portfolio quality.

How do I stand out when the consultancy is screening many candidates?

Make your process visible, not just your final output. Recruiters who see dozens of portfolios remember designers who explain what problem they solved and what changed because of user feedback, not just how the screen looks. Keep your STAR answers specific and under two minutes. Show at least one example where you adapted your approach to an unfamiliar context or user group.

Which city has the most Product Designer openings right now?

Based on knok's jobradar data from July 2026, Bangalore leads with 62 active Product Designer openings, followed by Delhi at 33 and Mumbai at 13. If you are open to relocation, Bangalore and Delhi give you the widest pipeline. Remote and hybrid listings are increasingly common for this role, so staying flexible on location can open up options well beyond these three cities.
