01 Overview

Overview

Haptik is one of India's leading conversational AI platforms, now part of Reliance Industries. Their DevOps team keeps high-traffic chatbot and virtual assistant infrastructure running reliably across cloud environments. With 16 open DevOps roles currently listed, the team is actively growing.

Candidates typically go through a screening call, a technical round covering infrastructure and CI/CD pipelines, and a system design or architecture discussion. Final rounds often include a culture and team-fit conversation. The entire process typically spans a few weeks, according to candidate reports.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly in Haptik DevOps interviews, based on candidate reports and the nature of their conversational AI platform:

  1. Walk me through how you would set up a CI/CD pipeline for a microservices-based application.
  2. How have you managed Kubernetes clusters in production? Describe a challenging incident.
  3. Haptik handles millions of bot interactions daily. How would you design auto-scaling for such a workload?
  4. What is your approach to infrastructure as code? Which tools have you used (Terraform, Pulumi, Ansible)?
  5. How do you monitor a distributed system? Which metrics, logs, and alerting tools do you rely on?
  6. Describe a production outage you were involved in. How did you diagnose and fix it, and what did you put in place afterward?
  7. How do you handle secrets management and environment-specific configuration across staging and production?
  8. What strategies do you use for zero-downtime deployments (blue-green, canary, rolling updates)?
  9. How would you reduce cloud infrastructure costs without compromising reliability or performance?
  10. Haptik's services need low-latency responses. How would you tune a Kubernetes deployment for latency-sensitive workloads?
  11. How do you approach container image security, vulnerability scanning, and supply chain hygiene?
  12. Describe how you have implemented or improved observability (metrics, logs, traces) in a previous role.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use these as templates and adapt them to your own experience.

Q: Describe a production outage you handled end-to-end.

*Situation:* Our primary API service started returning errors for a subset of users during peak traffic hours.

*Task:* I was the on-call engineer and needed to identify the root cause and restore service quickly.

*Action:* I checked our Grafana dashboards first and spotted elevated memory usage on two pods. I used 'kubectl top pods' to confirm, then looked at recent deployment logs. A config change had introduced a memory leak in a new image. I rolled back the deployment using 'kubectl rollout undo', verified pod health, and updated the runbook with steps to catch similar issues before they reach production.

*Result:* The service recovered and user-facing errors dropped to zero. The team added a memory-usage threshold alert so the same pattern would page on-call earlier next time.

---

Q: How have you used infrastructure as code in practice?

*Situation:* Our team was manually provisioning cloud resources, which made environments inconsistent and slowed down new feature work.

*Task:* I was asked to migrate our infrastructure to Terraform so that all environments could be reproduced reliably.

*Action:* I started by auditing existing resources, then wrote Terraform modules for networking, compute, and managed services. I set up remote state with locking in an S3 bucket, created separate workspaces for dev, staging, and production, and added a plan-approval step in our CI pipeline so changes were reviewed before apply.

*Result:* Environment drift disappeared, and spinning up a new staging environment became a single pipeline run instead of a multi-hour manual task.

---

Q: How would you design auto-scaling for a real-time chatbot platform?

*Situation:* A previous employer ran a customer support bot that saw sharp traffic spikes during business hours and promotions.

*Task:* I needed to make sure the platform could handle sudden load without over-provisioning during quiet periods.

*Action:* I configured Horizontal Pod Autoscaler in Kubernetes based on custom metrics (message queue depth and CPU) rather than CPU alone, since CPU alone lagged behind real demand. I also set pod disruption budgets and tuned liveness and readiness probes so that scale-out pods received traffic only when genuinely ready. I used cluster autoscaler to add nodes when pending pods were detected.

*Result:* The platform absorbed traffic spikes without dropped messages, and infrastructure costs during off-peak hours fell noticeably compared to the previous fixed-node setup.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For incident and troubleshooting questions, follow a Diagnose-Contain-Fix-Prevent structure. Start with how you detected the issue (metrics, alerts, user reports), what you did to limit damage while investigating, the root cause and fix, and what you changed afterward to prevent recurrence. Interviewers want to see that you learn from incidents, not just put out fires.

For design questions (pipelines, scaling, observability), use a Requirements-Architecture-Trade-offs pattern. State the requirements you are optimising for (latency, cost, reliability), describe your architecture, then call out the trade-offs you made. Haptik's real-time workloads mean latency and reliability usually outrank cost in your trade-off discussion.

For tools or technology questions, always pair tool names with outcomes. Saying 'I used Prometheus and Grafana' is weak. Saying 'I used Prometheus and Grafana, which let us catch a memory regression in staging before it hit production' is strong.

For behavioural questions, use the STAR structure: Situation (one or two sentences of context), Task (your specific responsibility), Action (what you did and why), Result (what changed, ideally in observable terms). Keep Situation and Task brief so Action gets the most airtime.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Haptik's interviewers are primarily looking for engineers who can keep high-throughput, real-time services healthy without constant manual intervention. Candidates report that the following signals matter most:

Platform depth over tool breadth. Knowing Kubernetes well, including networking, storage, and autoscaling internals, impresses more than listing a long set of tools you have touched lightly.

Operational instinct. Interviewers want to see that you think about failure modes before they happen. Bring up concepts like circuit breakers, graceful degradation, and alerting thresholds unprompted.

Communication during incidents. Haptik serves enterprise clients, so clear and calm incident communication matters. Mention stakeholder updates and postmortems in your answers, not just the technical fix.

Cost awareness. Conversational AI platforms scale unpredictably. Showing that you think about resource efficiency (right-sizing, spot instances, autoscaling) signals maturity.

Ownership mindset. Candidates who describe 'my pipeline' or 'my runbook' rather than 'the pipeline' or 'what the team did' come across as engineers who take responsibility rather than spectators.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Core Kubernetes and CI/CD
Revise Kubernetes objects (Deployments, StatefulSets, DaemonSets, HPA, PDB), networking (Services, Ingress, NetworkPolicy), and resource management. Practice writing a Dockerfile and a Helm chart from scratch. Set up a small local cluster with Kind or Minikube if you do not have one running.

Week 2: Infrastructure as Code and Cloud
Build or revisit a Terraform project covering VPC, compute, and a managed database. Focus on modules, remote state, and workspace separation. Review IAM policies and the principle of least privilege on whichever cloud (AWS or GCP) the Haptik role you are applying to mentions.

Week 3: Observability and Incident Practice
Set up a Prometheus and Grafana stack, even on your laptop. Write alert rules for common failure modes. Practise writing a brief incident postmortem for a real outage you have dealt with, and rehearse telling the story clearly and concisely.

Week 4: Mock Interviews and Haptik Context
Read Haptik's engineering blog to understand their architecture and scale. Do at least two timed mock interviews covering system design and past-experience questions. Prepare questions to ask interviewers about on-call culture, deployment frequency, and how the DevOps team collaborates with product squads.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Listing tools without outcomes. Saying 'I know Terraform, Ansible, Jenkins, and ArgoCD' without a story behind each tool tells interviewers nothing about your depth.

Vague incident stories. 'We had an outage and I fixed it' is not enough. Be specific about what you observed, what you ruled out, and what you changed.

Ignoring the scale context. Haptik handles real-time messaging at scale. Answers that assume a low-traffic environment (fixed node counts, manual deployments) signal a mismatch with the role.

Not mentioning monitoring and alerting proactively. Many candidates describe building a pipeline or a cluster without mentioning how they would know if it broke. Bring up observability in every relevant answer.

Skipping trade-offs in design questions. Saying 'I would use canary deployments' is less impressive than explaining why canary fits this specific risk profile while acknowledging that rolling updates might be simpler for lower-stakes services.

Underselling soft skills. DevOps roles at product companies involve constant collaboration with developers and on-call responsibilities. If you only talk about tools, you miss an opportunity to show that you can work effectively across teams.
