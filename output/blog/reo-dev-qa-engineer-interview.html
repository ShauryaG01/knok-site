03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for all behavioural questions. Here are three worked examples.

Q: Tell me about the most critical bug you caught before it reached production.

*Situation:* Our team was pushing a payment integration update two days before a major product launch.
*Task:* I was responsible for end-to-end testing of the checkout flow, including negative paths for failed transactions.
*Action:* While testing with an expired card, I noticed the system was silently marking the order as 'paid' instead of returning an error state. I replicated it across three card types, documented the exact API response mismatch, and immediately flagged it to the dev lead and product manager with a severity-1 label and clear steps to reproduce.
*Result:* The bug was patched within a few hours and the launch went ahead on schedule. The incident led the team to add negative-path tests to the standard regression suite going forward.

---

Q: How do you handle flaky tests in a CI/CD pipeline?

*Situation:* At my previous company, a large portion of our Selenium tests were failing intermittently, blocking builds multiple times a week.
*Task:* I was asked to reduce flakiness without significantly delaying pipeline runs.
*Action:* I audited the failing tests and grouped them by root cause: timing issues, test data conflicts, and environment instability. I replaced hardcoded sleeps with explicit waits, isolated test data per run using factories, and quarantined the remaining unstable tests with a 'flaky' tag so they ran in a separate nightly job rather than blocking the main pipeline.
*Result:* Build flakiness dropped substantially over the following weeks, and the team could trust the green signal again, which improved release confidence noticeably.

---

Q: How do you handle a developer disagreeing with your bug report?

*Situation:* I had filed a bug showing that a search filter returned incorrect results for certain Unicode inputs.
*Task:* The developer felt the edge case was not worth fixing before launch, and I needed to find a way forward without it becoming a standoff.
*Action:* Instead of escalating immediately, I prepared a short document showing three real user scenarios where the issue would surface, with a clear comparison of expected versus actual output. I framed it as a risk discussion with the product manager present rather than a technical dispute.
*Result:* The team agreed to fix the most common case before launch and scheduled the full fix for the next sprint. The product manager appreciated being looped in early, and the approach became a template for future severity discussions.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the core framework for behavioural questions. Lead with just enough context in the Situation and Task (2-3 sentences combined), spend most of your time on the Action describing what you specifically did rather than what the team did, and close with a concrete Result. If you do not have a metric, describe the impact in terms of team trust, release speed, or defect rate.

Risk-based framing works well for questions about test planning and prioritisation. Structure your answer around three points: what could go wrong, how likely is it, and how severe would the impact be. Interviewers at product companies want to see that you think like someone protecting the user, not just completing a test case list.

The 'why, what, how' ladder is useful for tool or process questions. Start with the problem you were solving (why), then what you chose, then how you implemented it. This shows that your technical decisions are driven by context, not just habit.

Clarifying questions as a signal is a small but effective move. Before answering design questions like 'how would you test X', briefly ask one clarifying question about scope or risk tolerance. It shows you think before acting, which is exactly what a strong QA candidate does.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Ownership over execution. reo-dev interviewers, like most product-company QA panels, want to see that you treat quality as your responsibility, not just a checklist handed to you. Answers that show you proactively flagged risk, suggested process improvements, or pushed back constructively on timelines carry more weight than answers that show you followed instructions well.

Automation that serves the team. Candidates who talk about how their test suite helped the team ship faster, not just how many tests they wrote, tend to stand out. Expect follow-up questions about flakiness, maintenance burden, and whether developers actually trusted the results.

Clear communication under disagreement. QA sits at the intersection of developer speed and user protection. Interviewers want to see that you can make your case clearly, use data and examples, and de-escalate without dropping the quality bar.

Curiosity about the product. Candidates who ask good questions about the product, the user base, or the team's biggest quality pain points leave a stronger impression than those who only answer what is asked. Prepare two or three genuine questions about where quality fits in reo-dev's engineering culture.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Technical foundations

Review the automation framework you know best and be ready to explain every design decision, not just the syntax. Practice writing test cases for an API from scratch: start with the happy path, then error codes, then edge cases like empty payloads and rate limits. Revisit concepts like the test pyramid, shift-left testing, and CI/CD integration.

Week 2: Behavioural prep and company research

Pick five stories from your career that cover: catching a critical bug, handling conflict with a developer, setting up a QA process, dealing with incomplete requirements, and improving automation reliability. Write each one out in STAR format and time yourself to keep each under two minutes. Research reo-dev's product and any public mentions of their engineering culture.

Days before the interview

Do a mock run with a peer or record yourself answering two technical and two behavioural questions. Check that you are saying 'I' when describing your contribution, not 'we'. Prepare three questions to ask the interviewer: one about team structure, one about the biggest quality challenge the team currently faces, and one about how QA success is measured.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Saying 'we' instead of 'I'. The interviewer is evaluating your contribution, not your team's. Use 'I' when describing what you specifically did, even if it was a collaborative effort.

Treating automation as the goal. Saying 'I automated everything' without explaining what problem it solved or what tradeoffs you made signals inexperience. Strong QA candidates also talk about what they chose not to automate and why.

Skipping the result. Many candidates give a detailed Situation and Action but trail off before the Result. The result is what proves your action worked. If you do not have a clean metric, describe the business or team outcome instead.

Being defensive about bugs that shipped. If asked about a defect that got past you, own it briefly, explain what you learned, and describe what you changed in your process. Blaming the deadline or another team member is a red flag for most interviewers.

Not asking questions at the end. Finishing with 'I think you covered everything' signals low curiosity about the role. Bring at least two genuine questions about the team or product.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the reo-dev QA Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates typically report 2-4 rounds: a short HR or recruiter screen, one or two technical rounds covering automation and scenario-based questions, and a final round with a hiring manager or senior team member. The exact structure can vary by team, so confirm the format with your recruiter after the first call. Some candidates also report a short take-home or live coding task as part of the technical round.

What automation tools should I prepare for?

Focus on the tools you have actually used rather than trying to cram new ones before the interview. Most QA roles in 2026 expect familiarity with at least one framework such as Selenium, Playwright, or Cypress, plus experience integrating tests into a CI/CD tool like Jenkins or GitHub Actions. Be ready to explain why you chose your stack and what its limitations are, not just how to use it. Depth on one tool is more impressive than surface-level knowledge of five.

What salary should I expect as a QA Engineer?

Based on industry data for QA Engineers in India, entry-level roles (0-2 years experience) typically fall in the 4-9 LPA range, mid-level (3-5 years) in the 9-17 LPA range, and senior roles (6-9 years) in the 17-30 LPA range. Lead roles are commonly cited at 28-45+ LPA. Actual offers depend on the company stage, your specific skills, and how well you negotiate.

Is there a coding round in QA Engineer interviews at product companies?

It depends on the role. For automation-heavy QA positions, candidates often report a practical task such as writing a small test suite, debugging existing automation, or solving a logic problem relevant to test design. Pure manual QA roles are less likely to include a coding round. Check the job description for terms like 'automation', 'SDET', or 'scripting' to gauge how technical the process will be.

How do I answer 'tell me about a bug you missed' without hurting my chances?

Own the miss clearly and briefly, then pivot to what you learned and what you changed in your process afterward. Interviewers ask this question to see self-awareness and a growth mindset, not to trap you. A strong answer follows this shape: 'A defect slipped through in X area, I should have caught it because Y, and after that I added Z to our process.' Blaming the deadline or another team member is the one thing to avoid.

As of mid-2026, there are 459 active QA Engineer openings across India, with the highest concentration in Bangalore at 87 roles and Delhi at 67. The market is active but competitive at the mid-to-senior level, where automation skills and product-company experience are increasingly expected.
