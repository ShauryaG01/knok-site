01 Overview

Overview

Gusto builds payroll, benefits, and HR software for small and mid-sized businesses across the US. Engineering Managers there own both the people side and the delivery pipeline: you grow engineers, shape technical direction for your squad, and partner closely with product and design to ship software that payroll-dependent customers cannot afford to see fail.

Knok's jobradar (data as of July 2026) shows 92 open roles at Gusto across functions. Candidates report a process that typically includes a recruiter screen, a hiring-manager conversation, and a panel loop. The loop usually covers leadership philosophy, a technical-direction discussion, and cross-functional collaboration scenarios. Gusto values empathy for small business owners who find payroll stressful, a high bar for reliability, and managers who build inclusive teams.

For Indian professionals exploring EM-track roles in product companies, knok's market data shows compensation bands of 35-60 LPA at Manager level, 55-90 LPA at Senior Manager, and 90-150+ LPA at Director level.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates at Gusto report a mix of behavioural, situational, and product-aware leadership questions. Here are the ones that come up most often:

  1. Tell me about a low performer on your team. What did you do and how did it resolve?
  2. How do you balance shipping speed with code quality and reliability, especially on a payroll product where bugs affect people's livelihoods?
  3. Describe a time you influenced a major decision without having direct authority.
  4. Walk me through how you run a performance-review cycle for your team.
  5. Gusto serves small businesses where payroll failures are catastrophic. How does that shape how your team approaches engineering?
  6. Tell me about a hard trade-off you made between team health and a business deadline.
  7. How do you set and communicate technical direction for your team? Give a concrete example.
  8. Describe your hiring process for an engineer. What signals do you look for beyond technical skill?
  9. How do you build psychological safety in a team? Give a specific example of what you did.
  10. Walk me through how you handle a production incident. What is your job as the manager during and after?
  11. Tell me about a time you disagreed with a product or business decision pushed from above. What did you do?
  12. How do you structure your 1:1s, and how do you know if they are working?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a time you had a low performer on your team.

*Situation:* One of my senior engineers was missing sprint commitments repeatedly and the team was quietly picking up the slack.

*Task:* I needed to address the performance gap honestly without losing the engineer or damaging team morale.

*Action:* I started with a direct 1:1 conversation to understand what was going on. It turned out the engineer was dealing with scope creep from ad-hoc requests that bypassed our planning process. I worked with the engineer to document a clear set of expectations, blocked off focus time on their calendar, and created a lightweight weekly check-in. I also fixed the planning gap so ad-hoc requests went through triage.

*Result:* The engineer's output improved noticeably over the next sprint cycle. The team noticed the process fix and it reduced interruptions for everyone.

---

Q: How do you handle a production incident as an Engineering Manager?

*Situation:* A payment-processing service my team owned started throwing errors during a peak processing window.

*Task:* My role was not to debug the code myself but to keep communication flowing and get the right people unblocked.

*Action:* I joined the incident channel immediately, confirmed an on-call engineer was leading the technical investigation, and set up a parallel thread for stakeholder updates so the engineers could stay focused. I made sure nobody was working the incident alone and checked in regularly on blockers.

*Result:* The service was restored within the SLA window. The next morning I ran a blameless post-mortem with the full team. We identified two process gaps and shipped fixes to both in the following sprint.

---

Q: Describe a time you disagreed with a product decision pushed from leadership.

*Situation:* Leadership wanted to fast-track a feature that my team flagged as needing significant infrastructure work first.

*Task:* I had to advocate for the right technical path without simply blocking the business goal.

*Action:* I put together a one-page brief showing the risk of skipping the infrastructure work, using a past incident as a concrete data point, and proposed a phased approach that delivered the visible feature milestone on the original date while scheduling the infrastructure work in the following cycle.

*Result:* Leadership agreed to the phased plan. The feature shipped on time and the infrastructure work was completed in the next cycle, preventing a class of reliability issues we had modelled as likely.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the baseline for every behavioural question. Keep Situation and Task short (a couple of sentences combined), spend most of your answer on Action, and close with a clear Result that is specific but honest.

Situation-Complication-Resolution works well for technical-direction questions where the 'task' is less clear. Open with the context, name the tension or competing priority (the complication), then describe how you resolved it.

For 'how do you approach X' questions covering 1:1s, performance reviews, or hiring, Gusto interviewers typically want a concrete example rather than a framework. Lead with 'Here is what I actually do, and here is a specific case where it played out.'

For reliability-and-customer-empathy questions, anchor your answer to the impact on the end user. Gusto's product touches people's paychecks. Interviewers respond well to candidates who talk about failure in terms of customer impact first, then engineering mechanics.

Quantify where you honestly can, but do not inflate numbers. If you improved something, say what changed and how you know it changed. A vague claim like 'significantly improved velocity' lands worse than 'we went from missing every other sprint goal to hitting commitments consistently across multiple quarters.'

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Gusto interviewers typically look for a few specific signals in EM candidates.

Customer empathy, not just product sense. Gusto's customers are small business owners who find payroll stressful and consequential. Interviewers want to see that you think about reliability and correctness in terms of what a missed paycheck means to someone, not just an SLA metric.

Honest people leadership. Expect deep follow-up on performance management and conflict scenarios. They want to hear that you have had hard conversations, not that you have avoided them. Candidates who give only positive outcomes get pushed on what went wrong.

Technical judgment without micromanagement. You should be able to discuss architecture trade-offs, system reliability patterns, and build-vs-buy decisions at a level that earns engineer trust. The signal they are looking for is whether you enable good technical decisions by the team, not whether you make them yourself.

Cross-functional collaboration. Gusto teams work closely with product, design, and go-to-market. Interviewers look for examples of you navigating scope disagreements, aligning on priorities, and building relationships outside your team.

Inclusion and team health. Questions about psychological safety, feedback culture, and building diverse teams come up frequently in candidates' reports. Have specific examples ready, not generic statements about values.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Research Gusto's product and customers. Spend time understanding how Gusto's payroll, benefits, and contractor payments work from a small business owner's perspective. Read public case studies and their engineering blog for insight into the technical challenges their teams solve.

Step 2: Map your experience to their themes. Before your loop, prepare at least one concrete story for each of these themes: low performer, incident management, technical direction, cross-functional disagreement, hiring, and team health. Write them out in STAR format so you are not improvising structure under pressure.

Step 3: Prepare a technical-direction example. Pick one example from your career where you shaped an architectural decision or drove an engineering standard. Be ready to walk through the trade-offs, who you involved, and what the outcome was.

Step 4: Prepare questions for each interviewer. Ask about the team's biggest current challenge, how product and engineering prioritise together, and what the path to Senior Manager or Director looks like. These signal genuine interest and give you information to evaluate the role.

Step 5: Sanity-check your resume metrics. If you cite any figures, be ready to explain how you measured them. Interviewers often ask 'how did you know that was true?' for any claim you make.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Talking about the team instead of your own actions. Interviewers want to know what YOU did. 'We decided to...' and 'The team shipped...' are not answers to a leadership question. Use 'I' for your decisions and actions, and 'we' only for shared outcomes.

Skipping the hard parts. If your story has no conflict, no trade-off, and no failure anywhere, it reads as rehearsed and incomplete. Gusto interviewers specifically probe for what went wrong and what you learned.

Treating reliability as a technical problem only. Gusto's product means reliability failures affect real people's finances. If you talk about an incident purely in terms of uptime and RCA process without mentioning customer impact, you miss the signal they are looking for.

Over-preparing a 'perfect' performance-management answer. Candidates sometimes describe an ideal process without any real texture. Interviewers typically follow up with 'and what happened to that person?' Be ready to answer honestly, including cases that did not end with the engineer turning around.

Not asking substantive questions. Candidates who ask nothing, or only ask about compensation, come across as disengaged. Have a couple of thoughtful questions ready for each interviewer.

Underselling cross-functional work. EM roles at Gusto require strong collaboration with product, design, and operations. Candidates from purely engineering-led cultures sometimes have strong people-management stories but thin cross-functional examples. If this is you, find and prep one deliberately.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Gusto EM interview typically have?

Candidates report a process that typically includes a recruiter phone screen, a hiring-manager conversation, and a panel loop. The loop usually has multiple interviewers covering leadership, technical judgment, and cross-functional collaboration. Round names and exact structure can vary by team and hiring manager, so confirm the format with your recruiter early.

Does Gusto ask coding questions for Engineering Manager roles?

Most candidates report that the EM loop at Gusto focuses on leadership, people management, and technical judgment rather than live coding. You may be asked to discuss system design trade-offs or walk through a past technical decision. Brush up on architecture concepts relevant to distributed payments or payroll systems, but do not expect a whiteboard coding round.

What salary can I expect for an EM role at Gusto?

Gusto is a US company and compensation for US-based roles will differ significantly from Indian market benchmarks. For Indian-market EM-equivalent roles at product companies, knok's data shows bands of 35-60 LPA at Manager level and 55-90 LPA at Senior Manager level. For US-based Gusto roles, check Glassdoor or levels.fyi for publicly reported figures specific to the company.

How important is Gusto product knowledge going into the interview?

Very important, candidates report. Gusto's interviewers frequently anchor questions in the reality of a payroll product, covering reliability, compliance, and the impact of failures on small business owners. Candidates who have clearly thought about what it means to build software that handles people's paychecks tend to stand out versus those who treat it as a generic tech leadership interview.

How long does the Gusto EM hiring process take from first contact to offer?

Candidates report the full process typically takes a few weeks from recruiter screen to offer, though this varies by team and role urgency. It is reasonable to ask your recruiter for an expected timeline after your first conversation. If you have competing offers, communicate that early so the team can work with your constraints.

What is the best way to stand out as an EM candidate at Gusto?

Candidates who do well typically demonstrate three things: genuine empathy for small business customers, honest and specific stories about hard people-management situations, and a track record of enabling strong engineering decisions without making all of them yourself. Specific stories with real texture, including what went wrong and what you learned, consistently land better than polished but vague answers.
