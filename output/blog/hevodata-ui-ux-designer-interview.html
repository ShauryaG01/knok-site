01 Overview

Overview

Hevodata is a no-code data pipeline and integration platform that helps businesses move data between sources and destinations automatically. As of July 2026, Hevodata had 40 open roles, reflecting active hiring across the company. UI/UX Designers here typically work on making complex data workflows feel intuitive, including for users who are not data engineers or analysts.

The interview process candidates report typically involves 3-4 rounds: an initial recruiter or HR screening, a portfolio presentation and design review, one or two product thinking rounds, and a final round with a senior stakeholder or founder. The process places heavy emphasis on your ability to explain design decisions with user evidence and demonstrate empathy for people working with technical, data-heavy interfaces.

Hevodata operates in the B2B data integration space, so interviewers expect you to understand the specific challenges of designing for enterprise and analyst users, not just general consumer audiences. Coming in with at least a surface familiarity with how data pipelines work will help you ask sharper questions and signal genuine interest in the product.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

  1. Walk us through a project where you simplified a complex workflow for a non-technical user.
  2. How do you approach designing data-heavy dashboards that are easy to scan at a glance?
  3. Describe a time you pushed back on a product requirement for design reasons. How did you handle it?
  4. How do you balance visual aesthetics with functional clarity in a SaaS product?
  5. What is your process for user research when timelines are tight?
  6. Tell me about a time you designed with accessibility in mind. What trade-offs did you face?
  7. How do you handle feedback from engineers that a design is 'too complex to build' on the current timeline?
  8. Describe your experience working with a design system or component library.
  9. How would you approach redesigning the onboarding flow for a first-time user of a data integration tool?
  10. What metrics do you track to measure whether a design change was successful?
  11. Tell me about a time you had to align multiple stakeholders with conflicting opinions on a design decision.
  12. How do you stay current with UX trends and apply them practically in a B2B SaaS context?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through a project where you simplified a complex workflow for a non-technical user.

*Situation:* At my previous company, we had an internal analytics tool used daily by marketing managers who had no SQL knowledge.

*Task:* I was asked to redesign the report creation flow, which required users to write raw queries to generate any custom report. Adoption was low and support requests were high.

*Action:* I ran 5 user interviews to map pain points, then designed a drag-and-drop report builder with pre-built templates. I ran two rounds of usability testing with actual marketing team members and iterated on the filter logic after watching where users got stuck.

*Result:* After launch, support tickets related to report creation dropped noticeably and adoption of the custom report feature grew within the first quarter. The redesign became a reference case study the product team used in internal reviews.

---

Q: Describe a time you pushed back on a product requirement for design reasons.

*Situation:* A PM wanted to surface 6 new data filter options on the main dashboard view before a client demo, all visible at once.

*Task:* I needed to communicate clearly that adding all 6 at once would hurt scannability for the exact users we were trying to impress.

*Action:* I built a quick prototype showing the 'before' and 'after' layouts side by side. I also pulled a heatmap from our analytics showing that users rarely engaged with the bottom section of the existing filter panel. I proposed a progressive disclosure approach: 3 primary filters visible by default with a 'More filters' option for the rest.

*Result:* The PM agreed to the approach. The demo went smoothly and the client specifically mentioned how clean and focused the interface felt.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you had to align multiple stakeholders with conflicting opinions on a design.

*Situation:* I was redesigning the onboarding flow for a B2B tool. The sales team wanted prominent upsell prompts early in the flow, while the customer success team felt this would feel pushy to new users and hurt activation.

*Task:* My role was to find an approach that served both the business goal and the user experience without either team feeling overruled.

*Action:* I facilitated a short alignment session where I mapped each team's goal to a specific moment in the user journey. I then designed two variants for an A/B test and walked through the rationale for each, framing them as hypotheses rather than finished decisions.

*Result:* Both teams felt their perspective was represented. We ran the test and the outcome data drove the final decision rather than anyone's personal preference. This process became our team's standard approach to cross-functional design disagreements.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR with a design layer. The STAR framework (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the foundation for behavioural questions. For design interviews, layer in specific method names during your Action step: user interviews, journey mapping, wireframing, usability testing, handoff documentation, design system components. Naming methods signals rigour and helps interviewers understand your process, not just your output.

Discover, Define, Design, Deliver for hypothetical questions. When interviewers ask how you would approach a problem on Hevodata's actual product, use this four-part structure. State what you would research first (competitor tools, existing user complaints, support tickets, product analytics), then how you would define the problem clearly, then how you would approach the design and what trade-offs you would consider, then how you would test and measure success.

Tension, Principle, Decision for conflict questions. When asked about disagreements with PMs, engineers, or stakeholders, name the tension clearly, state the design principle you used to resolve it (for example, 'progressive disclosure over feature parity'), and then describe the decision and what happened. This structure shows maturity and signals that your choices are grounded in principles rather than instinct or preference.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Hevodata is a B2B data product, so interviewers typically look for designers who can hold complexity without hiding it badly. They want to see that you understand both technical users (data engineers, analysts) and less technical ones (business stakeholders, operations teams) and can design thoughtfully for the gap between them.

Specific things to demonstrate. Explain your design decisions using user data or direct observations, not personal taste. Show familiarity with SaaS and dashboard-heavy products. Demonstrate comfort working closely with engineers and an understanding of what is actually buildable. Present a portfolio that shows before-and-after thinking, process artifacts, and iteration, not just polished final screens.

Depth over breadth. Candidates report that Hevodata interviewers ask detailed follow-up questions to test whether portfolio work is genuinely yours and whether you understand the reasoning behind each decision. Prepare to go deep on 2-3 projects rather than giving a surface-level overview of many.

Product sense matters. Beyond craft, interviewers want to see that you think about the business and user goals behind a design. Be ready to talk about what success looks like for a feature and how you would know if your design contributed to it.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Use the product. Before any round, explore Hevodata's product directly or watch detailed demo videos and read their product documentation. Note where the interface makes you think hard and where it feels effortless. These observations become strong talking points in portfolio and product thinking rounds.

Step 2: Prepare your portfolio. Select 2-3 projects that show: working on complex, data-rich interfaces; iteration based on user feedback; and close collaboration with engineers or PMs. Annotate your case studies to highlight your thinking process at each stage, not just the final deliverable.

Step 3: Research the competitive context. Understand the B2B data pipeline space at a basic level. Read recent Hevodata blog posts or product release notes to see which features they are actively building. This lets you speak to real product challenges rather than generic design scenarios.

Step 4: Prepare for a design exercise. Many product design interviews include a live or take-home exercise. Practise solving a brief from scratch, starting with clarifying questions before opening any design tool. Focus on showing your thinking, not just the visual output.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

1. Showing only final screens. Interviewers at product companies want to see your process including rough sketches, failed directions, and how user feedback or engineering constraints changed your approach. A polished portfolio with no visible thinking raises doubts about depth.

2. Connecting aesthetics to looks rather than outcomes. Every visual decision should connect to a user need or a business goal. Saying a design 'looks clean' without explaining why that serves the user will not land well with a product-focused interviewer.

3. Overstating your contribution. If another team member did the user research on a project you are presenting, say so clearly. Interviewers will probe, and being caught stretching the truth damages your credibility for the rest of that round.

4. Treating it like a consumer design interview. Generic examples from social or e-commerce apps without any translation to complex workflows suggest a lack of relevant B2B experience. Anchor your examples to enterprise or productivity software wherever possible.

5. Skipping clarifying questions in design exercises. Before sketching anything for a hypothetical scenario, ask about the user, the goal, the constraints, and what success looks like. Jumping straight to solutions signals shallow problem-solving to experienced interviewers.

6. Not preparing questions for the interviewer. Asking how the design team operates, how design decisions are made, and what the biggest unsolved UX challenge is right now signals seniority and genuine interest in the role.

Frequently asked

How many interview rounds does Hevodata typically have for UI/UX Designer roles?

Candidates report a process of typically 3-4 rounds. This commonly includes an initial HR or recruiter screening, a portfolio presentation round, one or two design and product thinking rounds, and a final round with a senior stakeholder or founder. The exact structure can vary by team and role level, so confirm the format with your recruiter at the start of the process.

Will I get a take-home design assignment or a live design exercise?

Many candidates for product design roles at B2B SaaS companies report receiving a take-home assignment or an in-interview design exercise. Hevodata's process has not been widely documented publicly, so treat this as a real possibility and prepare accordingly. Practise working through a design brief from scratch, focusing on the questions you ask and your reasoning process, not just the visual output.

What kind of portfolio projects are most relevant for Hevodata?

Projects involving dashboards, data visualisation, admin panels, or complex multi-step workflows are the most relevant. Interviewers want to see that you can handle information density without creating visual overload. If your portfolio leans toward consumer apps, prepare a personal case study or worked example showing how you would approach a data-heavy interface problem to fill that gap before your interview.

What salary can I expect for a UI/UX Designer role at Hevodata?

Specific salary data for Hevodata was not available for this report. Based on publicly reported ranges on Glassdoor and industry surveys, mid-level product designers at B2B SaaS companies in Bangalore and Delhi commonly cite figures in the 12-25 LPA range, though this varies significantly by years of experience and role level. Verify the actual band with the recruiter before your final round so you can negotiate from an informed position.

Does location matter for applying to Hevodata as a UI/UX Designer?

As of July 2026, knok's job radar showed Delhi with 8 UI/UX Designer openings and Bangalore with 6 across the broader Indian market, indicating strong demand in both cities. For Hevodata specifically, check whether the role is listed as remote, hybrid, or fully in-office, since their hiring location preferences may differ from the overall market picture.

How should I approach the portfolio presentation round?

Choose 2-3 projects and go deep rather than covering many projects briefly. For each project, walk through the problem, the users involved, your process from research to final design, the constraints you navigated, and how you measured success. Interviewers will ask detailed follow-up questions to test how well you understand your own work, so be ready to explain the reasoning behind every significant decision you made.
