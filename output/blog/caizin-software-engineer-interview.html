02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions reflect common themes candidates report from Software Engineer interviews at product-focused companies like Caizin. Prepare concrete examples for each.

Coding and Technical Depth

  1. Walk me through a system you built end to end. What were the key design decisions you made and why?
  2. How would you design a scalable REST API for a feature expected to handle sudden traffic spikes?
  3. What is your experience with microservices architecture, and when would you choose a monolith instead?
  4. Which databases have you worked with? How did you decide which one to use for a specific project?
  5. How do you ensure the code you write is testable, readable, and maintainable by the next engineer who touches it?

Debugging and Problem Solving

  1. Describe a critical production issue you debugged. How did you isolate the root cause and restore service?
  2. How do you approach performance optimisation when a service or feature is running slower than expected?
  3. Tell me about a time you had to refactor a messy codebase. How did you plan it without breaking existing behaviour?

Behavioural and Culture

  1. Tell me about a time you disagreed with a technical decision made by your team. What did you do?
  2. Describe a situation where you had to learn a new technology quickly to meet a project deadline.
  3. How do you handle cross-team dependencies that are blocking your work from moving forward?
  4. What is the most impactful technical contribution you have made to a product or platform, and how did you measure that impact?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a critical production issue you debugged under pressure.

*Situation:* Our payments microservice started throwing server errors for a significant portion of transactions on a Friday evening, directly affecting revenue and user trust.

*Task:* I was the on-call engineer. I needed to identify the root cause and restore service as quickly as possible.

*Action:* I pulled logs from our observability stack and noticed the spike in errors started immediately after a config change deployed that afternoon. I traced it to a database connection pool setting that was too low for evening peak load. I rolled back the config, confirmed error rates dropped to normal, then filed a post-mortem with a permanent fix: a higher pool ceiling and an alert that fires before the limit is reached.

*Result:* Service was restored within the same hour. The permanent fix eliminated similar incidents over the following quarter, and the post-mortem became a reference document the team used for future incident reviews.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you had to learn a new technology quickly.

*Situation:* My team was asked to migrate a legacy batch data pipeline to Apache Kafka. No one on the team had shipped a production Kafka system before.

*Task:* I volunteered to lead the technical spike and deliver a working prototype within two weeks so the team could make a confident decision.

*Action:* I spent the first three days going through official documentation and building a toy producer-consumer setup locally. I then mapped our existing pipeline's data flow onto Kafka topics and partitions, ran load tests at expected production volume, and documented every pitfall I hit, from offset management to consumer group rebalancing.

*Result:* The prototype was ready in 11 days. My documentation cut the team's onboarding time significantly, and we delivered the full migration on schedule with no data loss.

---

Q: Describe a time you disagreed with a technical decision.

*Situation:* My tech lead wanted to initialise a third-party analytics SDK on the main thread of our mobile app. I believed this would noticeably hurt app startup time for users.

*Task:* I needed to raise the concern constructively without creating conflict or derailing the release timeline.

*Action:* I ran a quick benchmark on a test build and measured a meaningful increase in cold start time. I then proposed an alternative: initialise the SDK lazily after the first user interaction. I brought both options with benchmark data to our next standup, framing it as 'here are two paths, here is the tradeoff' rather than 'your idea is wrong.'

*Result:* The team adopted the lazy initialisation approach. Startup time stayed within our target, we shipped on time, and my tech lead cited the episode in my next review as an example of constructive technical pushback.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Three frameworks cover most questions in a Caizin Software Engineer interview.

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the standard for behavioural questions. Keep the Situation to 1-2 sentences. Spend most of your time on Action, using 'I' not 'we' so the interviewer knows exactly what you did. Always close with a concrete Result. If you have no number, describe the qualitative outcome clearly: what changed, what improved, what you learned.

Problem-Solution-Tradeoff works well for system design rounds. State the problem and its constraints first, then propose a solution, then proactively name the tradeoffs you considered: cost, latency, complexity, scalability. Interviewers at product companies like Caizin respond well to candidates who think in tradeoffs rather than presenting one 'perfect' answer. Showing that you know what you gave up signals senior-level thinking.

Situation-Complication-Resolution is useful for questions about conflict, disagreement, or a plan that went wrong. Describe the original plan (Situation), what made it complicated (Complication), and how you resolved it (Resolution) without making others look bad. Keep the tone collaborative. Interviewers are not looking for drama, they are looking for judgment.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates report from product-focused engineering teams of similar size and stage to Caizin, interviewers are typically looking for four things.

Ownership mindset. Engineers at smaller companies handle ambiguity and wear many hats. Interviewers want to hear that you have shipped things end to end, not just picked up tickets and handed them off. Use examples where you identified a problem, drove the solution, and saw it through to production.

Clear technical communication. In coding and design rounds, you are expected to explain your thinking out loud as you work. Interviewers find it hard to evaluate candidates whose reasoning they cannot follow. Practise narrating your approach before your interview, even if it feels awkward at first.

Pragmatic engineering judgment. A common mistake is proposing an over-engineered solution. Caizin, like most growing product teams, values engineers who balance the ideal with the real: time constraints, team size, existing infrastructure. Show that you know when 'good enough and shipped' beats 'perfect and delayed.'

Receptiveness to pushback. Candidates report that interviewers at companies like Caizin often challenge your approach mid-answer to see how you respond. Stay calm, acknowledge the point, and engage with it. Digging in stubbornly on a flawed answer is a common red flag. Thoughtful reconsideration is a green flag.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Spread your preparation over 3 to 4 weeks for the best results.

Week 1: Strengthen your fundamentals. Revise data structures and algorithms with a focus on arrays, strings, trees, graphs, and dynamic programming. Solve 2-3 problems daily on a timed coding platform. Also revisit core computer science concepts: how HTTP and TCP work, database indexing, basic concurrency, and caching.

Week 2: System design. Study common patterns such as load balancing, caching layers, message queues, and database sharding. Pick 2-3 systems you use daily and practise designing them from scratch on paper or a whiteboard. Prioritise explaining your tradeoffs out loud, not just drawing diagrams.

Week 3: Behavioural prep. Write 6-8 STAR stories from your own work history. Cover: a technical challenge, a conflict, a time you failed, a time you learned fast, a time you led without a title, and your proudest delivery. Say them out loud so they feel natural, not recited.

Week 4: Mock interviews and final checks. Run at least 2 full mock interviews with a peer or on a practice platform. Review weak spots from the mocks. Research Caizin's product and any public engineering content they have shared. Confirm the interview format with your recruiter before the first round.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping straight to technical detail without context. Candidates who skip the setup are hard to follow. Always give 1-2 sentences of context before diving into the technical detail, even in a coding round.

Saying 'we' when interviewers need to hear 'I'. In team project stories, be explicit about your individual contribution. 'We built the pipeline' tells an interviewer nothing about what you specifically did. Own your part clearly.

Skipping clarifying questions in design rounds. Starting a system design without asking about scale, constraints, and expected usage is a common mistake. Asking clarifying questions shows senior-level thinking and prevents you from designing the wrong thing.

Presenting one answer as obviously correct. Naming a tradeoff you considered, even if you rejected it, shows engineering maturity. 'I chose SQL here because consistency mattered more than scale at this stage' is far stronger than just 'I chose SQL.'

Walking in without a salary number. Know the market range before your HR call. Entry-level Software Engineer roles in India typically fall in the 6-12 LPA range, mid-level in 15-25 LPA, and senior in 28-45 LPA, based on knok jobradar data. Have a number ready so you are not anchored by the first offer.

Not following up after rounds. A short, genuine note to your recruiter after completing a round is rare in Indian hiring but candidates report it is noticed positively. Keep it brief and specific to the conversation.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many interview rounds does Caizin typically have for a Software Engineer role?

Candidates report typically 3 to 4 rounds, though this can vary with the seniority of the role. The process commonly includes an initial HR or recruiter screening, one or two technical rounds covering coding and system design, and a final round focused on behavioural or culture fit. Confirm the exact format with your recruiter after your first call so you know what to expect.

Does Caizin ask data structures and algorithms questions?

Candidates report that DSA questions do appear, particularly in early technical rounds. Common topics include arrays, strings, trees, and graphs, typically at a medium difficulty level. Strong fundamentals combined with clear verbal reasoning as you solve will serve you better than memorised solutions alone.

What salary can I expect for a Software Engineer role at Caizin?

Caizin's specific salary bands are not publicly reported in detail. Based on knok jobradar market data for Software Engineer roles across India, entry-level (0-2 years) typically falls in the 6-12 LPA range, mid-level (3-5 years) in 15-25 LPA, and senior (6-9 years) in 28-45 LPA. Your actual offer depends on the specific role level, your experience, and how you negotiate.

Should I prepare system design even for a junior Software Engineer role at Caizin?

For entry-level roles, a dedicated system design round is less common, but basic questions like how you would structure a simple API or choose between SQL and NoSQL do come up, per candidate reports. For mid-level and above, system design is typically a full round. Preparing the basics regardless of your seniority level is a safe call.

Is there an online coding test before the live interview rounds?

Many product companies use an online coding assessment as an initial screening step before live rounds, and candidates report this is common for Software Engineer roles at companies of this type. Prepare for 1-2 algorithmic problems within a timed window, and practise on a timed platform beforehand so the format does not catch you off guard.

How long does it take to hear back after a Caizin interview?

Feedback timelines vary, but candidates report hearing back within 5 to 10 business days after the final round. If you have not received a response after 7 business days, a short and polite follow-up to your recruiter is appropriate and generally well received. Mention the role title and the date of your last round to make it easy for them to respond quickly.
