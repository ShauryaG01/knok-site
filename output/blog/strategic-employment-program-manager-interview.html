01 Overview

Overview

Strategic Employment is one of the most active Technical Program Manager recruiters in India right now, with 107 open TPM positions on knok jobradar as of July 2026. Across the wider market, there are 313 TPM roles live, with Bangalore leading at 41 positions, Delhi at 14, Pune at 13, Hyderabad at 12, Chennai at 5, and Mumbai at 1.

Strategic Employment is a staffing and workforce solutions firm, which means its TPMs often operate at the intersection of client organisations and engineering delivery teams. The role typically involves owning complex, cross-functional programs end to end: building the plan, managing dependencies across teams, surfacing risks before they become crises, and keeping senior stakeholders informed with clarity and confidence.

Candidates report that the interview process typically includes a recruiter screening call, a round or more of technical and behavioural assessment, and a final conversation with a hiring manager or senior leader. The process is focused heavily on real examples from your past. Interviewers want to see that you can own a program, not just coordinate one.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly in TPM interviews at Strategic Employment, based on what candidates report:

  1. Walk me through a large, cross-functional program you owned from kickoff to delivery.
  2. How do you prioritise when multiple engineering teams are pulling you in opposite directions?
  3. Describe a time you spotted a critical risk before it became a crisis. What did you do?
  4. Tell me about a program that slipped its deadline. How did you handle it?
  5. How do you get realistic estimates from engineers who are already stretched thin?
  6. Describe a situation where you had to influence a team you had no authority over.
  7. How do you decide when to escalate an issue versus resolving it yourself?
  8. Walk me through how you build and maintain a program roadmap when requirements keep changing.
  9. How do you track cross-team dependencies and make sure blockers surface early?
  10. Tell me about a time stakeholders had conflicting requirements. How did you align them?
  11. How do you measure whether a program has actually succeeded after it goes live?
  12. How does the TPM role interface with client-facing or account teams here?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a program that slipped its deadline. How did you handle it?

*Situation:* I was running a platform migration program involving multiple engineering teams and several external vendors. Midway through, a vendor delayed a critical API integration by several weeks.

*Task:* My job was to protect the overall launch date as much as possible and keep the business sponsor informed without creating panic.

*Action:* I immediately mapped the downstream impact on every dependent workstream. I found tracks that could proceed in parallel while the vendor issue was resolved, so I resequenced the plan to keep those teams productive. I also restructured the weekly status call to focus on blockers only, cutting meeting time so engineers could stay heads-down. I gave the sponsor a concise summary each Friday: what slipped, what we were doing about it, and the revised go-live window.

*Result:* We launched later than the original date, but the parallel resequencing saved us from a much larger delay. The sponsor said the communication made the slip manageable because there were no surprises.

---

Q: How do you influence a team you have no authority over?

*Situation:* An infrastructure team owned a dependency that was blocking my program, but my program was not their top priority that quarter.

*Task:* I needed their bandwidth without being able to assign it, and arguing urgency alone was not working.

*Action:* I set up a meeting with their engineering lead to understand their constraints first. Once I understood their backlog, I reframed my ask: I offered to handle all the coordination overhead and documentation for their side of the work, so their engineers only had to show up for the technical decisions. I also connected their lead with my program sponsor, so they could see the business visibility this work carried.

*Result:* The team allocated a part-time engineer to my program within the week. We cleared the blocker on time, and the infrastructure lead later cited this as a model for cross-team collaboration.

---

Q: Walk me through how you track cross-team dependencies.

*Situation:* I joined a program that had multiple teams contributing, but nobody had a single view of what depended on what. Teams were discovering blockers in stand-ups the day they hit.

*Task:* I needed to build a dependency-tracking practice fast, without adding heavy process overhead that teams would abandon.

*Action:* I introduced a shared dependency log in Confluence, with a handful of fields per item: owner, consumer, due date, and status. I reviewed it with all engineering leads in a weekly sync. If something turned amber, I flagged it to both owners the same day so they could talk directly rather than routing through me. I also added a 'risk horizon' view that showed everything due in the next couple of sprints, so teams had early visibility.

*Result:* Blocker discovery moved from same-day surprises to several sprints of lead time. The program delivered its first major milestone on schedule.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use STAR for every behavioural question. Strategic Employment interviewers are trained to probe for specifics. If your answer stays general, they will keep asking 'can you give me a specific example?' until you do. Build your answers around Situation, Task, Action, and Result before you walk in.

For scope and complexity questions, lead with the program's scale, not the technology. Describe how many teams were involved, what the business stakes were, and what made coordination hard. Interviewers for a staffing-focused firm like Strategic Employment are often assessing whether you can operate at enterprise scale, so give them the context to judge that.

For stakeholder questions, show the 'why' behind your communication choices. Do not just say 'I kept stakeholders informed.' Explain what you chose to communicate, at what frequency, and why that cadence matched the stakeholder's needs. This signals that you think systematically about communication rather than just doing it instinctively.

For risk and escalation questions, name your threshold. A strong answer states the criteria you use to decide when to escalate versus handle an issue yourself. For example: 'I escalate when an issue crosses a scope or timeline threshold I cannot resolve within my own authority, or when it affects a commitment made at the leadership level.' This shows judgment, not just activity.

For 'how do you get estimates' questions, show empathy and structure. The best answers acknowledge that engineers dislike being asked for estimates under pressure, then describe a specific process: breaking work into smaller pieces, using past velocity as a reference, and building in buffer explicitly rather than hiding it.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Program ownership, not coordination. Strategic Employment is looking for TPMs who own outcomes, not just meeting notes. Every answer should show that you were accountable for the result, not just a facilitator who connected the dots.

Structured thinking under ambiguity. TPM roles at this level routinely involve unclear requirements and shifting priorities. Interviewers will introduce ambiguity deliberately. Show them you have a repeatable process for breaking a fuzzy problem into concrete next steps.

Stakeholder credibility. The TPM role at Strategic Employment often involves senior business stakeholders and client contacts. Interviewers want evidence that you can communicate with a VP-level sponsor clearly and confidently, without over-explaining technical detail or under-explaining risk.

Metrics and outcomes. Vague answers like 'the program went well' will not land. Tie your results to something measurable: delivery timelines, risk items closed, team velocity, or business milestones hit. If exact numbers are sensitive, describe the direction and scale of the impact.

Self-awareness about failure. At least one question will probe a program that did not go as planned. Candidates who acknowledge what went wrong and what they learned consistently score higher than those who minimise the failure or blame external factors.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Start with your own story bank. Before anything else, write down several programs from your career that demonstrate different skills: a large delivery, a stakeholder conflict, a risk you caught early, a program that slipped, a time you influenced without authority, and a process you built from scratch. These become the raw material for almost every question.

Research Strategic Employment's business model. They are a staffing and workforce solutions firm, which means their clients are enterprises running large transformation programs. Think about how your TPM experience connects to that context: have you worked on programs where the delivery team and the business team were in different organisations? That experience will resonate.

Practice speaking in STAR format out loud. Reading a STAR answer and saying it confidently are very different. Record yourself answering a few questions. Listen for vagueness, filler words, and answers that run too long. Trim until the core story is clear and tight.

Prepare smart questions for each round. Ask about the programs you would own, how the TPM team is structured, and what success looks like in the first few months. Avoid questions whose answers are on the company website. Good options: 'What does the handoff from sales to delivery look like for a new program?' or 'How does the TPM role interface with the client-facing account team here?'

Do a dependency-mapping exercise on paper. Many candidates stumble on the live problem-solving portion of TPM interviews. Practice drawing a dependency map for a fictional program, identifying critical path items, and naming the risks. This warms up the muscle before you need it on the call.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Staying too high-level. The single most common mistake is giving an answer that sounds like a job description instead of a real story. Interviewers at Strategic Employment probe until they hit a concrete example, so save everyone time by leading with specifics.

Confusing tasks with outcomes. Many candidates describe what they did without saying what happened as a result. Every STAR answer needs a result, and that result should be tied to something the business or the team actually cared about.

Underestimating the stakeholder management focus. Because Strategic Employment sits between clients and engineering delivery, TPM interviews here lean heavily on how you communicate upward and across organisational boundaries. If your answers are all about delivery mechanics and none are about stakeholder communication, rebalance.

Skipping the 'why' on process choices. Saying 'I used a RACI matrix' is not an answer. Saying 'I introduced a RACI because multiple teams kept doing duplicate work and nobody thought it was their responsibility' shows judgment. Always explain why you chose the approach you did.

Treating salary negotiation as a surprise topic. Candidates report that compensation conversations can come up early in the process at staffing firms. Know your number before the first call. Publicly reported ranges for TPM roles in India vary significantly by city and company size, so check Glassdoor and levels.fyi for current data rather than relying on any single figure.

What is the typical interview process at Strategic Employment for a TPM role?

Candidates typically report a recruiter screening call, followed by a round or more of technical and behavioural assessment, and a final conversation with a hiring manager or senior leader. Round names and exact order can vary by team, so confirm the structure with your recruiter after you apply.
