01 Overview

Overview

VAST Data is a data infrastructure company that builds storage and data management platforms for AI workloads, research institutions, and large enterprises. With 247 open roles currently listed, they are actively scaling across product, engineering, and design. The Product Designer role at VAST Data sits at the intersection of complex technical systems and user experience, so expect interview questions that probe how well you simplify dense, data-heavy interfaces for sophisticated enterprise users.

Candidates typically report a process that includes a recruiter screen, a portfolio review with the design team, one or two design or case study rounds, and a cross-functional panel that may involve engineers, a product manager, and a design leader. 02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

  1. Walk us through a project where you designed or redesigned a complex, data-heavy product. What was your end-to-end process?
  2. How do you approach UX design for technical users such as storage administrators, data engineers, or IT architects?
  3. Tell us about a time you pushed back on a PM or stakeholder request. How did you make your case?
  4. How do you balance exposing deep functionality for power users while keeping the interface approachable for less technical users?
  5. Describe how you collaborated with engineers during the build phase to preserve design intent without slowing down delivery.
  6. VAST Data's products deal with enterprise-scale data infrastructure. How do you design for complexity and scale?
  7. How do you conduct user research when your target users are hard to access, such as enterprise IT buyers or data architects?
  8. Walk us through your experience contributing to or scaling a design system or shared component library.
  9. Tell us about a time you used analytics, usage data, or usability testing to challenge or validate a design decision.
  10. How do you handle starting a new product area with no existing patterns, no prior research, and a tight deadline?
  11. Describe a design you shipped that did not perform as expected. What did you do next?
  12. How do you communicate design rationale to engineers and product managers who have different priorities from yours?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through a project where you designed a data-heavy product.

*Situation:* At my previous company, the data ops team used a monitoring dashboard that displayed metrics in a flat, undifferentiated table. Users were overwhelmed and frequently raised support tickets because they could not tell which alerts needed immediate action.

*Task:* I was asked to redesign the dashboard to reduce alert fatigue and help users surface critical information faster.

*Action:* I started with user interviews with five data ops engineers to understand how they actually triaged incidents day to day. I mapped where attention was being lost and identified the core problem: all alerts looked equally urgent. I designed a tiered information hierarchy that visually separated critical alerts from advisory notices and introduced a 'focus view' that collapsed secondary metrics by default. I ran two rounds of usability testing with the same engineers and refined the grouping logic before handoff.

*Result:* After launch, the ops team reported faster incident triage in their quarterly feedback and support tickets related to missed alerts dropped according to our internal tracking. The redesigned dashboard became the template for two other product areas.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you pushed back on a stakeholder request.

*Situation:* A senior product manager wanted to add a bulk export feature as a prominent call to action on the main screen of an enterprise data tool I was working on. Analytics showed it was used by a small subset of users in any given week.

*Task:* My job was to advocate for the majority of users while keeping the PM's valid business need in scope.

*Action:* I pulled usage data from our analytics dashboard and ran a short survey with five power users to understand when they actually needed the bulk export. I then presented two alternatives: a contextual action that appeared only when multiple items were selected, and a secondary actions menu for the same feature. I framed both options around cognitive load for new users rather than making it a disagreement about priorities.

*Result:* The PM agreed to the contextual approach after reviewing the data. The feature shipped in the next sprint and onboarding completion for new users improved in the following quarter according to our internal product metrics.

---

Q: How do you handle a new product area with no existing patterns and a tight deadline?

*Situation:* I was assigned to design an AI-powered data tagging feature with a two-week design window, no existing user research, and no comparable internal product to reference.

*Task:* I needed to deliver a prototype for an engineering kickoff while ensuring it was grounded in real user needs and not just assumptions.

*Action:* I spent the first two days on rapid competitive analysis of analogous products, then ran three short generative interviews with internal users who matched the target persona. I sketched on paper first, got alignment from the PM and tech lead on the core flow using rough wireframes, and moved to high-fidelity only after key decisions were locked. I documented all open assumptions explicitly in the design file so the team could revisit them after launch.

*Result:* The prototype was ready on time. Two of my core assumptions were validated in follow-up user testing and one was corrected, leading to a meaningful change in the labeling flow before engineering finished the build.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For design case study questions: Structure your answer around four elements: context (who the user was and what they needed), constraint (the challenge or limitation you were working within), process (the specific methods you used), and outcome (a measurable or observable result). Avoid spending too long on aesthetics. VAST Data interviewers will probe your thinking, not just your visuals.

For 'how do you handle X' questions: Lead with your principle, then back it with a concrete example. If asked how you handle engineering constraints, first state your general approach ('I try to understand the constraint at the code level, not just accept it as a blocker'), then support it with a specific situation. This shows strategic thinking and practical experience together.

For pushback or conflict questions: Use a 'data, options, align' structure. Show that you used evidence (research, analytics, or usability testing) rather than opinion, offered more than one solution so the stakeholder had a genuine choice, and reached alignment rather than 'winning' the argument. Enterprise design culture at technical companies values composure and evidence over persuasion.

For ambiguity and open-ended design prompts: Show that you narrow scope before you start designing. Ask clarifying questions, name your constraints early, and state your assumptions out loud. Jumping to solutions without framing the problem first is one of the most common ways candidates lose points in live design rounds at companies like VAST Data.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

VAST Data is a technical company selling into data-intensive enterprises. Interviewers there typically look for a specific combination of skills and habits. Here is what candidates report matters most.

Comfort with complexity. Show that you have worked on products with many states, edge cases, and power-user needs. Generic consumer app examples may not land as well as B2B, data tool, or enterprise examples.

Systems thinking. Can you zoom out and place your screen within a larger user journey or product ecosystem? Candidates who only talk about individual screens without connecting them to the broader system tend to struggle in design panels.

Engineering empathy. VAST Data has a strong engineering culture. Interviewers notice when candidates demonstrate that they understand technical constraints, have worked closely with engineers, and have shipped in environments where design and engineering move together.

Evidence-based decisions. Every design choice should trace back to a user need or a data point. Relying on opinion alone is a red flag in their evaluation process.

Clear communication across functions. You will present to engineers, product managers, and business stakeholders. Candidates who explain design trade-offs in plain, jargon-free terms consistently receive positive signals in cross-functional panels.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Research and portfolio prep. Read VAST Data's publicly available product pages, blog posts, and any customer case studies they have published. Note the language they use: terms like 'data fabric,' 'disaggregated storage,' and 'AI infrastructure' signal the product context your designs will live in. Select two or three portfolio projects that best demonstrate data-heavy, enterprise, or complex-system design. Prepare to walk through each using the context-constraint-process-outcome structure.

Week 2: Practice and mock interviews. Run at least two full portfolio walkthroughs out loud, ideally with a peer who can ask follow-up questions. Prepare STAR-format answers to the 12 questions listed above. Record yourself at least once to catch filler words and pacing. Pay extra attention to your 'pushback' and 'ambiguity' stories because those come up often in VAST Data panels and need to feel natural, not rehearsed.

Week 3 and beyond: Design exercise prep. Candidates often report a take-home or live design exercise. Practice whiteboard-style design problems focused on enterprise data tools: dashboards, admin panels, complex settings flows, and permission systems. Time yourself. Practice narrating your reasoning as you work, since live design rounds typically assess your decision-making process, not just the final artifact.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Showing only consumer product work. If your portfolio is all mobile apps and social products, interviewers may find it hard to picture you handling enterprise complexity. Reframe your case studies to highlight decision-making, constraints, and research process rather than visual polish alone.

Skipping the 'why' in portfolio walkthroughs. Saying 'I made it cleaner and more intuitive' without explaining which user problem that solved or what evidence supported the decision is a fast path to rejection. Always anchor your design choices to a user need or a data point.

Diving into solutions without clarifying the problem. In live design exercises, candidates who jump straight to wireframes without asking questions first signal poor cross-functional habits. VAST Data interviewers specifically look for how you frame a problem before you solve it.

Not knowing VAST Data's product space. Walking in without any understanding of data infrastructure or enterprise storage UX is a missed opportunity. Spend at least an hour on their product pages and public customer stories before your first call.

Over-explaining visuals instead of decisions. In portfolio reviews, time is limited. Spend it on trade-offs and design decisions, not describing what is visible on the screen. Interviewers can see the design. They want to understand the thinking behind it.

Softening failure stories into non-failures. Many candidates try to present 'what went wrong' stories as near-successes. Senior design panels specifically probe for genuine reflection. A clear account of what you got wrong and what you changed as a result is far more credible than a story where everything worked out perfectly.
