03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: A key engagement metric is dropping week over week. How do you diagnose the root cause?

*Situation:* At a previous role, we noticed that a core engagement metric for a mobile title had been declining steadily over several weeks after what had seemed like a successful content update.

*Task:* My job was to find the root cause quickly and recommend a fix before the next sprint planning cycle.

*Action:* I started by segmenting the data: new players versus returning players, paying versus non-paying, and by device type. The drop was concentrated in returning players on Android. I then looked at the release notes for recent app store updates and found a session-loading bug introduced in the last patch on certain Android versions. I flagged this to engineering with a detailed breakdown of affected player cohorts and proposed an emergency hotfix alongside a short-term re-engagement push notification for affected users.

*Result:* Engineering shipped the hotfix within a few days, and the metric recovered. The exercise also prompted the team to add platform-segmented monitoring so similar issues could be caught earlier.

---

Q: How would you design a monetisation strategy for a new free-to-play mobile game?

*Situation:* During a product exercise at a previous company, I was asked to design the monetisation layer for a casual puzzle game targeting the Indian market.

*Task:* The goal was to generate sustainable revenue without alienating the large non-paying player base that drives virality.

*Action:* I proposed a three-layer approach. First, a cosmetics-only store so core gameplay stayed fair for all players. Second, an energy system with a generous free replenishment rate, giving paying players the option to skip wait times. Third, a seasonal battle pass priced in a range that industry surveys suggest sits within the impulse-purchase comfort zone for Indian mobile gamers. I scoped each layer with rough effort estimates and ranked them by expected revenue contribution versus implementation cost.

*Result:* The approach was well received in the exercise debrief. The interviewers appreciated that I had anchored the strategy in player psychology and local market context rather than copying a Western model directly.

---

Q: Describe a time you used data to reverse or change a product decision.

*Situation:* The team had decided to surface a new social feature prominently on the home screen of a game, based on qualitative feedback from a small group of power users.

*Task:* I was tasked with tracking the feature's post-launch performance and reporting back after the first few weeks.

*Action:* I set up a funnel from home screen impression to feature adoption, then ran a cohort comparison between players who engaged with the social feature and those who did not. The data showed that players who skipped the feature were churning at a meaningfully lower rate than the team expected, suggesting the feature was not the retention driver we had hoped for. I presented the findings to the team with a recommendation to move the feature to a secondary tab and use the prime home screen real estate for a personalised content feed instead.

*Result:* The team agreed to a phased test. The personalised feed variant performed better on the key retention metric, and the social feature was repositioned as a secondary discovery tool rather than a core hook.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Product thinking questions respond well to a simple structure: define the player, name the problem, propose solutions, pick one and explain the trade-offs. Start by asking who is playing, at what stage of the game lifecycle, and what friction or delight they are experiencing.

Metrics and root-cause questions work best with a top-down funnel approach. Start at the broadest metric, segment by player type or platform, then drill down until you isolate the anomaly. Explicitly state what you would rule out first and why.

Prioritisation questions are well served by an impact-versus-effort grid combined with a clear statement of the current business goal. At a gaming company, the business goal almost always maps to one of: acquisition, retention, or monetisation. Name which one is the current priority before scoring features.

Behavioural questions follow the STAR format: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep the Situation and Task brief and spend most of your time on Action and Result. Quantify the Result wherever you can, and if exact numbers are not available, describe the direction and significance of the outcome clearly.

Monetisation design questions benefit from anchoring early in player psychology and the specific market context. For India, this means factoring in price sensitivity, UPI-first payment behaviour, and the dominance of casual and mid-core genres.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

A genuine understanding of gaming as a product. Interviewers at gaming companies quickly spot candidates who have only played one or two titles casually. Come prepared to talk about games you play regularly, what retention loops they use, and what you would change.

Data-first thinking without losing the player. Gaming PMs are expected to balance analytics with empathy. Interviewers want to see that you can read a funnel, segment a cohort, and still explain decisions in terms of what the player is feeling, not just what the numbers show.

Comfort with engagement and retention metrics. Free-to-play games live and die by metrics like daily active users, session length, and early-day retention rates. Be fluent in these terms and ready to explain how a change in one metric ripples into others. Avoid citing specific benchmark figures unless you attribute them to a public source such as industry surveys or publicly reported data from known studios.

Cross-functional collaboration skills. Game development involves close coordination with designers, artists, and engineers who each have strong opinions. Interviewers want evidence that you can align a team without overriding creative judgment.

Ownership and speed. Gaming moves fast. Candidates who show they can make a decision with incomplete information and iterate quickly tend to stand out.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Get fluent in gaming product basics. Play two or three mobile titles in the genre ewargames focuses on. Map out their onboarding flow, monetisation mechanics, and retention hooks. Write a brief critique of each as if you were the PM. This exercise alone will give you concrete material for almost every question.

Week 2: Sharpen your analytics skills. Practise breaking down a metric drop using a funnel approach. Take any public case study from a gaming company and walk through how you would diagnose a retention problem. Publications like Deconstructor of Fun offer detailed free-to-play analyses you can use as raw material.

Week 3: Prepare your STAR stories. Identify three to five experiences from your career that demonstrate data-driven decisions, cross-functional collaboration, and product ownership. Write them out in STAR format and practise saying them aloud until they feel natural, not rehearsed.

Week 4: Research ewargames specifically. Look up their titles, read recent app store reviews, and check their social media for any announcements about new features or upcoming launches. Knowing the specifics of their games will let you ask sharp questions and show genuine interest.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating gaming like any other product. Candidates who apply a generic PM framework without gaming-specific context (retention loops, live ops, free-to-play economics) come across as unprepared. Reference the mechanics of actual games in your answers.

Using exact numbers without a source. If you claim a feature improved retention by a specific percentage, an interviewer may ask how you know. Either cite the source clearly or describe the outcome in directional terms: 'retention improved meaningfully' rather than inventing a figure.

Ignoring the Indian player context. ewargames is an Indian company. Answers that ignore local payment behaviour, device constraints, or genre preferences for Indian audiences miss an easy opportunity to show market understanding.

Skipping the 'so what' in data questions. Describing what the data showed is only half the answer. Interviewers want to hear what you decided to do because of it and what happened next.

Talking only about features, never about trade-offs. Gaming has real constraints: engineering capacity, live-ops schedules, monetisation targets. Show that you understand what you are giving up when you choose one direction over another.

Not asking questions. At the end of most rounds, candidates who ask thoughtful questions about the team's biggest current challenge or the metrics they care most about leave a stronger impression than those who have no questions at all.

A dedicated gaming background is not always required, but genuine familiarity with mobile games is important. Interviewers typically want to see that you understand free-to-play mechanics, retention loops, and player psychology. If you are coming from a non-gaming background, spending a few weeks playing and analysing titles in their genre will give you credible material for interviews.

What kind of case study should I expect?

Candidates report product exercises that ask you to design a feature, diagnose a metric drop, or build a monetisation strategy for a hypothetical or real game. The exercise is typically sent in advance or completed during the interview. Practise structuring your thinking out loud: start with the player, name the problem, propose solutions, and make a clear recommendation with trade-offs.

How is a gaming PM role different from a standard tech PM role?

Gaming PMs work closely with live-ops cycles, seasonal content drops, and real-time player behaviour data. Monetisation is more central to the role than in many SaaS products, and the line between product and design is often thinner because game feel matters as much as feature functionality. If you are moving from a SaaS or e-commerce background, be ready to explain how your skills transfer to a player-engagement context.
