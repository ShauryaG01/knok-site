02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions appear frequently in publicly reported Asana DevOps interview experiences. Prepare a concrete story or answer for each one.

  1. Asana runs on AWS. Walk us through how you would design a highly available deployment for a critical microservice.
  2. How have you managed Kubernetes cluster operations at scale, including version upgrades, node pool changes, and handling unexpected failures?
  3. Describe a CI/CD pipeline you built or significantly improved. What were the key design decisions and trade-offs?
  4. How do you approach infrastructure as code? Walk us through your Terraform or Pulumi workflow, including state management and how your team collaborates on it.
  5. What does a good on-call setup look like to you? How have you structured alerting to reduce noise while still catching real issues quickly?
  6. Tell us about a production incident you personally led. How did you detect it, coordinate the response, and prevent recurrence?
  7. How do you manage secrets and credentials securely in a cloud-native environment? Which tools or patterns have you relied on?
  8. How do you think about cost optimisation in AWS without trading off reliability?
  9. Describe your zero-downtime deployment strategy for a service that cannot tolerate interruptions.
  10. What is your experience with container image scanning and supply chain security?
  11. How do you partner with software engineers to embed reliability and security practices earlier in the development cycle?
  12. Tell us about an operational task you automated end-to-end. What was the measurable impact on the team?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for every behavioral and scenario question. Here are three model answers you can adapt to your own experience.

---

Q: Tell us about a production incident you personally led.

*Situation:* Our primary API service began returning errors late at night, affecting a significant portion of paying users. On-call pages fired across the team simultaneously.

*Task:* As the on-call engineer, I owned the incident bridge, had to diagnose the root cause quickly, and keep stakeholders informed while coordinating recovery.

*Action:* I pulled dashboards in Datadog and spotted a memory spike on containers from a deployment earlier that evening. I rolled back the release using our Helm rollback workflow, confirmed error rates dropping in Grafana, then declared recovery. The next morning I led the post-mortem and added a memory saturation alert designed to fire before customers felt any impact.

*Result:* Service was restored well within the team's incident SLA. The new alert has since caught two similar issues before they reached production, which the team noted in the next reliability review.

---

Q: Describe a CI/CD pipeline you built or significantly improved.

*Situation:* Our team was deploying services manually by running scripts on servers. Deployments were slow, error-prone, and occasionally failed silently with no automatic rollback.

*Task:* I was asked to own the design and delivery of a fully automated pipeline that would let engineers deploy on every pull request merge.

*Action:* I built the pipeline in GitHub Actions to run unit tests, build a Docker image, push it to ECR with a commit-tagged version, and deploy to EKS via Helm with automated smoke tests and rollback on failure. I added Slack notifications at key stages so developers had visibility without watching logs directly.

*Result:* Deployment time dropped from a slow manual process to a fully automated flow that completed in a fraction of the time. Rollback on failure became automatic, which eliminated the need for manual intervention during off-hours incidents.

---

Q: How have you managed Kubernetes cluster operations at scale?

*Situation:* I was responsible for a set of EKS clusters running across multiple environments, each hosting dozens of microservices with different resource profiles.

*Task:* The clusters had grown organically and were over-provisioned, costing more than necessary, while still occasionally running out of capacity during traffic spikes.

*Action:* I introduced Cluster Autoscaler and Karpenter for node provisioning, set resource requests and limits consistently across all workloads using a policy enforcer, and moved batch jobs to spot instances. I also standardised namespace-level resource quotas so no single team could starve others.

*Result:* Cloud spend dropped materially (the exact figure is internal), and the team had zero capacity-related incidents in the two quarters that followed.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (the foundation). Every behavioral question at Asana responds well to Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task brief (one or two sentences each) and spend most of your time on Action. The Result should be specific: time saved, incidents prevented, or team friction reduced.

For system design questions, clarify requirements first. Candidates report that Asana interviewers want to see you ask about constraints before jumping to architecture. Ask about expected scale, SLA targets, team size, and failure tolerance before drawing a single diagram. This signals senior thinking and helps you avoid designing for the wrong problem.

For 'how do you approach X' questions, use a three-part structure. State your default stance, give a concrete past example that supports it, then name what you would adapt given Asana's scale. This shows both experience and flexibility.

For incident and debugging questions, use a timeline format. Walk through: detect, diagnose, mitigate, resolve, prevent. Interviewers are not just checking that you fixed the problem. They want to see how you communicate under pressure and how you close the loop so the issue does not recur.

Quantify without inventing numbers. If you cannot share exact figures, say 'the reduction was significant enough to come up in the quarterly review' rather than guessing a percentage. Honesty about data builds credibility, especially at a company that values transparency in its own culture.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Asana DevOps interviewers, based on publicly reported candidate experiences, consistently look for a few qualities beyond raw technical skill.

Ownership mindset. Asana has a strong culture around personal accountability. Interviewers want to hear 'I owned the outcome,' not 'the team handled it.' Use first-person language when describing your contributions, while still giving credit to collaborators where it is genuinely due.

Depth over breadth. It is better to know Terraform, Kubernetes, and AWS deeply than to list 15 tools at surface level. Candidates report that follow-up questions go several layers deep on whatever you mention, so only claim expertise in tools you can genuinely defend under questioning.

SRE thinking. Asana treats reliability as a product feature, not purely an ops concern. Be ready to discuss SLOs, error budgets, and the trade-off between shipping velocity and system stability. Interviewers notice candidates who think about reliability from the customer's perspective.

Developer empathy. DevOps at Asana means enabling engineering teams to move faster safely. Candidates who frame their work as 'how does this help a developer ship more confidently' tend to stand out over those who frame it purely as infrastructure management.

Clear, structured communication. The virtual interview format means interviewers rely entirely on what you say. Practice narrating your thought process as you work through problems. Silence while you think is fine, but narrating your reasoning signals senior-level problem-solving.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Two to three weeks before.

Audit your experience against Asana's stack. Based on publicly reported job descriptions, they rely heavily on AWS, Kubernetes (EKS), Terraform, and GitHub Actions. If you have gaps, build a small hands-on project rather than just reading documentation. Practical familiarity comes through clearly in technical rounds.

Prepare five to seven STAR stories from your own work. Cover: incident response, automation impact, cross-team collaboration, a decision you pushed back on, and a time you failed and learned from it. Asana interviewers probe these stories from multiple angles, so know each one thoroughly.

One week before.

Practice system design out loud, ideally with a peer who will push back on your assumptions. Common topics include: designing a deployment pipeline, a monitoring and alerting system, and a secrets management strategy. The goal is not the perfect diagram. The goal is articulating trade-offs clearly under pressure.

Read Asana's engineering blog and any publicly available talks from their infrastructure team. Referencing real architectural decisions they have shared publicly shows genuine interest and gives you context for their scale.

Day before.

Review your STAR stories once without over-rehearsing. Prepare two or three thoughtful questions for your interviewers, focused on on-call culture, current technical challenges, and how the team defines and measures reliability. Avoid questions whose answers are on their careers page.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Being vague about tooling. Saying 'I have used Kubernetes' without being able to discuss resource management, networking, or upgrade strategies will raise doubts. Go one layer deeper on every tool you mention and be ready for the follow-up question.

Framing team wins as personal ones without nuance. Interviewers probe. If you say 'we reduced deployment time,' they will ask what specifically you did. Have your individual contribution ready. It is perfectly fine to say 'I led this piece while a colleague owned that piece.'

Skipping behavioral prep. Many candidates over-prepare technically and walk into behavioral rounds cold. At Asana, behavioral rounds are weighted heavily alongside technical ones. Treat them as seriously as the Kubernetes deep-dive.

Giving generic answers with no Asana-specific context. Answers that could apply to any company feel low-effort. Reference the reliability and scale demands of a SaaS platform used by millions of teams, even if you are drawing on their public engineering content rather than inside knowledge.

Inventing numbers under pressure. If you do not remember the exact impact of a project, say so honestly and describe it qualitatively. Engineers at product companies like Asana will notice an implausibly round figure, and it damages your credibility on everything else you say.

Not asking questions. Candidates who ask nothing signal low interest. Prepare at least two genuine questions, particularly around on-call expectations and how the team defines and tracks reliability goals.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many interview rounds does Asana typically have for a DevOps Engineer?

Candidates report a process that typically starts with a recruiter screen, followed by one or two technical phone rounds, and then a virtual onsite with multiple interview slots covering system design, technical depth, and behavioral questions. The exact number of rounds varies by level and team. Ask your recruiter for the specific format before you begin, since processes change between hiring cycles.

Does Asana ask LeetCode-style coding questions in DevOps interviews?

Based on publicly reported experiences, Asana DevOps interviews focus far more on system design, infrastructure problem-solving, and scripting or automation tasks than on classical algorithms. You may be asked to write a short Python or Bash script, but heavy LeetCode prep is not the priority. Invest your preparation time in Kubernetes, Terraform, and AWS scenario questions instead.

What salary can I expect as a DevOps Engineer at Asana in India?

Asana does not publish India-specific salary bands publicly. Industry surveys and Glassdoor data commonly cite mid-level DevOps engineers in the 15-28 LPA range and senior engineers in the 30-50 LPA range at comparable SaaS companies in India. Actual compensation at Asana typically includes equity, which can be a meaningful portion of total pay, so always negotiate the full package rather than just base salary.

Is Asana's DevOps interview conducted remotely?

Based on publicly reported candidate experiences, Asana's India-based interview process has been fully remote. You will typically use a shared coding environment or whiteboard tool for technical rounds. Test your audio, internet connection, and screen-sharing setup well before the day, as technical glitches can break your concentration at a critical moment in the interview.

How important is knowledge of Asana's product for a DevOps interview?

You do not need to be a power user of Asana's task management product. Showing awareness of the reliability and scale demands of a SaaS platform used by millions of teams signals the right mindset, though. Candidates who frame infrastructure decisions in terms of customer impact tend to resonate with Asana's engineering culture. Spend 30 minutes reading their engineering blog before the interview.
