02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These are the questions that come up most often in Sigma Infosolutions QA interviews, based on what candidates report:

  1. Walk me through the full defect lifecycle on your current project, including how your team decides on severity versus priority.
  2. Sigma works on diverse client projects. How quickly can you pick up domain knowledge when you join a new engagement?
  3. How do you prioritise test cases when a client-facing release deadline is close and full coverage is not possible?
  4. Describe your hands-on experience with automation tools like Selenium or TestNG. How much of your current testing is automated?
  5. How would you write a test plan when the client's requirements are still incomplete or changing?
  6. Walk me through how QA fits into a two-week Agile sprint, from planning to release sign-off.
  7. What is the difference between retesting and regression testing? Give a real example from your work.
  8. How would you test a login feature end to end, covering both functional and non-functional aspects?
  9. Tell me about a critical bug you found close to a release and how you handled the situation.
  10. A developer on the project rejects your defect as 'not a bug.' What do you do next?
  11. What SQL queries do you write regularly to validate data as part of your QA work?
  12. Have you done API testing? Describe the tools you used and how you verified response accuracy on a real project.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format for all experience-based questions. Here are three worked examples.

Q: Tell me about a critical bug you found close to a release.

*Situation:* In my previous project, we were two days before go-live on a payment module when I noticed the confirmation screen was showing a total different from the amount actually being processed.

*Task:* I needed to escalate this quickly and clearly so the team could act on it without confusion or panic.

*Action:* I logged a high-severity defect with step-by-step reproduction steps, screenshots comparing both amounts, and a short note on the client risk. I tagged the tech lead and BA directly in the ticket and followed up on the team chat the same day.

*Result:* The team traced it to a rounding error in the calculation logic. The fix took a few hours, the release moved by one day, and the client said the early catch prevented a billing dispute.

---

Q: A developer rejects your defect as 'not a bug.' What do you do?

*Situation:* I raised a defect on a quantity field that accepted negative values. The developer closed it as 'by design.'

*Task:* I needed to reopen it with clear evidence tied to the documented requirements, without turning it into a personal conflict.

*Action:* I found the line in the requirements document that stated 'quantity must be a positive integer,' attached it as a comment on the ticket, and asked the BA to confirm in the same thread. I kept the message factual and short.

*Result:* The developer agreed the fix was needed and resolved it the next day. We also added a negative-value check to the regression suite to prevent a recurrence.

---

Q: How did you handle writing a test plan when requirements were unclear?

*Situation:* I was assigned to test a new reporting module mid-sprint. Large sections of the requirements document were marked incomplete or pending client sign-off.

*Task:* I had to produce a working test plan within two days, without waiting for perfect requirements.

*Action:* I set up a short call with the BA and the lead developer, listed every gap, got verbal confirmation on each point, and documented all assumptions separately in the test plan. I kept test cases modular so they could be updated quickly.

*Result:* The plan was approved with minor edits. We also identified three requirement gaps early enough for the BA to get client sign-off before the developer reached those sections.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Two frameworks cover most question types at Sigma Infosolutions.

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) works for any question that starts with 'tell me about a time' or 'describe a situation.' Keep each element brief, two to three sentences at most. Many candidates spend too long on the Situation and rush past the Result. Flip that focus: invest more time explaining what you did and what the outcome was.

Concept plus example works for technical questions like 'what is regression testing?' Give a one-sentence definition, then immediately follow with a concrete example from your own work. Interviewers at IT services companies care more about applied knowledge than textbook accuracy. If you have not used something in a real project yet, say so clearly and describe how you would approach it.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Candidates report that Sigma Infosolutions QA interviewers look for four things in particular.

Practical testing instinct. Can you look at a feature and immediately think of edge cases and failure scenarios? Interviewers probe this with open questions like 'how would you test X.' Practise thinking out loud so the interviewer can follow your logic.

Defect ownership. They want to see you follow a bug to resolution, not just log it and hand it off. In your STAR answers, always explain what the final outcome was, not just what you did.

Agile comfort. Most Sigma client projects run in Agile environments. Be ready to explain how QA fits into sprint planning, daily standups, and release sign-off cycles.

Clear communication. Sigma works with international clients, so the ability to explain a defect or test status to a non-technical stakeholder matters. Keep your answers concrete and free of testing jargon.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused one-week plan covers most of what comes up in Sigma Infosolutions QA interviews.

Days 1-2: Revisit core concepts, specifically STLC, SDLC, defect lifecycle, and the main testing types (smoke, sanity, regression, UAT). Write down one real example from your own work for each concept.

Days 3-4: Practise the 12 questions listed above out loud. Draft STAR answers for the three experience questions most likely to come up. Lead each answer with what you did and close with the outcome.

Day 5: Brush up on SQL basics, including SELECT with WHERE, GROUP BY, and JOIN. If the role mentions API testing, run through Postman and practise validating a response body and status code.

Day 6: Research Sigma Infosolutions: their main service lines, client domains, and the specific role's job description. Map two or three of your past achievements directly to their stated requirements.

Day 7: Do a mock interview with a colleague or record yourself answering out loud. Check whether your answers sound like real stories from your work history, and adjust your pacing so you are not rushing or overrunning.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Candidates who do not clear Sigma Infosolutions QA interviews typically fall into one or more of these patterns.

Giving only definitions. Saying 'regression testing means testing unchanged functionality' is not enough on its own. Follow every concept with a specific example from your actual work.

Dropping the Result in STAR answers. Many candidates describe what they did but never say what happened as a result. Always close with a clear, observable outcome.

Weak SQL preparation. QA roles at IT services companies almost always involve some database validation. If you cannot write a basic SELECT with a JOIN or WHERE clause, prepare a few common queries before your interview.

Apologising for manual testing experience. Candidates with mostly manual backgrounds sometimes treat it as a weakness. It is not. Manual testing depth is genuinely valued at Sigma, especially for client-side functional validation.

Not preparing questions to ask. Leaving the 'any questions for us' moment blank signals low interest. Prepare two or three genuine questions about the team structure, client domain, or how QA collaborates with developers on a typical project.
