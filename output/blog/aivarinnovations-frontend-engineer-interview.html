03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a feature you owned end-to-end. What trade-offs did you make?

*Situation:* Our product team wanted to add a real-time notification feed to the main dashboard. Users were missing important updates and dropping off without taking action.

*Task:* I was asked to design and build the component from scratch, including the data-fetching strategy, the UI, and a fallback for poor connections.

*Action:* I evaluated WebSockets vs long-polling and chose long-polling initially because our backend team could ship it within the same sprint without infrastructure changes. I built the feed with a virtual list to handle large payloads, added an optimistic 'mark as read' interaction, and wrote unit tests for empty states and error scenarios.

*Result:* The feature shipped on time and user engagement with the dashboard increased noticeably by internal tracking in the weeks after launch. We later migrated to WebSockets once the backend team had capacity.

---

Q: How do you debug a slow page load reported by users in production?

*Situation:* Users on a B2B product I worked on started reporting that the main listing page felt sluggish, especially on mobile devices.

*Task:* I needed to find the root cause without a reproduction environment that exactly matched production.

*Action:* I started with Chrome DevTools Performance tab and Lighthouse on the production URL. I found two problems: a large uncompressed JavaScript bundle and a waterfall of sequential API calls firing on mount. I code-split the heavy charting library behind a dynamic import and parallelised the API calls using Promise.all.

*Result:* Lighthouse scores improved according to our internal tracking and the mobile experience became noticeably smoother. Support tickets about slow loading dropped in the two weeks that followed.

---

Q: Describe a disagreement with a backend engineer or designer. How did you resolve it?

*Situation:* A designer pushed for a complex animated page transition. The backend engineer and I both felt it would hurt perceived performance on mid-range Android devices.

*Task:* I needed to push back without damaging the working relationship or dismissing a valid design idea.

*Action:* I built a quick prototype showing the full animation and a simplified fade on a mid-range device, then shared a screen recording in our team chat so the designer could see the difference directly. We set up a short call to agree on a middle ground: a lighter animation with reduced motion support baked in for accessibility.

*Result:* The designer appreciated seeing the data rather than just being told 'no'. The final animation shipped with full team support and passed our accessibility review.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For technical 'how does X work' questions: Open with a one-sentence big-picture summary, then go deeper. This shows you understand the concept, not just the keywords. Mention a real situation where this knowledge mattered in your own work if you can.

For debugging or problem-solving questions: Walk through your process in order: observe, hypothesise, isolate, fix, verify. Name the actual tools you use (Chrome DevTools, Lighthouse, Sentry). Interviewers want to see structured thinking, not a lucky guess.

For behavioural questions: Use STAR: Situation (one or two sentences of context), Task (what you were personally responsible for), Action (use 'I' not 'we'), Result (a concrete outcome, even if qualitative). Keep Situation and Task short so you spend most of the answer on Action.

For trade-off questions: Name at least two options, state the criteria you used to choose (time, team skill, scalability), and be honest about what you gave up. Product-stage companies want engineers who think in trade-offs, not engineers chasing the textbook-perfect solution.

For architecture or design questions: Narrate a structure even if there is no whiteboard. Try: 'At the top we have the component layer, below that the state layer, and the API integration sits at the bottom.' A clear structure beats an exhaustive but disorganised answer every time.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Interviewers at Aivar Innovations are typically checking for several things beyond raw technical knowledge.

Ownership mindset. They want to see that you treat a feature as yours from requirement to production, not just from design to code review. Bring at least one example where you spotted a problem that was not assigned to you and took action on it.

Communication clarity. Frontend engineers at a scaling company interact with designers, product managers, and backend engineers every day. Interviewers listen for whether you can explain a complex technical decision to a non-engineer without losing accuracy.

Practical depth. Surface knowledge of React hooks or the browser rendering model is not enough. Candidates report being asked follow-up questions that go two or three levels deeper. Prepare to explain not just 'what' but 'why' for every concept you bring up.

Quality habits. Testing, accessibility, and performance should come up naturally in your project stories, not only when you are directly asked. Mentioning these unprompted signals they are part of your default workflow.

Collaboration under disagreement. They are not looking for someone who always wins arguments, but for someone who can influence decisions with data and keep working relationships intact afterward.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Core technical review

Revise browser fundamentals (rendering pipeline, event loop, HTTP caching) and React internals (reconciliation, hooks lifecycle, render optimisation). Pick one state management library you have used and be ready to explain why you chose it and what its limitations are.

Week 2: Coding practice and story preparation

Practise a solid set of frontend coding problems covering DOM manipulation, async patterns, and component design. For each of your past two or three projects, write out the STAR version of one story you can tell in under three minutes. Prepare a short answer for every question in the section above.

Three to four days before the interview

Research Aivar Innovations: their product, the problem it solves, and any publicly available updates. Prepare two or three thoughtful questions for the interviewer focused on engineering culture, team structure, or the product roadmap.

The day before

Do a mock interview with a friend or record yourself answering three behavioural questions out loud. Check your audio, camera, and internet connection if the interview is remote. Sleep well.

On the day

Think out loud consistently. If you are stuck, say what you know and what you would look up. Asking a clarifying question before diving in is a sign of seniority, not weakness.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Stopping at the surface. Saying 'I use useEffect for side effects' is a starting point. Follow it immediately with when you would avoid it, or describe a bug it caused that you had to trace and fix.

Using 'we' instead of 'I' in behavioural answers. 'We built the feature' tells the interviewer nothing about your personal contribution. Be specific about what you personally did, decided, or built.

Skipping the Result in STAR answers. Many candidates wrap up their story at Action. Always close with a concrete outcome. If you do not have a metric, use a qualitative one: 'the feature shipped on time and had no rollback in the first month'.

Treating accessibility and testing as optional extras. If you only mention them when directly asked, it signals they are afterthoughts in your workflow. Weave them into your project stories naturally.

Preparing for a generic React interview, not this company. Aivar Innovations has 26 open roles, which means the team is scaling fast. Interviewers will likely ask how you handle onboarding, documentation, or maintaining code quality under growth pressure. Prepare for those angles specifically.

Forgetting to ask questions. Candidates who ask nothing at the end signal low interest. Prepare at least two genuine questions about the team or the product.
