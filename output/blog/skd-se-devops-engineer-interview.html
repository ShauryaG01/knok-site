02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly in DevOps rounds at companies like SKD SE, based on what candidates typically report:

  1. Walk me through how you have set up a CI/CD pipeline from scratch. What tools did you choose and why?
  2. How do you handle secrets and credentials in a Kubernetes environment? What mistakes have you seen teams make?
  3. Describe a production incident you owned. How did you detect it, respond, and prevent recurrence?
  4. We run microservices on Kubernetes. How would you approach cluster autoscaling and cost optimisation together?
  5. How do you write infrastructure as code? Walk us through a recent Terraform or Pulumi module you built.
  6. A deployment breaks in production and rollback is not straightforward. What is your decision process?
  7. How do you monitor application health versus infrastructure health? Which alerting tool would you recommend and why?
  8. Explain how you would implement a GitOps workflow. What does 'source of truth' mean in practice for your team?
  9. How do you enforce security policies across a multi-cloud or hybrid environment without slowing down developer velocity?
  10. Tell me about a time you automated something that saved significant manual effort. How did you measure the impact?
  11. How do you manage configuration drift across hundreds of servers or containers?
  12. What does your on-call process look like? How do you write runbooks that a junior engineer can actually follow?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a production incident you owned.

*Situation:* Our payment service started throwing errors late on a Friday evening. Alerts fired but the on-call engineer was unclear on next steps.

*Task:* As the senior DevOps engineer on rotation, I took ownership and had to both resolve the issue and guide a junior colleague through the process.

*Action:* I pulled logs from our centralised logging tool, cross-referenced CPU and memory metrics in our monitoring dashboard, and traced the issue to a misconfigured resource limit on the pod. I patched the Kubernetes manifest, triggered a rolling restart, and pushed the fix via our GitOps pipeline. I updated the incident channel at regular intervals so stakeholders were not left guessing.

*Result:* Service was restored within the hour. I then wrote a postmortem and updated the runbook so the same scenario would be resolved much faster next time.

---

Q: How have you implemented infrastructure as code at a previous company?

*Situation:* The company I joined had infrastructure managed entirely by hand. Environments were inconsistent and provisioning a new staging environment took several days.

*Task:* I was asked to lead the migration to infrastructure as code using Terraform, with the goal of making environment creation repeatable and auditable.

*Action:* I modularised the Terraform codebase by service domain, enforced a code review process for all infrastructure changes, and integrated plan and apply steps into our existing CI pipeline. I held knowledge-sharing sessions so developers could self-serve basic changes without raising tickets.

*Result:* New environment provisioning dropped from days to a matter of hours. Infrastructure changes became reviewable and rollback-friendly, which directly reduced the number of config-related incidents.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you improved deployment frequency without increasing risk.

*Situation:* Our team was releasing once every couple of weeks because manual testing and approval gates made more frequent releases feel risky.

*Task:* I was tasked with redesigning the release pipeline to allow daily deployments while keeping regression risk low.

*Action:* I introduced automated integration tests as a mandatory gate, implemented feature flags so incomplete work could ship safely, and added canary deployments so new versions rolled out to a small slice of traffic first. I also set up automatic rollback triggers based on error-rate thresholds.

*Result:* The team moved to daily releases within a couple of months. Rollback-triggering incidents dropped because canary deployments caught issues before full rollout.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use STAR for behavioural questions. Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep the Situation short (one or two sentences), spend most of your time on Action, and always land on a concrete Result. Vague endings like 'it went well' lose marks.

Use a structured walkthrough for technical questions. When asked 'how would you...' questions, candidates who score well typically follow this pattern: (1) state the goal and constraints, (2) name the tools or approach, (3) call out the tradeoffs, (4) mention how you would validate or monitor the outcome. This shows systems thinking, not just tool knowledge.

For 'tell me about a failure' questions, interviewers are not looking for perfection. They want to see that you do honest postmortems, own your part, and change your behaviour. Candidates who blame the tools or the team without self-reflection rarely move forward.

For salary discussions, know your target range before the call. The market data shows mid-level DevOps roles are commonly cited in the 15-28 LPA range. Anchor to your value, not just market averages.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates typically report from DevOps rounds at companies like SKD SE, interviewers are looking for a few consistent things.

Ownership mindset. DevOps sits at the intersection of development and operations, and companies want people who will own an issue end-to-end rather than hand it off. Concrete stories of 'I fixed it and then prevented it' score higher than 'I escalated it.'

Tool depth over tool breadth. Listing a long set of tools on your resume but being shallow on all of them is a red flag. Interviewers would rather hear you go deep on two or three: how you set them up, what went wrong, and what you would do differently.

Security-aware thinking. Candidates who mention secrets management, least-privilege IAM policies, and image scanning without being prompted stand out. Security is no longer optional in DevOps roles.

Communication under pressure. Incident response questions often test not just technical skill but your ability to communicate clearly while a system is down. Use your sample answers to demonstrate calm, structured thinking.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Foundations and gap review. Go through the core areas likely to come up: CI/CD pipelines (Jenkins, GitHub Actions, GitLab CI), container orchestration (Kubernetes), infrastructure as code (Terraform), monitoring (Prometheus, Grafana, or similar), and cloud platforms (AWS, GCP, or Azure). List the areas where you are weakest and prioritise those first.

Week 2: Hands-on practice. Build a small project that demonstrates your skills. A simple app deployed to Kubernetes via a CI/CD pipeline, with monitoring and alerting, covers most of what interviewers want to see. Candidates who can demo real work are far more credible than those who only talk about past projects.

Week 3: Story preparation. Write out five to seven STAR stories covering incidents, automation wins, cross-team collaboration, and a failure with a lesson. Practice saying them out loud. Aim for two to three minutes per story so you stay focused and do not ramble.

Before your SKD SE interview, research the products or services the company runs. Understanding their scale and stack helps you tailor your answers. Candidates typically report that asking thoughtful questions about the team's current pain points leaves a strong impression.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Talking tools without context. Saying 'I used Kubernetes' is not enough. Interviewers want to know the scale, the problem you were solving, and the decisions you made.

Skipping the 'why'. Candidates who explain why they picked a particular tool or approach are more convincing than those who just describe what they did. Tradeoffs show seniority.

Vague results in STAR answers. 'It improved performance' is weak. Describing a concrete, measurable outcome is far stronger. If you do not have exact figures, use honest qualifiers like 'roughly' or 'by our estimation' rather than inventing numbers.

Under-preparing for incident response questions. These are almost universal in DevOps interviews. If you do not have a real incident story ready, prepare a realistic hypothetical and be upfront that it is a constructed scenario.

Ignoring security topics. Candidates who cannot speak to secrets management, network policies, or image scanning increasingly lose points as security expectations have risen across the industry.

Not asking questions. Closing an interview without questions signals low curiosity. Ask about the team's current on-call load, their biggest infrastructure challenge, or how DevOps and development teams collaborate day-to-day.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the SKD SE DevOps interview typically have?

Candidates typically report two to three technical rounds plus a final managerial or culture-fit conversation. Exact formats vary by team and role level, so confirm the structure with your recruiter before each round. The technical rounds usually focus on hands-on problem-solving and past-project deep dives rather than purely theoretical questions.

What salary can I expect as a DevOps Engineer at SKD SE?

Individual offers depend on your experience, the specific team, and your negotiation. Broadly, the market for DevOps Engineers in India shows entry-level roles in the 6-12 LPA range, mid-level in 15-28 LPA, and senior roles in the 30-50 LPA range, based on knok jobradar data from July 2026. For company-specific figures, check Glassdoor or levels.fyi for reported compensation at SKD SE. Always negotiate: published ranges are typically starting points.

Which tools should I focus on before the interview?

Focus on the tools you have actually used and can speak to in depth. The most commonly tested areas in DevOps interviews are CI/CD (GitHub Actions, Jenkins, or GitLab CI), Kubernetes, Terraform or another IaC tool, and at least one major cloud platform. Monitoring tools like Prometheus and Grafana come up frequently as well. Depth on a few tools beats a shallow list of many.

Is SKD SE actively hiring DevOps Engineers right now?

Yes. As of July 2026, SKD SE has 86 open DevOps Engineer roles tracked by knok jobradar, which is a significant volume. This suggests active hiring across multiple teams or projects. Check the company careers page for the most current listings, as positions open and close quickly.

How important is cloud certification for this role?

Certifications like AWS Solutions Architect or CKA (Certified Kubernetes Administrator) can help your resume clear initial screening, but candidates report that interviewers care far more about demonstrated hands-on experience than paper credentials. A working project you can demo or explain in detail is typically worth more in the interview itself. Studying for certifications does build systematic knowledge, which tends to show up positively in technical rounds.

What questions should I ask at the end of the SKD SE interview?

Good questions show genuine interest and curiosity. Try asking about the team's biggest current infrastructure challenge, how on-call is structured and what the rotation looks like, or how DevOps engineers collaborate with development teams day-to-day. You can also ask about the stack and what migrations or modernisation efforts are currently in progress. Avoid asking only about salary or benefits in the first technical round.
