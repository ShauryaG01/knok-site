01 Overview

You are validating payroll calculations, statutory compliance, leave management, and employee self-service workflows that businesses depend on every month to pay their staff correctly.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How would you design test cases for a statutory deduction feature?

*Situation:* At my previous company, we built a PF and ESI deduction module for a payroll product serving multiple clients with varied employee profiles.

*Task:* I had to create test cases ensuring deductions were calculated correctly for all employee salary brackets, employment types, and joining scenarios.

*Action:* I mapped out the salary thresholds that affect ESI eligibility and wrote boundary value test cases for employees just above and just below each threshold. I also covered new joiners, mid-month exits, employees with salary revisions mid-pay-period, and contract workers. I verified both employee and employer contribution splits and cross-checked what the database stored against what appeared on the payslip.

*Result:* We caught a rounding error causing small discrepancies across a large batch of payslips. Fixing it before go-live saved the client from a compliance issue. The test suite became a reusable regression set for every future statutory change.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you found a critical bug just before a release.

*Situation:* The day before a major release of a payroll processing module, I was running final sanity checks with the dev team on standby.

*Task:* I needed to quickly validate all high-priority workflows without slowing down the release timeline.

*Action:* During a smoke test of the full payroll run, I noticed that salary slips for employees who had resigned mid-month were showing an incorrect count of paid working days. I documented the reproduction steps, attached screenshots and database query results, and filed the bug with a blocker priority label, tagging the developer and the release manager directly.

*Result:* The team resolved the issue within a few hours and we released on schedule. The incident prompted us to add a dedicated 'exit-month payroll' scenario to our core regression checklist, which has caught similar issues in later releases.

---

Q: How do you handle a situation where a developer disagrees with your bug report?

*Situation:* I raised a bug on a tax computation feature where the output did not match what the product's own help documentation described.

*Task:* The developer believed the behavior was intentional and wanted to close the ticket without a fix.

*Action:* Rather than arguing, I pulled up the product documentation and the relevant compliance guideline side by side, noted the exact mismatch, and asked the developer and the product manager to review together in a short call. I kept the discussion factual, focused on what the end user would experience, and acknowledged upfront that it might be a documentation gap rather than a code defect.

*Result:* The team agreed the code needed a small correction. The experience reinforced that bug reports backed by clear evidence and explicit user impact are much harder to dismiss, and I now always include a brief 'business impact' line in every bug I file.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) works best for behavioral questions like 'tell me about a time.' Keep the Situation brief (one or two sentences), spend most of your answer on the Action (what you specifically did, not what the team did), and close with a concrete, observable Result.

Boundary value analysis framing is the go-to structure for test case design questions. Walk the interviewer through: identify the valid input range, test the lower boundary, the upper boundary, and values just inside and just outside each. For payroll features, boundaries often sit at salary thresholds that trigger statutory eligibility changes, so mentioning those explicitly shows domain awareness.

Bug reporting structure is worth practising out loud before the interview. A strong bug report covers: steps to reproduce, expected vs. actual behavior, environment details, severity and priority, and supporting evidence such as screenshots, logs, or database query results. At Greytip, adding a 'business impact' line (how many employees or clients are affected) typically makes your reports stand out from those that describe only the technical symptom.

For SQL questions, state your query intent before writing any syntax. Saying 'I would join the salary table with the deduction table and filter by pay period' before writing the SELECT statement shows you think before you type, which interviewers value over someone who jumps straight to syntax and then backtracks.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Greytip QA interviewers look for a combination of solid testing fundamentals and genuine interest in how payroll and HR software works.

Domain awareness matters more here than at a generic software company. You do not need to be a payroll expert on day one, but understanding concepts like gross-to-net calculation, statutory deductions, leave encashment, and attendance integration signals that you can ramp up quickly and ask the right questions when requirements are unclear.

Structured, edge-case thinking is central to the role. Interviewers want to see that you naturally consider boundary values, negative scenarios, and data consistency, not just the happy path. When asked to design test cases in the interview, cover scenarios like employees with zero pay days, mid-month joiners, salary revisions, and employees working across multiple states.

Communication and ownership come up strongly. Greytip's product directly affects payroll runs that cannot have errors. Interviewers want someone who takes quality seriously, escalates blockers clearly and early, and writes bug reports that any team member can act on without asking follow-up questions.

Collaboration without ego rounds out the picture. Payroll software involves many edge cases that surface from real client usage. Interviewers want QA engineers who treat disagreements with developers as a search for the right answer, not a contest to win.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Solidify testing fundamentals first. Review test case design techniques: equivalence partitioning, boundary value analysis, and decision table testing. Practice writing test cases for payroll scenarios, focusing on what could go wrong in a calculation rather than only what should go right.

Understand Indian payroll basics conceptually. Read up on how gross-to-net payroll works in India: what components make up CTC, how statutory deductions like PF and ESI work logically, how professional tax varies by state, and how leave deductions feed into net pay. You do not need to memorize specific statutory rates. Understanding the logic and knowing where edge cases live is what the interview is testing.

Practice SQL. Greytip's technical rounds commonly include database validation questions. Practice joins, aggregations, filtering by date ranges, and writing queries that find mismatched or duplicate records in a payroll dataset. A useful exercise: write a query that checks whether the sum of individual deduction rows matches the total deduction column in a salary table.

Prepare three STAR stories. Have ready: a high-impact bug you found and its consequence, a time you improved a testing process or automated something repetitive, and a conflict or disagreement you resolved constructively with a developer or stakeholder.

Research the greytHR product. Sign up for a trial account or watch product demo videos. Being able to reference the payroll run workflow, the leave module, or the employee self-service portal in your answers makes a noticeably stronger impression than speaking only in the abstract.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Covering only the happy path. When asked to write test cases, many candidates list only the expected scenario. Greytip interviewers specifically probe for edge cases: what if an employee has zero pay days? What if a salary revision happens mid-pay-period? Always include at least two or three boundary or negative scenarios without waiting to be asked.

Not connecting testing to business impact. Saying 'I found a bug in the calculation' is weaker than 'I found a bug that would have caused incorrect net pay for employees who joined mid-month.' In payroll software, bugs have direct financial and compliance consequences. Make that connection explicit in every example you give.

Being vague about tools. Saying 'I use Selenium' without explaining what you automated, how the framework is structured, or how it fits into the CI/CD pipeline adds little. Be specific about what you built, why you built it, and what defects it actually caught.

Skipping SQL preparation. Many candidates underestimate database validation questions. Verifying data correctness at the database level is a real part of the QA job at Greytip. Prepare a few queries you would actually run to validate a payroll run, and be ready to explain your reasoning.

Not asking any questions at the end. Candidates who ask nothing signal low curiosity. Strong questions to close with: how does the team handle compliance changes when a new government notification is released? What does the release cycle look like? How early does QA get involved in the requirements stage?

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many interview rounds does Greytip typically have for QA Engineer roles?

Candidates report the process typically involves an initial screening call, one or two technical rounds, and a final HR discussion. The technical rounds cover testing fundamentals, SQL, and payroll domain scenarios. The exact number of rounds can vary based on the seniority of the role and the hiring manager's process.

Do I need payroll domain knowledge before applying to Greytip as a QA Engineer?

Prior payroll experience helps but is not typically listed as a hard requirement for entry or mid-level roles. What matters more is demonstrating structured thinking about complex calculations and a willingness to learn domain concepts quickly. Reading up on how Indian payroll works conceptually before the interview, including PF, ESI, and gross-to-net logic, will set you apart from candidates who show up without any domain preparation.

Is there a coding or automation test in the Greytip QA interview?

Candidates report that the focus is more on manual testing concepts, test case design, and SQL than on writing automation code from scratch during the interview. That said, if your resume mentions automation tools like Selenium or Rest Assured, expect detailed follow-up questions about your specific work. Preparing a clear explanation of an automation framework you have built or contributed to is a good idea.

What salary can I expect as a QA Engineer at Greytip?

Based on knok jobradar data for QA Engineer roles across India as of July 2026, typical market ranges are 4-9 LPA at entry level (0-2 years), 9-17 LPA at mid level (3-5 years), and 17-30 LPA at senior level (6-9 years). Greytip-specific compensation may differ from these market ranges. For current offers, checking Glassdoor or AmbitionBox for recent employee-reported data is a good complement.

How long does the Greytip hiring process take from application to offer?

Candidates report the full process typically takes a few weeks from the first screening call to an offer, though this varies based on role urgency and interview scheduling. Following up politely after each round is both acceptable and often expected. Applying through Greytip's own careers page may get faster responses than applying through third-party job boards.

What testing tools and technologies are most relevant for a Greytip QA role?

Candidates report that Greytip QA roles commonly involve manual testing, SQL for data validation, and API testing using tools like Postman. Familiarity with bug tracking and test management tools such as JIRA is useful across all levels. For senior roles, experience with automation frameworks like Selenium or Rest Assured is a plus. Domain knowledge of payroll and HRMS workflows matters as much as, or more than, specific tool expertise for most mid-level and above positions.
