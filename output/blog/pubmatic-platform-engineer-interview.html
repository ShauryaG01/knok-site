01 Overview

Overview

PubMatic is an ad-technology company that operates a supply-side platform connecting digital publishers to programmatic buyers. Platform Engineers here own the infrastructure that makes real-time ad auctions possible: container orchestration, deployment pipelines, observability stacks, and reliability systems. The scale of PubMatic's operations means the role demands deep infrastructure knowledge and a strong reliability mindset.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These are the types of questions candidates report seeing most often in PubMatic Platform Engineer interviews. Questions reflect the company's focus on real-time, high-scale infrastructure.

  1. How would you design infrastructure to support real-time ad bidding at very high throughput with strict latency requirements?
  2. Describe a Kubernetes cluster upgrade you managed in production. How did you ensure zero downtime?
  3. How do you approach capacity planning for systems that experience sudden traffic spikes during peak ad-buying windows?
  4. Walk through how you have set up or improved a CI/CD pipeline for a microservices-based platform.
  5. What does your on-call process look like? Describe a high-severity production incident you personally owned from detection to resolution.
  6. How have you used infrastructure-as-code tools such as Terraform or Ansible? What challenges did you run into at scale?
  7. How would you diagnose and fix a Kafka consumer group that is consistently falling behind in a high-volume event stream?
  8. What is your approach to observability? What metrics, logs, and traces would you set up before a new service goes to production?
  9. Have you managed multi-region or multi-cloud deployments? What trade-offs did you navigate?
  10. How comfortable are you with Linux performance tuning? Describe a situation where you identified and resolved a kernel or OS-level bottleneck.
  11. How would you handle a scenario where a platform change you pushed caused a production incident with direct revenue impact?
  12. Describe a time you reduced infrastructure costs without sacrificing reliability or developer productivity.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a Kubernetes cluster upgrade you managed. How did you ensure zero downtime?

*Situation:* My team ran a Kubernetes cluster that served our core API traffic. We were two minor versions behind and needed to upgrade before the current version lost community support.

*Task:* I was responsible for planning and executing the upgrade with no downtime for production workloads.

*Action:* I first audited all running workloads for deprecated API versions using 'kubectl' tooling and updated manifests ahead of time. I upgraded control plane nodes one at a time, then cordoned and drained worker nodes in small batches while monitoring pod disruption budgets closely. I prepared a detailed rollback runbook and validated the entire upgrade sequence in staging before touching production.

*Result:* We completed the upgrade with zero service disruption. Post-upgrade monitoring showed normal latency and error rates, and the team gained a reusable runbook for future upgrade cycles.

---

Q: Describe a high-severity incident you personally owned from detection to resolution.

*Situation:* I was on call when our monitoring flagged a sudden drop in successful bid responses from one of our ad-serving pods.

*Task:* I had to identify the root cause quickly because any delay directly impacted publisher revenue.

*Action:* I pulled recent deployment logs and noticed a configuration change had shipped about thirty minutes before the alert fired. I correlated this with a spike in connection errors in the service logs. I rolled back the configuration change, confirmed that metrics stabilised, and opened a detailed incident report. Once the rollback was confirmed safe, I completed a root-cause analysis and added a pre-deploy configuration validation step to the pipeline.

*Result:* The service recovered within a few minutes of detection. The post-incident review was accepted by the engineering team, and the new validation step caught two similar misconfigurations in the weeks that followed.

---

Q: Describe a time you reduced infrastructure costs without sacrificing reliability.

*Situation:* Our platform had grown quickly and a significant portion of our cloud spend was going to always-on compute that sat mostly idle outside business hours.

*Task:* My manager asked me to identify savings opportunities without impacting our agreed SLAs.

*Action:* I profiled resource usage across our clusters over a two-week window and identified non-critical batch jobs and internal tooling workloads that could safely run on spot or preemptible instances. I also right-sized several over-provisioned node groups based on actual CPU and memory utilisation data. For stateful production services I kept on-demand instances but introduced autoscaling policies with conservative thresholds.

*Result:* We reduced our monthly cloud bill meaningfully (exact figures are confidential to the company) while keeping all production SLAs intact. The autoscaling changes also improved burst capacity during peak traffic periods.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For system design questions: Start by clarifying requirements before drawing any architecture. Ask about expected scale, latency targets, and acceptable failure modes. Then sketch your high-level components before going deep on any single one. PubMatic's domain is real-time bidding, so demonstrate that you treat low latency and high availability as core constraints, not nice-to-haves.

For behavioural questions: Use STAR: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep the Situation brief (one or two sentences). Spend most of your time on Action, specifically what you personally did rather than what the team did. Make the Result concrete. If exact numbers are confidential, describe the outcome qualitatively and say so.

For infrastructure depth questions: The pattern that works well is: state your understanding of the concept, give a real example from your own production experience, then name one trade-off or limitation you had to manage. Interviewers at platform-focused companies want to see that you have operated these tools in production, not just read documentation about them.

For troubleshooting questions: Walk through your diagnostic process step by step. Name the specific tools or commands you would use (for example 'kubectl describe', 'kafka-consumer-groups.sh', 'perf'). Show that you form a hypothesis, gather evidence, and confirm before acting. Avoid jumping straight to solutions without showing your reasoning.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

PubMatic's platform teams run real-time, high-scale workloads where downtime translates directly to lost revenue. Interviewers are looking for engineers who understand what that means operationally.

Proven infrastructure depth. Candidates who speak concretely about Kubernetes, Linux, cloud platforms, and CI/CD pipelines in production contexts consistently receive stronger feedback than those who describe only conceptual knowledge. Have specific examples ready before you walk in.

Scale awareness. Ad-tech platforms handle very large request volumes. Interviewers want to hear you think about bottlenecks, failure modes, and capacity limits proactively rather than as an afterthought.

Reliability mindset. Expect questions about on-call culture, incident response, and how you build systems that fail gracefully. Candidates who treat reliability as a core engineering concern rather than a secondary concern stand out clearly.

Cost consciousness. Cloud spend matters significantly in ad-tech. Showing that you balance reliability with cost efficiency signals engineering maturity beyond just technical skill.

Cross-team communication. Platform Engineers support product developers. Interviewers look for candidates who can translate technical constraints into plain language and who work well with teams outside their immediate group.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Infrastructure and cloud depth. Review your Kubernetes experience end to end: cluster setup and upgrades, pod scheduling, networking, storage, and RBAC. Revisit your infrastructure-as-code work in Terraform or whatever tool you use most. Brush up on Linux fundamentals: process management, networking tools, and basic performance profiling commands.

Week 2: System design for scale. Practice designing distributed systems with throughput and reliability as primary constraints. Study how message queues like Kafka behave under load and what you would do when a consumer falls behind. Review observability patterns: what metrics, logs, and traces you would set up for a new service. Read up on the basics of a supply-side platform and real-time bidding so you can contextualise your answers naturally.

Week 3: Behavioural preparation. Write out three to five STAR stories covering incidents you owned, cost optimisation work, cross-team collaboration, and times you pushed back on or escalated a technical decision. Practice delivering each story in under two minutes. Review the on-call incidents you have handled and be ready to walk through your exact decision-making process.

Week 4: Mock rounds and final review. Do at least two full mock interviews, one system design and one behavioural. Review the job description keywords and map each to a concrete experience you can discuss. Prepare three to four thoughtful questions for your interviewers about on-call rotation, deployment cadence, and upcoming infrastructure projects.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Staying too high level in system design. Saying 'use Kubernetes and Kafka' is not enough. Interviewers want to hear how you would configure, scale, monitor, and operate those tools in production. Go one level deeper than you think is necessary.

Describing team actions instead of your own. Candidates often say 'we fixed the incident' rather than 'I specifically did X.' Use first-person language when describing your contribution. Interviewers are assessing you, not your former team.

Ignoring the ad-tech context. Platform Engineers at PubMatic are not building general-purpose infrastructure. Understanding that the platform serves real-time bidding with direct revenue implications will help you frame answers far more relevantly than a generic infrastructure answer would.

Not preparing questions to ask. Candidates who ask no questions at the end of the interview signal low interest in the role. Prepare genuine questions about team structure, on-call expectations, and upcoming infrastructure projects.

Overcomplicating system design answers. Some candidates propose elaborate architectures to show depth. If the question does not call for that complexity, a proportionate answer with clear reasoning is stronger than a sprawling design you cannot fully defend.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does PubMatic's Platform Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically involves three to five rounds. These commonly include a recruiter screen, one or two technical rounds covering Kubernetes, cloud infrastructure, and scripting, a system design round, and a behavioural or hiring manager round. The exact structure can vary by team, so it is worth asking the recruiter for a clear outline when you schedule your first round.

Does PubMatic ask coding questions in Platform Engineer interviews?

Typically, Platform Engineer interviews at PubMatic are not heavy on algorithmic coding. Candidates report more focus on scripting in Bash or Python, infrastructure troubleshooting scenarios, and system design. Some teams may include a light scripting task, so brushing up on automation scripts is a worthwhile part of your preparation.

How important is ad-tech knowledge going into the interview?

You do not need to be an ad-tech expert, but knowing the basics of real-time bidding and how a supply-side platform works will help you contextualise infrastructure answers more effectively. Interviewers appreciate candidates who understand why low latency and high availability matter in PubMatic's specific business context, not just as abstract engineering principles.

What cloud platforms should I prepare for?

PubMatic publicly reports using a mix of on-premise and cloud infrastructure. Candidates report interview questions touching AWS and GCP concepts. Strong fundamentals across any major cloud provider, combined with solid infrastructure-as-code experience, transfer well regardless of which specific platform the team uses day to day.

Is the PubMatic interview process remote or in-office?

Candidates report that initial rounds are typically conducted remotely via video call. Later rounds, especially final interviews, may be held in-office depending on the team and location. Confirm the format with your recruiter after receiving your invite, as this can vary by hiring manager and city.

How long does it take to hear back after the interview?

Candidates report that PubMatic's feedback cycle typically takes one to two weeks after the final round. If you have not heard back after that window, a single polite follow-up to the recruiter is appropriate. Sending a brief thank-you note within a day of your interview is also good practice and keeps you visible.
