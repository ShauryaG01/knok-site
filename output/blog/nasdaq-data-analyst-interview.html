02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions are drawn from candidate reports and reflect what interviewers at Nasdaq's data teams typically focus on.

  1. Walk me through how you would clean a dataset with missing values and duplicates before analysis.
  2. Write a SQL query to find the top clients by trade volume over a rolling time window.
  3. How would you design a dashboard to monitor anomalies in real-time trading data?
  4. Explain the difference between a fact table and a dimension table in a data warehouse context.
  5. You notice a sudden data spike for one region but not others. How do you investigate the root cause?
  6. How have you handled large datasets that could not fit into memory on a single machine?
  7. Describe a time you communicated a complex data finding to a non-technical stakeholder.
  8. What metrics would you use to measure the health and reliability of a trading or market data platform?
  9. How do you ensure data quality and catch silent failures in an automated pipeline?
  10. Tell me about a project where your analysis directly influenced a business decision.
  11. How would you design a schema for storing and querying historical stock price data efficiently?
  12. What Python libraries do you rely on for financial or time-series data, and why?

For SQL questions, candidates report being asked to write queries on the spot. Practise window functions, CTEs, and aggregations on financial-style datasets ahead of time.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a project where your analysis directly influenced a business decision.

*Situation:* At a previous company, the product team was debating whether to invest in building a new client-facing reporting module.

*Task:* I was asked to analyse usage data from existing reports to give leadership a data-backed recommendation.

*Action:* I pulled several months of event log data, segmented clients by usage frequency and industry type, and identified which report types drove genuine engagement versus which were being ignored. I summarised the findings in a concise presentation with a clear recommendation.

*Result:* Leadership chose to deprecate two low-usage reports and fast-track one high-demand feature. Engineering capacity was freed up, and the decision was reached in a single meeting rather than dragging across weeks of debate.

---

Q: Describe a time you had to communicate a complex data finding to a non-technical stakeholder.

*Situation:* At a previous role, a daily reconciliation report was flagging mismatches between two internal systems, and the finance manager needed a clear explanation before month-end close.

*Task:* I had to translate a technical data lineage problem into language a non-technical audience could act on quickly.

*Action:* I traced the root cause to a timezone conversion error in the ETL pipeline. Instead of showing code, I drew a simple process flow diagram showing where data entered each system and where the gap appeared.

*Result:* The finance manager understood the issue immediately, approved a fix the same day, and the reconciliation report was clean for the next cycle.

---

Q: How have you handled large datasets that could not fit into memory?

*Situation:* On a fraud detection project, I needed to analyse several years of transaction logs. The full dataset was too large to load into a standard pandas DataFrame on the available machine.

*Task:* I had to complete the analysis within a short deadline without access to a distributed cluster.

*Action:* I used chunked reading in pandas to process data in batches, applied early filtering in SQL before pulling data into Python, and cached intermediate aggregations to disk to avoid recomputation.

*Result:* The analysis finished on time, and the chunked pipeline approach was documented and adopted by the team for future large-dataset work.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For behavioural questions, use the STAR structure: Situation (brief context), Task (your specific responsibility), Action (the steps you personally took), Result (measurable or observable outcome). Keep each part focused. Interviewers lose interest when the Situation runs longer than a minute.

For SQL questions, think aloud in layers: Confirm what the question is really asking. State which tables you need and how they join. Write the core logic first, then add filters, grouping, and window functions. If you spot an edge case (such as NULLs or duplicate rows), mention it before writing the fix.

For system design or schema questions, anchor to the use case: Ask what queries will run most often. Choose between a normalised model (good for writes and data integrity) and a denormalised or star schema (good for fast reads). Nasdaq candidates report that interviewers appreciate awareness of time-series data patterns and partition strategies for large historical tables.

For analytical case questions, show a structured breakdown: Restate the business problem. List the data sources you would need. Describe the analysis steps. Name the output format (dashboard, report, alerting rule) and who the audience is. Nasdaq interviewers typically want to see that you think about the consumer of the analysis, not just the query.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Financial domain awareness matters. Nasdaq builds tools for exchanges, brokers, and regulators. You do not need to be a trader, but you should be comfortable with concepts like trade reconciliation, market data feeds, OHLCV (open/high/low/close/volume) data, and latency-sensitive reporting. Candidates who have never encountered these terms tend to struggle in case rounds.

SQL and Python fluency, not just familiarity. Expect to write queries without autocomplete, explain query plans, and discuss how you would optimise a slow query. On the Python side, pandas and NumPy are standard. Knowledge of Spark or Dask for larger datasets is a plus that candidates report being asked about at the senior level.

Clear communication under pressure. Data analyst roles at Nasdaq often interface with business stakeholders, compliance teams, and engineers. Interviewers look for candidates who can explain a finding clearly, flag data quality concerns proactively, and push back with evidence when a stakeholder assumption looks wrong.

Ownership and initiative. Strong candidates do not just describe what tools they used. They describe what problem they saw, why it mattered, what they chose to do, and what changed as a result. Passive descriptions ('I was part of a team that built...') score lower than active ownership in behavioural rounds.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Phase 1: SQL and Python refresh (first week)
Practise window functions (RANK, ROW_NUMBER, LAG, LEAD), CTEs, and aggregations on financial-style datasets. In Python, revise pandas operations: groupby, merge, resample for time-series, and handling missing values. Use freely available SQL practice platforms to build speed and confidence writing queries under time pressure.

Phase 2: Financial domain grounding (start of second week)
Read up on how stock exchanges work at a high level. Understand what market data feeds contain, what trade reconciliation means, and what a risk report typically tracks. You do not need deep finance knowledge, but you should be able to discuss these concepts without freezing when a case question uses them.

Phase 3: Behavioural story bank (mid second week)
Write out five to six STAR stories covering: influencing a business decision, handling a data quality issue, communicating to non-technical stakeholders, working under a tight deadline, and disagreeing with a team decision based on data. Practice saying each story out loud. Hearing yourself helps cut filler and tighten the narrative.

Phase 4: Mock rounds (third week)
Do at least two full mock interviews. One focused on SQL and Python (timed), one on case and behavioural questions. Ask for honest feedback. Candidates typically report that the live coding round is where unprepared applicants drop out, so simulate the pressure before the real thing.

On the day: Read Nasdaq's recent investor news or product announcements so you can reference real context if asked about business priorities. Have your STAR stories ready but keep them conversational, not rehearsed. 07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping the financial context. Many candidates prepare for a generic analyst interview and are caught off guard by questions about trade data, reconciliation, or market data quality. Spending a few days on domain basics pays off significantly in case and stakeholder rounds.

Writing SQL without explaining it. Interviewers want to follow your thought process. Narrate as you write. If you hit a wall, say what you are trying to do even if the exact syntax is not perfect yet.

Vague STAR answers. Saying 'I improved the dashboard' is weak. Say what specifically changed, what the result was, and why it mattered to the business. If you genuinely do not have a metric, describe the qualitative outcome clearly and concisely.

Ignoring data quality edge cases. In financial data, NULL values, duplicate records, and late-arriving events are common and consequential. Not mentioning them in a design or coding question signals inexperience with real-world messy data.

Over-explaining tools instead of outcomes. Saying 'I used Tableau and built calculated fields' is not a story. The story is what question you answered, what the stakeholder did with the insight, and why it mattered to the business.

Not asking clarifying questions. In case and schema design questions, jumping straight to an answer without confirming the scope suggests you skip requirements gathering in real work. A short clarifying question often impresses interviewers more than a fast but poorly scoped answer.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does a Nasdaq Data Analyst interview typically have?

Candidates typically report three to five rounds in total. These commonly include an HR screening call, a technical assessment (SQL or Python, sometimes both), one or two rounds with data team members or senior analysts, and a final round with a hiring manager. Some teams add a case presentation round, so confirm the full process with your recruiter early.

What SQL topics should I prepare for the Nasdaq interview?

Candidates report being tested on JOINs, GROUP BY with HAVING, window functions (RANK, ROW_NUMBER, LAG, LEAD), and CTEs. Questions often use financial-style scenarios such as computing running totals, finding top clients by volume, or identifying gaps in time-series data. Practice writing these without autocomplete and be ready to explain how you would handle edge cases like NULLs or duplicate rows.

Do I need a finance background to interview for a Data Analyst role at Nasdaq?

A formal finance degree is not required, but familiarity with financial data concepts helps significantly. Terms like OHLCV data, trade reconciliation, market data feeds, and risk reporting come up in both interviews and day-to-day work. Candidates with no exposure to these concepts tend to struggle in case rounds, while a few days of self-study gives a meaningful edge.

What salary can I expect for a Data Analyst role at Nasdaq in India?

Nasdaq's Data Analyst roles are typically filled at the mid-to-senior experience level. Based on knok's job radar data for India (July 2026), mid-level roles (3-5 years experience) show market ranges of 10-18 LPA, and senior roles (6-9 years) show 18-30 LPA. For Nasdaq-specific compensation figures, check Glassdoor or levels.fyi for recent self-reported numbers from employees.

How long does the Nasdaq hiring process take from application to offer?

Candidates typically report the process taking two to five weeks from the first HR call to offer. The timeline can extend if multiple stakeholders are involved in the final decision or if the team is interviewing several candidates in parallel. Following up politely with your recruiter after each round is reasonable and generally expected.

Is there a take-home assignment in the Nasdaq Data Analyst interview?

Some Nasdaq teams include a take-home SQL or Python assignment, while others use a live coding screen instead. Candidates report both formats depending on the specific team and role level. If a take-home is given, focus on clean readable code and a concise written explanation of your approach, since reviewers often value how you communicate your thinking as much as the solution itself.
