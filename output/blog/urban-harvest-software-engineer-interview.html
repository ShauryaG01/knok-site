01 Overview

Overview

Urban Harvest builds technology for the food and agriculture supply chain, connecting farmers, distributors, and retailers through digital platforms. Their engineering team works on real-time inventory systems, marketplace features, and data pipelines that handle perishable goods logistics.

As of July 2026, Urban Harvest had 5 open Software Engineer roles, part of a broader market of 5,395 Software Engineer openings tracked across India. The team values engineers who think about product impact alongside code quality. Expect questions that blend technical depth with domain curiosity about food supply chains and sustainable agriculture.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

The questions below are ones candidates report most often in Urban Harvest Software Engineer interviews. Technical rounds focus on data structures, system design, and debugging. Later rounds lean into behaviour and domain thinking.

  1. Walk me through a complex system you designed from scratch and the trade-offs you made.
  2. How would you design a real-time inventory tracking system for perishable goods with short shelf lives?
  3. Tell me about a time you optimised a slow database query or API endpoint under production pressure.

  5. Describe a situation where you disagreed with a technical decision your team made. What did you do?

  9. How do you make sure the APIs you build stay reliable for third-party logistics and delivery partners?
  10. Tell me about a time you improved your team's engineering practices or helped a junior developer grow.
  11. How would you design a search and filter feature for a marketplace listing thousands of perishable SKUs?
  12. What draws you to the intersection of technology and food or agriculture?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Three sample answers using the STAR format. Adapt these to your own experience.

Q: Tell me about a time you optimised a slow query under production pressure.

*Situation:* Our order management service ran a query joining four tables to calculate daily fulfilment rates. During morning peak hours it was timing out for a meaningful share of requests.

*Task:* I needed to fix the bottleneck without taking the service down or deploying a risky schema change during business hours.

*Action:* I ran EXPLAIN ANALYZE and found a missing composite index. I added it on a read replica first to validate the improvement, then applied it to the primary with zero downtime. I also added a short-lived cache layer for the daily aggregate so the heavy query ran less frequently.

*Result:* Query latency dropped significantly and timeout errors went to zero during the next morning peak. The on-call alert never fired again for that issue.

---

Q: Give an example of pivoting mid-sprint without losing quality.

*Situation:* Three days into a two-week sprint, product told us a retail partner needed a bulk-upload CSV feature within the week or they would delay their onboarding.

*Task:* I had to re-scope the sprint, protect work already in code review, and deliver the new feature without cutting corners on validation.

*Action:* I mapped what was already in progress and moved two tasks with no external dependency to the next sprint. I wrote the CSV parser with clear per-row error messages so the partner could self-serve on bad data. I kept test coverage at the same standard the team always uses.

*Result:* The feature shipped in four days. The partner onboarded on schedule, and the deferred tasks made it into the next sprint with no issues.

---

Q: Describe a time you disagreed with a technical decision.

*Situation:* My team wanted to call a third-party logistics API directly from our order service. I believed that would make swapping providers very painful later.

*Task:* I needed to make the case for an abstraction layer without being seen as over-engineering under a deadline.

*Action:* I wrote a one-page design doc showing that a thin adapter interface would take one extra day and would let us run A/B tests between logistics providers. I focused on business risk rather than technical purity.

*Result:* The team agreed to the adapter. Several months later we switched our cold-chain provider and the change was straightforward, where a direct integration would have touched dozens of files.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Three frameworks cover most question types at Urban Harvest.

STAR for behavioural questions (Situation, Task, Action, Result): Keep Situation and Task brief, one to two sentences each. Spend most of your answer on Action. Always close with a concrete Result, even an imperfect one.

Trade-off thinking for system design: Urban Harvest deals with perishable inventory, so interviewers look for awareness of consistency vs. availability trade-offs, data freshness requirements, and failure modes. For every design choice, state what you gain and what you give up. Acknowledge what you would revisit with more time.

Situation-Impact-Fix for debugging questions: Describe the situation, quantify the impact (users affected, error rate, latency spike), then walk through your diagnostic steps in order. Interviewers want to see a methodical process, not just the final answer.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Domain curiosity: Urban Harvest solves real problems in food and agriculture. Interviewers respond well to candidates who have thought about why these problems are hard, not just candidates who write clean code.

Product thinking: Expect follow-up questions like 'what would you measure to know this is working?' or 'how would a farmer on a slow mobile connection experience this feature?' Thinking beyond the ticket is valued here.

Engineering fundamentals: Candidates report that system design rounds focus on databases, message queues, and API contracts relevant to inventory and logistics. Review indexing strategies, eventual consistency, and idempotent API design.

Collaborative communication: Urban Harvest has cross-functional teams. Interviewers look for engineers who can explain trade-offs to non-technical stakeholders and who push back respectfully when they see a better path.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused two-week plan based on what candidates have found effective.

Week 1: Technical foundations

  1. Revise core data structures and algorithms. Focus on arrays, hash maps, trees, and graphs, as these come up most in coding rounds.
  2. Practice system design problems daily. Prioritise inventory systems, real-time notifications, and marketplace search, as these map directly to Urban Harvest's products.
  3. Review SQL optimisation: indexes, query plans, and joins on large tables.

Week 2: Company context and behaviour prep

  1. Read through Urban Harvest's publicly available product updates and any engineering blog posts. Understand how their platform connects farmers to retailers.
  2. Write out three to five STAR stories from your own experience covering ownership, disagreement, and a technical failure you recovered from.
  3. Do a mock interview focused on the behavioural questions listed above, ideally with a peer who gives honest feedback.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping straight to a solution in system design: Urban Harvest interviewers typically want you to clarify requirements and state assumptions before drawing any architecture. Skipping this step signals that you do not think before you build.

Ignoring domain constraints: A generic e-commerce inventory design misses the point. Perishable goods have expiry windows, seasonal demand swings, and cold-chain logistics requirements. Showing awareness of these constraints sets strong candidates apart.

Vague STAR answers: Saying 'I improved performance by a lot' does not land. Use specific numbers from your own experience. If you cannot share exact figures, say 'the team measured a clear improvement, though I cannot share the exact metrics publicly.'

Not asking questions: Candidates who never ask clarifying questions are seen as either overconfident or disengaged. Prepare two or three questions about the team's current technical challenges or how success is measured in the role.

Underselling collaboration: Urban Harvest is a product-driven company. Focusing only on your individual code and skipping the cross-team coordination makes you look like a solo engineer in a team-first culture.
