01 Overview

Overview

Moveworks builds an enterprise AI platform that automates IT, HR, and finance support using large language models and semantic search. The ML Engineer role sits at the core of this product, working on NLP pipelines, retrieval systems, intent classification, and production ML systems that serve enterprise employees at scale.

As of July 2026, Moveworks has 6 open ML Engineer roles. Candidates report a process that typically spans multiple rounds: a recruiter screen, a coding or take-home problem, one or more deep technical ML interviews, and a final round with senior engineers or leadership. Expect detailed questions on NLP, LLMs, retrieval-augmented generation, and production ML challenges.

The work is applied, not research-focused. Interviewers want to see how you think through real enterprise problems, such as routing employee IT requests, extracting entities from support tickets, and building feedback loops for continuously improving models.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time you improved an NLP model's performance in a production system.

*Situation:* The intent classification model behind our customer support chatbot started declining in accuracy several months after launch, as user query patterns shifted from what the training data had covered.

*Task:* I owned diagnosing the drop and restoring performance without triggering a full retrain, which would have taken weeks.

*Action:* I analysed production logs to find which intent categories had the most errors. A few new intent types had emerged organically but were absent from the original label set. I added them using weak labelling and active learning, then fine-tuned only the classification head rather than the full model. I also set up an automated distribution-drift monitor to alert the team early on any future shift.

*Result:* Accuracy on the affected categories recovered. The targeted fine-tune took just a couple of days rather than weeks, and the drift monitor caught a similar shift a few months later, cutting our response time to the problem significantly.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you worked with noisy or limited labelled data.

*Situation:* I joined a document-routing project mid-way. The training set had been labelled by non-experts, and our team's initial audit flagged a label error rate that was concerningly high.

*Task:* I needed to clean the dataset and retrain without waiting months for expert re-labelling of every sample.

*Action:* I applied a confidence-learning approach (similar to the cleanlab method) to surface likely mislabelled examples. Instead of reviewing everything, I focused expert annotation on only the high-uncertainty samples, cutting the review burden to a small fraction of the original dataset. I also added cross-validation filtering to drop samples where multiple model snapshots all disagreed with the given label.

*Result:* The cleaned dataset was smaller but substantially higher quality. The retrained model's validation accuracy improved noticeably, production precision on the routing task increased, and this two-step cleaning process became our standard approach for future labelling work.

---

Q: Describe a project where you built an NLP pipeline for a new domain from scratch.

*Situation:* My team was tasked with building a knowledge-retrieval system for a finance operations team. There was no prior training data and no existing NLP tooling in the organisation for this domain.

*Task:* I led the full pipeline design, from raw document ingestion to a query interface that finance staff could actually use day to day.

*Action:* I chose a retrieval-augmented generation approach: chunked and embedded documents using a sentence transformer model, stored vectors in a FAISS index, and used a hosted LLM to generate answers grounded in retrieved passages. I built a small evaluation harness using gold question-answer pairs that domain experts created, allowing us to track retrieval recall and answer quality separately. I iterated on chunk size and overlap settings based on this eval harness.

*Result:* The system went live within a few weeks. Finance staff reported spending far less time hunting for policy documents, and retrieval recall on our gold eval set was strong enough that the product team asked us to expand the system to two more business units.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For system design and architecture questions (such as 'design a semantic search system'), use a four-part structure. First, clarify constraints: latency budget, data volume, and freshness requirements. Second, sketch the data pipeline before choosing models. Third, explain your approach and the trade-offs clearly, for example RAG vs fine-tuning, or sparse vs dense retrieval. Fourth, describe how you would evaluate and monitor the system in production. Moveworks interviewers particularly value candidates who think about failure modes and feedback loops, not just initial accuracy.

For debugging and production questions, walk through your reasoning in layers: data layer first (is the input distribution different from training?), then model layer (is there a specific category or query type that fails?), then infrastructure (is there a latency or batching issue?). Show that you separate offline evaluation from online production metrics.

For behavioral questions, use the STAR structure: Situation (brief context), Task (what you were responsible for), Action (the specific steps you took, be precise), and Result (a concrete outcome). Moveworks interviews tend to focus on collaboration, handling ambiguity, and making decisions with incomplete information. Prepare examples where you made trade-offs rather than simply executing a clear plan.

For trade-off questions (RAG vs fine-tuning, precision vs recall, latency vs accuracy), avoid picking a side immediately. State the conditions under which each option is better, then give a recommendation based on the specific scenario in the question. This signals senior-level thinking and shows you understand that context determines architecture.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Moveworks interviews are technically demanding and focused on applied NLP and production ML. Based on what candidates report, interviewers are looking for the following:

Deep NLP and LLM fluency. You should be comfortable with transformer architectures, embeddings, semantic similarity, and the practical differences between retrieval-based and generative approaches. Be ready to reason about when fine-tuning is better than RAG, and why.

Production ML instincts. Moveworks ships AI to enterprise customers at scale, so interviewers want evidence you have thought seriously about latency, reliability, monitoring, and model degradation, not just benchmark accuracy. Examples from real deployments carry more weight than academic projects.

Enterprise domain awareness. Moveworks' core use cases involve IT ticketing, HR queries, and knowledge retrieval. Candidates who have worked on intent classification, entity extraction, conversational AI, or internal knowledge systems will find it easier to connect their experience to what the role requires.

Clear communication. ML at Moveworks involves working with product managers and enterprise customers, not only other ML engineers. Interviewers look for candidates who can explain a technical decision in plain terms without losing accuracy.

Ownership and curiosity. Candidates who proactively ask about data quality, edge cases, and real-world constraints during design questions signal that they think like engineers who own outcomes, not just deliverables.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Understand the Moveworks product and stack
Read Moveworks' engineering blog posts and any publicly available research they have published on enterprise NLP and conversational AI. Understand how their platform classifies intents, retrieves knowledge, and generates responses. Refresh your understanding of transformer models, sentence embeddings, FAISS, and RAG architectures.

Week 2: System design practice
Practice designing at least three ML systems from scratch: a semantic search engine, an intent classification system for a chatbot, and an entity extraction pipeline. For each, be ready to explain your data pipeline, model choice, evaluation strategy, and production monitoring approach. Writing these out longhand helps more than just thinking through them.

Week 3: Coding and ML fundamentals
Brush up on applied ML coding: implementing attention mechanisms, writing a fine-tuning loop in PyTorch, and building data preprocessing pipelines. Candidates report that Moveworks coding problems are applied rather than purely algorithmic, so focus on ML-flavoured problems rather than pure competitive programming.

Week 4: Behavioral and communication prep
Prepare five to six STAR stories covering: improving a model in production, handling bad or limited data, making a technical trade-off under time pressure, working with non-technical stakeholders, and a project you led end to end. Practice them aloud until they sound natural.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping the reasoning behind design choices. Candidates often name a correct approach without explaining why it fits the specific problem. Moveworks interviewers want to hear your reasoning, not just your conclusion.

Treating production as an afterthought. Describing a model architecture without mentioning monitoring, latency, or failure handling signals that your experience may be mostly experimental. Weave production thinking into every design answer.

Focusing only on accuracy metrics. In enterprise AI, user trust, latency, and explainability often matter as much as model accuracy. Candidates who only discuss F1 and AUC miss the full picture.

Giving vague STAR answers. Stories like 'I improved accuracy by retraining the model' lack the specificity interviewers need. Be clear about the exact problem, what you did, and what changed as a result.

Not asking clarifying questions during design rounds. Jumping straight into a solution without confirming constraints (latency, data volume, label availability) is a common signal of junior-level thinking. Take a moment to ask a few scoped questions before designing.

Underestimating the NLP depth required. This is not a generalist ML role. Candidates who are strong in tabular ML or computer vision but thin on NLP, embeddings, and conversational AI typically struggle in the technical rounds.
