01 Overview

Overview

Zenoti is a cloud-based SaaS platform built for the wellness, beauty, and fitness industry. Salon chains, spa groups, and fitness studios use it for scheduling, payments, inventory, point-of-sale, and analytics across multiple locations. This makes it a genuine enterprise B2B product, which means PM interviews here test a different skill set than consumer-app roles.

Zenoti currently has 52 open roles on knok as of July 2026. The broader India PM market shows 2,009 active openings, with Bangalore leading at 271 and Delhi close behind at 177. Candidates report that Zenoti's interview process typically runs 3-5 rounds: a recruiter or HR screen, a hiring manager conversation, one or two rounds with senior PMs or cross-functional leaders, and sometimes a product case or take-home assignment. The structure varies by team and level, so confirm the process with your recruiter early.

Salary bands in the India PM market, based on knok's job radar data: Associate PM roles sit at 12-20 LPA, mid-level PMs (3-6 years of experience) at 24-40 LPA, Senior PMs at 40-60 LPA, and Group or Principal PMs at 55-90+ LPA.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions reflect what candidates report from Zenoti PM rounds, combining product thinking, domain context, and execution skills.

  1. Walk me through a product you owned end-to-end. What was your north star metric, and how did it evolve over time?
  2. Zenoti serves multi-location wellness businesses. How do you balance the needs of a large franchise operator versus a single-location owner on the same platform?
  3. How would you prioritise a feature backlog when your top two enterprise clients have directly conflicting requests?
  4. Design a dashboard for a spa chain owner who wants to track performance across multiple locations. What metrics would you surface, and why those?
  5. After a recent release, appointment booking completions have dropped for several clients. Walk me through how you investigate and respond.
  6. How would you improve Zenoti's customer retention for mid-market wellness businesses that are scaling from one to five locations?
  7. Tell me about a time you said no to a feature request from an important customer. How did you handle the conversation, and what happened?
  8. Sales has promised a custom reporting feature to close a large deal. How do you handle it as the PM?
  9. What does a good product discovery process look like when your end users are salon managers and staff, not tech-savvy consumers?
  10. Describe a time you used data to change a strongly held opinion on your team or with a stakeholder.
  11. How would you approach adding an AI-powered recommendation feature to a wellness platform, given uneven data quality across clients?
  12. Zenoti operates across markets in India, the US, and Southeast Asia. How do you think about localisation and market-specific product decisions?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format for behavioural questions: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Here are three sample answers built for common Zenoti question types.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you said no to a feature request from an important customer.

*Situation:* At my previous company, a large enterprise client asked us to build a fully custom reporting module that would take the team roughly two sprints. It was not on our roadmap, and two other clients had requested conflicting versions of the same feature.

*Task:* I needed to handle the request in a way that kept the client relationship intact while protecting the team's roadmap and avoiding a precedent for custom builds.

*Action:* I first explored what outcome the client actually needed. It turned out they wanted to export data for their finance team, not a full custom UI. I worked with engineering to scope a lightweight CSV export with configurable columns, which served their actual need and was reusable for other clients. I went back to the customer with this solution, walked them through the tradeoffs, and committed to a clear timeline.

*Result:* The client accepted the solution and renewed their contract. The feature shipped in one sprint instead of two, and other clients adopted it at launch. The lesson was that 'no to the specific request' does not mean 'no to the underlying need.'

---

Q: How would you prioritise a backlog when two enterprise clients have conflicting requests?

*Situation:* While managing a scheduling product, two large clients escalated feature requests in the same quarter. One wanted bulk-rescheduling tools for franchise managers. The other wanted a self-service rescheduling flow for their end customers.

*Task:* I had to allocate limited engineering capacity in a way that kept both accounts healthy and aligned with our broader platform strategy.

*Action:* I scored each request against our strategy pillars: operational efficiency and client retention. I used a simple scoring model weighing revenue impact, the breadth of accounts that would benefit, and engineering effort. I also spoke directly with both clients to understand urgency and the specific pain behind each request. The self-service flow served a wider set of clients and aligned more strongly with retention goals, so it got prioritised first. I gave the second client a transparent explanation and committed to their feature in the following quarter.

*Result:* Neither client churned. The self-service feature launched on time and was adopted by several other accounts. The second client's feature shipped the next quarter as committed, which strengthened that relationship.

---

Q: Describe a time you used data to change a strongly held opinion on your team.

*Situation:* My engineering lead was confident that a new onboarding flow was working well because support ticket volume had dropped after the redesign.

*Task:* I suspected the drop in tickets meant users were giving up before reaching the point where they would raise a ticket, not that the flow had become clearer.

*Action:* I pulled funnel data for the onboarding flow and found that completion rates had actually declined after the redesign. I also ran a short usability session with three new users and recorded where they dropped off. I presented both the funnel data and the session recordings side by side so the team could see the 'what' and the 'why' together.

*Result:* The team agreed to revisit the onboarding design. We simplified two key steps based on the usability findings, and completion rates improved in the next release. The experience reinforced the value of pairing quantitative data with direct user observation.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

A few frameworks that work well for Zenoti's interview style.

For product design and feature questions: Start with the user and their goal, not the feature. A useful structure: Who is the user, what is their job to be done, what is the biggest pain today, what does a good solution look like, and how do you measure success. For a B2B platform like Zenoti, always distinguish between the buyer (the business owner), the primary user (the manager or staff member), and the end consumer (the person booking an appointment). Mixing these up is a fast way to give an unfocused answer.

For prioritisation questions: The RICE framework (Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort) works well and is easy to explain out loud. For enterprise SaaS, also factor in strategic fit: does this help retain large accounts, expand to new segments, or deepen platform stickiness? Be ready to defend your scoring with specifics.

For metrics and analytics questions: Lead with your north star metric, then break it into input metrics. For a wellness platform like Zenoti, relevant metrics include booking completion rate, no-show rate, staff utilisation, client retention rate, and upsell attachment. Tie every metric to a business outcome rather than treating it as an end in itself.

For 'how would you improve X' questions: A clean structure is: understand the current state and goals, identify the biggest user pain, generate a few solution directions, evaluate tradeoffs, and recommend one with a clear success metric. Do not jump to solutions before diagnosing the problem.

For stakeholder and influence questions: Describe the stakeholder dynamic, explain what approach you chose and why, then share the outcome. Zenoti interviewers are particularly interested in how you handle sales-driven feature pressure and multi-client prioritisation conflicts.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Zenoti builds software for operators who run businesses, not casual consumers. Interviewers typically look for a few specific things.

Domain empathy without domain obsession. You do not need a background in wellness or beauty, but you do need to quickly understand the life of a salon manager: staff scheduling, walk-ins, no-shows, upselling, loyalty programs, and multi-location reporting. Show that you can learn a domain fast and ask the right questions about it.

Enterprise product instincts. Consumer PM habits (growth hacks, viral loops, B2C funnels) are less relevant here. Interviewers want to see that you understand contract renewals, account health, onboarding complexity, and the significance of a single enterprise client to the overall business.

Metrics clarity. Be precise about what you measure and why. Vague answers like 'I would track engagement' will not land well. Know the difference between a leading and a lagging indicator, and be ready to define success metrics before jumping to solutions.

Cross-functional confidence. Zenoti PMs work with engineering, design, sales, customer success, and implementation teams. Candidates report that interviewers probe for how you handle conflicting priorities across these teams and how you hold a position while remaining open to new evidence.

Execution credibility. Strategy without delivery does not impress here. Be ready to talk about how you manage sprints, handle scope creep, communicate when timelines shift, and keep cross-functional partners aligned through the full delivery cycle.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused preparation approach for Zenoti PM interviews.

Know the product and the domain. Sign up for a Zenoti trial or watch product demo videos on their website. Map the core user journeys: a client booking an appointment, a manager viewing daily reports, an owner tracking multi-location performance. Read reviews on G2 and Capterra to understand real customer pain points and complaints. Look at recent news about the wellness SaaS market to understand the competitive context Zenoti operates in.

Build your story bank. Write out a strong set of work stories using the STAR format. Cover: a prioritisation decision under pressure, a time you said no to a customer, a metrics-driven product decision, a stakeholder conflict you navigated, a launch that did not go as planned, and a cross-functional win. Zenoti interviews for mid-senior PM roles lean heavily on behavioural questions.

Practise product cases. Work through enterprise SaaS scenarios out loud: designing a new analytics view, building an onboarding flow for a multi-location operator, or adding an AI feature for staff scheduling. Time yourself. Practise thinking aloud rather than arriving silently at a final answer, since interviewers want to follow your reasoning.

Prepare for the conversation. Research Zenoti's recent product announcements and any publicly reported expansions or partnerships. Prepare two or three specific questions for each interviewer that show you have done your homework. Candidates report that Zenoti interviewers appreciate genuine curiosity about the product roadmap and team structure.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

These are the patterns that typically cost candidates an offer at Zenoti PM interviews.

Jumping to solutions before understanding the problem. Interviewers consistently note candidates who start designing features before asking who the user is and what they actually need. Always diagnose before prescribing.

Using only consumer-app examples. If your entire story bank draws on B2C products and you cannot translate those learnings to an enterprise context, it signals a potential mismatch. Prepare at least a few examples from B2B, SaaS, or platform products, even if they come from smaller or earlier roles.

Vague metrics. Saying 'I would track user satisfaction' without defining how to measure it or what would change your priorities is a red flag. Know your metrics, know what moves them, and know how you would instrument and monitor them.

Ignoring the business buyer. In Zenoti's world, the person using the product daily (a receptionist or manager) is not always the person who renews the contract (the business owner). Missing this distinction in a product design question is a common and avoidable mistake.

Not asking clarifying questions on case prompts. Candidates report that interviewers deliberately leave case prompts open-ended to see if you scope appropriately. Always clarify the goal, the user, and any constraints before diving into your answer.

Underestimating implementation complexity. Zenoti integrates with payment processors, hardware terminals, and third-party booking tools across multiple markets. A solution that ignores these integration realities, even in a hypothetical case, will not land well with technical interviewers.
