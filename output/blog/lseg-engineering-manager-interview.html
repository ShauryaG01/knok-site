01 Overview

Overview

LSEG (London Stock Exchange Group) is one of the world's leading financial markets infrastructure companies, operating trading, data, and risk platforms that banks and asset managers depend on globally. After completing the Refinitiv acquisition, LSEG significantly grew its India engineering footprint, and Bangalore became its largest India technology centre. As of mid-2026, LSEG has 129 open roles tracked in India, reflecting sustained engineering investment.

The Engineering Manager role at LSEG is a genuine people-plus-delivery leadership position. You will own a team of engineers working on high-availability financial systems, manage delivery against business commitments, and work closely with product and business stakeholders across geographies (typically London, New York, and India). LSEG's products span real-time market data feeds, trading platforms, post-trade and clearing systems, and data analytics tools. Because your team's output directly touches regulated markets, reliability and compliance awareness matter as much as delivery pace.

The interview process typically spans multiple rounds. Candidates report a mix of behavioural leadership discussions, a technical or system design conversation, and a senior stakeholder round. There is no single fixed format across teams, so confirm the structure with your recruiter and prepare for depth in all three areas.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for all behavioural questions. Here are three worked examples tailored to LSEG's context.

---

Q: Tell me about a time your team managed a major production incident on a critical system.

*Situation:* Our team owned a real-time data pipeline feeding pricing information to internal trading desks. One morning, a bad deployment caused a latency spike that triggered alerts across downstream systems.

*Task:* As EM, I needed to coordinate a fast resolution while keeping senior stakeholders informed, and ensure the same failure mode could not recur.

*Action:* I pulled together the on-call engineer, the SRE lead, and the product manager immediately. I set up a short recurring standup cadence and took personal ownership of stakeholder communication so my engineers could focus entirely on the fix. We rolled back the deployment within the first hour. I then ran a blameless post-mortem the following day and pushed for automated canary deployments for that pipeline going forward.

*Result:* Service was fully restored within the hour. The post-mortem produced three process changes the team still references. We had no repeat of that failure class in the months that followed.

---

Q: Describe how you handled conflicting priorities from two senior stakeholders.

*Situation:* My team had two senior product owners, one from a trading platform group and one from a data products group, both claiming their feature as the top priority for the quarter. My team could realistically deliver one well, not both.

*Task:* I needed to make a recommendation both stakeholders could accept without damaging either relationship or the team's delivery credibility.

*Action:* I asked each stakeholder to quantify the business case behind their request, framed as revenue risk or user impact. I then brought both into a joint session with my engineering lead, presented the capacity reality clearly, and walked through what full delivery on each option would actually look like. I proposed sequencing: the trading platform feature first (it had a harder regulatory deadline), with a committed start date for the data products work.

*Result:* Both stakeholders agreed to the plan. The trading platform feature shipped on time. The data products work kicked off as promised. My relationship with both teams actually strengthened because I had been transparent about trade-offs early rather than overpromising.

---

Q: Tell me about an underperforming engineer and how you handled it.

*Situation:* A senior engineer on my team was consistently missing deadlines and their code reviews were becoming a bottleneck. Other team members were starting to notice and quietly picking up the slack.

*Task:* I needed to address the performance issue fairly and quickly, while protecting overall team morale.

*Action:* I started with a one-on-one conversation to understand what was actually going on. The engineer had unclear expectations about their scope and felt isolated on a module they were new to. I clarified their deliverables in writing, paired them with another senior engineer for two sprints, and set up weekly check-ins. I was direct that the current delivery pace needed to change, and I documented our conversations.

*Result:* Within six weeks, delivery improved noticeably. The engineer later told me it was the clearest feedback they had received in their career. They went on to lead a module independently the following quarter.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For behavioural questions, use STAR tightly. LSEG interviewers typically move on quickly from long answers, so practise keeping each STAR element to two or three sentences. The Result element matters most. Do not rush it.

For 'how do you approach X' questions, use a Situation-Approach-Principle structure. Briefly describe a real scenario, explain your approach in that context, and then name the underlying principle ('I generally believe that...'). This shows both experience and self-awareness, two things LSEG interviewers value.

For technical or system design questions, use a Scope-Constraints-Trade-offs structure. Start by clarifying what the system needs to do (scope), name the constraints (latency, compliance requirements, team size), and then walk through trade-offs explicitly. LSEG systems often have strict availability and data-integrity requirements, so naming those constraints early signals that you understand the domain.

For stakeholder conflict questions, use an Interests-not-Positions frame. Do not describe the conflict as 'Stakeholder A wanted X, Stakeholder B wanted Y.' Instead, describe what each stakeholder was actually trying to achieve (their underlying business interest) and how you found a path that addressed both. Candidates report this framing resonates well with LSEG interviewers.

Quantify where you can, and be honest where you cannot. LSEG interviewers value precision. If you know an exact figure from your own experience (team size, time to recovery, uptime improvement), use it. If you are estimating, say so: 'roughly' or 'approximately.' Vague claims like 'significantly improved performance' without any supporting context weaken your answer.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

LSEG Engineering Manager interviewers are typically looking for five things, based on what candidates report from their rounds.

Technical credibility without arrogance. You do not need to write production code day-to-day, but you must be able to read architecture diagrams, challenge technical decisions intelligently, and recognise when a system design is cutting corners on reliability for financial markets. Interviewers will probe whether you can hold your own in a design conversation.

Genuine people development instincts. LSEG, like most large technology organisations, competes hard for engineering talent and values managers who actively grow engineers rather than just assign tasks. Expect questions about how you have developed engineers into senior roles, how you handle underperformers, and how you build psychological safety on a team.

Stakeholder management across geographies. LSEG's India engineering teams work closely with business stakeholders in London and New York. The ability to communicate clearly across cultures and time zones, and to manage expectations without over-promising, is a recurring theme in interview feedback.

Delivery discipline in a regulated environment. Financial markets infrastructure has very low tolerance for data errors, outages during trading hours, or compliance gaps. Interviewers want evidence that you understand this operating context, that you build teams with strong operational practices, and that you take ownership when things go wrong.

Self-awareness and a growth mindset. LSEG has gone through significant organisational change following its major acquisitions and restructuring. They want managers who can reflect honestly on their own failures and show how they updated their approach as a result. Interviewers are often put off by candidates who only discuss successes.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused four-week prep plan for the LSEG Engineering Manager interview.

Week 1: Know the company and the role. Read LSEG's recent product announcements and understand what Refinitiv brought to the group (data, analytics, trading tools) and how India engineering centres fit into the global picture. Map your own experience to the systems LSEG runs: real-time data, clearing, post-trade processing, analytics. Write down three or four areas where your background directly overlaps.

Week 2: Build your story bank. Prepare at least eight STAR stories covering the most asked question categories: incident management, stakeholder conflict, underperforming engineer, technical debt decision, hiring, and organisational change. For each story, make sure you know the Result clearly, including any concrete figures from your own work that you can honestly use.

Week 3: Practise out loud. Record yourself answering five questions. Watch the playback. Most engineering managers talk too long on Situation and rush Result. Aim to keep each behavioural answer under three minutes. Ask a peer or mentor to run a mock interview covering at least two of the harder questions.

Week 4: Targeted research and logistics. Look at what LSEG engineering teams have published publicly: tech blogs, open-source contributions, conference talks. Prepare three or four sharp questions for your interviewers that show you understand the business and have thought about the role's specific challenges. Confirm the interview format with your recruiter before the final round, as process details vary across LSEG's India centres.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating the EM role as a senior engineer role. Many candidates spend most of their answers describing technical decisions they made personally, rather than how they enabled their team to make better decisions. LSEG interviewers are hiring a people leader, not a lead engineer.

Generic leadership language. Saying 'I foster a culture of collaboration' without a concrete example is a red flag. Every claim needs a specific story behind it. LSEG interviewers have heard the management clichés and move past them quickly.

Not knowing LSEG's business. Candidates who only know LSEG as 'a stock exchange' come across as uninterested. LSEG is a data and markets infrastructure company. Knowing the difference between their clearing business, their data products, and their trading platforms signals genuine interest in the role.

Underselling the Result. Many candidates remember the Situation well and the Action in detail, but trail off on Results with 'and it went well.' Practise stating the concrete outcome first, then backing it up with specifics from your own experience.

Over-claiming regulated domain experience. If you have not worked in a regulated financial environment, say so honestly and pivot to parallels: high-availability systems, strict data pipelines, audit trails. Interviewers can tell when someone is bluffing about compliance knowledge, and honesty builds more trust than a rehearsed claim.

Arriving with no questions. LSEG interviewers consistently report that candidates who ask nothing at the end seem unengaged. Prepare at least three genuine questions about the team structure, the product roadmap, or the engineering challenges ahead.
