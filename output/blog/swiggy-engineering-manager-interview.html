01 Overview

Overview

Swiggy operates at a scale few Indian tech companies match, handling food and quick-commerce orders across hundreds of cities. That scale shapes what the company looks for in Engineering Managers: leaders who keep systems reliable under pressure, grow engineers fast, and work across product, data, and operations without creating bottlenecks.

As of July 2026, Swiggy has 26 open Engineering Manager roles across its engineering org. The broader EM market in India shows 975 active openings tracked by knok jobradar, with Bangalore leading at 182 positions, followed by Delhi at 53, and Pune and Chennai at 20 each.

Salary bands for EM roles in India, as commonly cited on Glassdoor and levels.fyi: Manager roles typically fall in the 35-60 LPA range, Senior Manager in 55-90 LPA, and Director-level positions at 90-150+ LPA.

Candidates report that Swiggy's interview process typically spans multiple rounds covering system design, people management scenarios, cross-functional leadership, and a final discussion with senior leadership. The company moves fast, and interviewers consistently probe for how you handle ambiguity, build accountability, and deliver outcomes under pressure.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly in Swiggy EM interviews, based on what candidates report. Prepare a concrete story for each one before your first call.

  1. Tell us about a time your team faced a high-severity production incident. What was your role, and how did you lead the recovery?
  2. Swiggy handles massive demand spikes on match days, festive sales, and rain surges. How have you prepared your team for sudden load or capacity challenges?
  3. Describe a time you had to push back on a product or business request. How did you handle the conversation, and what was the outcome?
  4. How do you set and track goals for engineers on your team? How do you connect individual goals to business outcomes?
  5. Tell us about a time you hired the wrong person. What went wrong, and what did you change in your hiring process after?
  6. How do you handle an engineer who is technically excellent but creates friction in the team or consistently misses commitments?
  7. Describe a significant technical decision you fully delegated to your team. What was your role, and what did you learn from the outcome?
  8. How do you balance shipping new features with reducing technical debt when both feel equally urgent?
  9. Give an example of shipping something quickly without sacrificing quality. How did you manage the trade-offs?
  10. How do you build an environment where engineers feel safe raising problems early, before they become incidents?
  11. Tell us about a cross-functional project you led. How did you align engineering, product, and operations stakeholders?
  12. Walk us through how you would approach your first 90 days as an EM at Swiggy.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell us about a time your team faced a high-severity production incident.

*Situation:* Our payments service went down during a peak evening slot. Orders were failing silently for a portion of users, and the on-call engineer was new to the role.

*Task:* I needed to coordinate a fast recovery, keep business stakeholders informed, and make sure we learned from the incident without burning the team out.

*Action:* I joined the incident channel immediately, assigned a dedicated incident commander from the experienced engineers, and set a regular update cadence for stakeholders. I kept the new engineer involved but paired them with a senior colleague so they could learn without pressure. Once the fix was deployed, I scheduled a blameless post-mortem within two days.

*Result:* We restored service in under an hour. The post-mortem produced three concrete action items, two of which shipped in the next sprint. The new engineer gained confidence and led the next incident themselves.

---

Q: How do you handle an engineer who is technically excellent but creates friction in the team?

*Situation:* A senior engineer on my team consistently delivered strong code but dismissed others' ideas in code reviews, which discouraged junior engineers from speaking up.

*Task:* I needed to address the behaviour without losing a high performer or creating resentment.

*Action:* I had a direct one-on-one conversation using specific examples rather than general feedback. I framed it as: 'your technical bar is exactly what we need, and your influence grows when junior engineers feel safe bringing ideas to you.' We agreed on a concrete behaviour change: ask a clarifying question before pushing back in any review. I followed up in subsequent one-on-ones to check whether it was sticking.

*Result:* The behaviour improved noticeably over two sprints. Two junior engineers who had been quiet started contributing actively in design discussions.

---

Q: Tell us about a cross-functional project you led.

*Situation:* We were rebuilding our order tracking feature, which involved engineering, product, customer support, and the logistics operations team, each with different priorities and timelines.

*Task:* I needed to deliver a working product on a tight deadline while keeping multiple stakeholders aligned and my engineers focused.

*Action:* I set up a weekly sync with one representative from each team and maintained a shared decision log with owners and open questions. When product and operations disagreed on notification frequency, I facilitated a structured trade-off discussion and pushed for a time-boxed experiment rather than a prolonged debate. I kept my engineers shielded from stakeholder noise so they could focus on building.

*Result:* We shipped on time. The experiment resolved the disagreement with real data. Customer support ticket volume for tracking queries dropped noticeably in the weeks that followed.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions. Structure every answer as Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task brief (two to three sentences each) and spend most of your time on Action and Result. Swiggy interviewers typically follow up with probing questions, so know your stories well enough to go deeper on any part.

'Compass' for people management questions. When asked about a difficult team member or performance issue, cover four points: what you observed (facts, not opinions), the conversation you had (direct and empathetic), the agreement you reached (concrete behaviour change), and the follow-up (did it stick). This shows you manage with both clarity and care, which Swiggy values.

DACI for cross-functional alignment questions. For stories involving multiple stakeholders, briefly identify who was the Driver, Approver, Contributor, and who was kept Informed. This signals that you understand how decisions get made at scale, which matters at a company where engineering, product, and operations are constantly interdependent.

'Zoom in, zoom out' for technical depth questions. Start at the system level (what was the business or architectural problem), then zoom into the specific technical decision, then zoom back out to the outcome and what you learned. This shows technical credibility without getting lost in implementation details, which is the right balance for an EM interview.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Technical credibility without micromanaging. Swiggy's engineering problems are genuinely hard: real-time logistics, payments at scale, surge pricing, multi-city fulfillment. Interviewers want to see that you understand these domains deeply enough to guide your team, but that you delegate decisions and trust engineers to own solutions.

Speed with quality. Swiggy moves fast. Every answer about trade-offs should show that you know how to ship quickly while maintaining reliability. Candidates who treat speed and quality as opposites tend to struggle in this process.

People development as a core output. Hiring bar, onboarding, career growth, and retention are treated as engineering deliverables at Swiggy, not HR tasks. Be ready to talk specifically about how you have grown engineers, not just shipped features.

Comfort with ambiguity. Swiggy's business environment changes quickly. Interviewers probe for how you make decisions when requirements are unclear, data is incomplete, or priorities shift mid-sprint. Show that ambiguity does not paralyse you.

Cross-functional maturity. EM roles at Swiggy sit at the intersection of product, data, and operations. Interviewers look for evidence that you can work across those boundaries without creating bottlenecks or turf conflicts.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know Swiggy deeply. Search for Swiggy's engineering blog and read their public posts on logistics, real-time tracking, and payments infrastructure. Study recent product announcements and understand the core business lines: food delivery, Instamart dark stores, and platform services. The more you understand their specific engineering challenges, the more your stories will connect with interviewers.

Week 2: Build your story bank. List several situations from your career covering: production incidents, difficult conversations, cross-functional projects, hiring decisions, and technical trade-offs. Write each one in STAR format and tag it so you know which questions it fits. Practice going deeper on any part of each story, because Swiggy interviewers probe.

Week 3: Practice out loud. Run mock interviews with a peer or mentor, not just written prep. Pay attention to whether your answers are crisp (two to three minutes each) and whether you lead with the outcome rather than burying it at the end. Record yourself once and watch it back.

Week 4: Prepare your questions. Candidates who ask sharp questions leave a stronger impression. Good questions for Swiggy: 'What does the team's biggest reliability challenge look like right now?', 'How do EMs here balance technical involvement with people work?', 'What does growth look like for an EM who is performing well?' Avoid questions that are easily answered on the careers page.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Talking only about what the team built, not how you led. EM interviews are not tech lead interviews. If your answer is mostly about the system design and barely mentions how you organised the team, coached engineers, or navigated disagreements, you will likely not advance. Interviewers listen specifically for what your contribution was as a manager, separate from what the team built.

Vague outcomes. 'The project was successful' and 'the team improved' do not land well. Anchor your results to something concrete and observable, even without precise numbers. 'We went from weekly escalations to one in three months' is more convincing than 'things got better.'

Not knowing Swiggy's business. Candidates who treat Swiggy as a generic tech company and give generic answers lose points quickly. If you understand that Swiggy runs Instamart dark stores or that their logistics engine handles real-time partner allocation, your stories will connect to what interviewers actually care about.

Avoiding conflict in your stories. Candidates sometimes only share situations where everything went smoothly. Swiggy interviewers want to see how you handle genuinely difficult situations: the engineer who pushed back, the stakeholder who disagreed, the deadline that slipped. Sanitised stories signal low self-awareness.

Underpreparing the 'why Swiggy' question. Interviewers at this level almost always ask why you want to join. 'Great company' or 'fast growth' is not enough. Be specific about the engineering scale, the logistics complexity, or the business domain that genuinely excites you. Interviewers can tell the difference.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Swiggy EM interview typically have?

Candidates report that the process typically involves multiple rounds, often including a recruiter or hiring manager screen, a technical or system design discussion, one or more behavioural and leadership rounds, and a final discussion with senior leadership. The exact structure can vary by team and level. Confirm the format with your recruiter after the first call so you can prepare accordingly.

Does Swiggy ask system design questions for Engineering Manager roles?

Yes, candidates commonly report at least one system design discussion, though the expectation differs from an IC interview. You are not expected to produce a detailed low-level design. Interviewers typically want to see that you can frame a problem, ask clarifying questions, make trade-off decisions, and explain how you would guide a team through a design, rather than solving it alone.

What salary can I expect for an Engineering Manager role at Swiggy?

Swiggy does not publicly disclose compensation bands, but Glassdoor and levels.fyi show Manager-level EM roles in India are commonly cited in the 35-60 LPA range, with Senior Manager roles in the 55-90 LPA range. Actual offers vary based on your experience, the specific team, and negotiation. Verify current numbers through community forums or direct conversations with Swiggy employees before your offer discussion.

How long does the Swiggy EM hiring process take from application to offer?

Candidates report timelines that vary widely. Some receive offers within a few weeks of the first round, while others report the process taking longer when interviewer schedules need to align across multiple rounds. Following up politely with your recruiter after each completed round is standard practice and rarely hurts your candidacy. Ask your recruiter for an expected timeline at the start of the process.

Is there a coding round for Engineering Manager roles at Swiggy?

Candidates typically do not report a traditional LeetCode-style coding test for EM roles at Swiggy. However, you should be comfortable reading code, discussing algorithms at a high level, and engaging in system design conversations. Some hiring managers may share a small design or debugging scenario to gauge technical instincts, so do not drop your technical preparation entirely.

How should I prepare for the 'why Swiggy' question?

Go beyond 'great company' or 'fast growth.' Research specific engineering challenges Swiggy has addressed publicly, such as real-time logistics, dark store operations, or payments infrastructure at scale. Connect those to your own experience or interests as an EM. Interviewers at this level respond well to candidates who can articulate what makes Swiggy's engineering problems unique and why that genuinely excites them.
