01 Overview

Overview

Druva is a cloud-native data protection and management company serving enterprise customers globally. Their ML team focuses on practical problems: ransomware detection in backup data, data classification by sensitivity, anomaly detection in metadata streams, and intelligent storage tiering.

As of July 2026, Druva has 28 open Machine Learning Engineer roles, making it an active recruiter in this space. Bangalore is the primary hiring hub, with strong ML hiring visible across major Indian cities.

Candidates typically go through 4-5 rounds: a recruiter screen, a coding or take-home round, an ML depth discussion, a system design round, and a final conversation with a hiring manager. The exact structure varies by team and level, so confirm the format with your recruiter early. The interview leans heavily on applied ML tied to Druva's product context, so expect questions about production systems, multi-tenant cloud constraints, and explaining model decisions to non-technical users.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up frequently in Druva ML Engineer interviews, based on candidate reports and the company's product focus.

  1. How would you design an anomaly detection system to flag ransomware activity in customer backup data?
  2. Walk through your approach to classifying data sensitivity (PII, confidential, general) across millions of enterprise files.
  3. How do you handle extreme class imbalance when malicious events are very rare in your training data?
  4. Describe the features you would engineer from backup metadata (file counts, sizes, access patterns) for a threat detection model.
  5. How do you monitor a production ML model? What signals tell you the model has started to degrade?
  6. Druva serves thousands of enterprise tenants on a shared cloud platform. How does multi-tenancy affect your ML design choices?
  7. A customer calls in upset because our system flagged their legitimate file migration as a ransomware attack. How do you explain this to them?
  8. Walk us through how you shipped an ML feature end-to-end. What surprised you during deployment or in production?
  9. How would you build a pipeline to detect unusual data access patterns across a large, distributed customer base in near real time?
  10. What trade-offs would you weigh between model accuracy and inference latency in a real-time threat detection system?
  11. How do you evaluate model quality when ground-truth labels are noisy, delayed, or partially missing?
  12. How would you design an A/B test for a new ML-powered alert feature without exposing enterprise customers to degraded protection?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How would you design an anomaly detection system to flag ransomware in backup data?

*Situation:* At my previous company, we ingested backup metadata (file counts, entropy scores, change rates) from customer endpoints every few minutes.

*Task:* I was asked to build a system that would flag unusual patterns, such as sudden spikes in file encryption or mass deletions, before they caused irreversible data loss.

*Action:* I started with a simple baseline using Isolation Forest on a rolling per-customer feature window. I engineered features around change rate, entropy delta, and file-type distribution shift. I created synthetic attack scenarios based on publicly reported ransomware behavior to generate weak labels, then layered a gradient-boosted classifier on top. I also added rule-based fast-path checks for the most obvious spikes to keep latency low for clear-cut cases.

*Result:* The system reduced mean detection time significantly in internal testing. False positive rate stayed within the agreed threshold after two tuning cycles, and the operations team adopted the alerts without needing a manual triage step first.

---

Q: How do you handle extreme class imbalance in a threat detection model?

*Situation:* In a security-adjacent ML project, the positive class (malicious events) was extremely rare compared to normal activity.

*Task:* I needed a model that caught real threats without overwhelming the operations team with false alerts.

*Action:* I used SMOTE to oversample the minority class during training and adjusted class weights in the loss function to penalize misses on the positive class more heavily. I switched the primary evaluation metric from accuracy to precision-recall AUC, which is more meaningful under heavy class skew. I set the decision threshold by picking the point on the precision-recall curve that matched the ops team's acceptable false alert rate per day.

*Result:* The model caught the target anomaly type with far fewer false positives than the previous rule-based system. The ops team adopted it without requiring a dedicated triage step, which was the key acceptance criterion.

---

Q: A customer is upset about a false positive alert. How do you explain it to them?

*Situation:* A customer called in worried that our system had flagged their planned large-scale file migration as a ransomware attack.

*Task:* My manager asked me to join the call and explain the situation in plain terms without making the customer feel like the product had failed them.

*Action:* I avoided all jargon and said something like: 'Your system moved a very large number of files very quickly, and that pattern looks identical to how ransomware behaves at the start of an attack. Our system is designed to be cautious and flag anything matching that pattern so your team can verify everything is safe.' I then walked them through the alert details and explained how they could mark planned maintenance windows to prevent similar alerts in future.

*Result:* The customer left the call reassured rather than frustrated. That conversation also led us to add a 'scheduled maintenance window' feature to the product backlog, which reduced this category of false positive for all customers.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For ML system design questions: Start by clarifying scale and constraints. How many customers, how much data per day, what latency is acceptable, and whether labels are available. Then walk through data ingestion, feature engineering, model selection with rationale, and monitoring post-launch. Druva interviewers value production thinking at least as much as modeling accuracy.

For ML concept questions: Use a three-part structure: the problem the technique solves, two or three candidate approaches, and the one you would pick given a specific constraint (speed, interpretability, label scarcity, explainability to non-technical users). Avoid listing techniques without connecting them to a trade-off.

For behavioral questions: Use STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result). Keep the Situation short. Spend most of your time on Action and Result. Quantify results wherever you honestly can. If you no longer have access to exact figures, be upfront: 'I do not have the precise number, but the ops team reported a clear reduction in escalations per week.'

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Druva interviewers typically look for four qualities in ML Engineer candidates.

Applied intuition over pure theory. They want to see you connect ML techniques to real product problems. Reciting textbook definitions without grounding them in a concrete use case is a common miss.

Production mindset. Can you reason about monitoring, retraining triggers, latency budgets, and what happens when a model silently drifts in production? A model that works in a notebook but breaks under real enterprise data is a genuine concern for a company whose customers depend on continuous uptime.

Domain awareness. You do not need prior data-protection experience, but showing you have read about Druva's products and can reason about enterprise security at cloud scale signals genuine interest and preparation.

Clear communication. ML decisions often need to reach product managers, security analysts, or enterprise customers. Interviewers test whether you can translate technical trade-offs into plain language under pressure.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Product and domain context. Read Druva's public blog and product pages to understand their backup, disaster recovery, and threat detection products. Identify where ML fits in their stack and what customer problems they are solving.

Weeks 1-2: Core ML revision. Review anomaly detection (Isolation Forest, autoencoders, statistical baselines), classification under class imbalance, time-series feature engineering, and evaluation metrics beyond accuracy (F1, PR-AUC, ROC-AUC). Be ready to explain why you pick one metric over another for a given problem.

Week 2: System design practice. Practice designing an end-to-end ML pipeline for a security or data-classification problem. Think about multi-tenancy, data drift, retraining strategies, and how you handle delayed or noisy labels.

Weeks 2-3: Coding practice. Candidates report a mix of data-manipulation problems and applied ML coding tasks in Python. Practice clean feature engineering on tabular data and model implementation using scikit-learn.

Week 3: Behavioral preparation. Write out four or five STAR stories covering model deployment, cross-functional collaboration, handling a production failure, and improving a metric under a hard constraint.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping straight to deep learning. Interviewers expect you to consider simple baselines first. Proposing a transformer for a problem that a gradient-boosted tree solves cleanly signals poor engineering judgment.

Stopping at model accuracy. Ending your system design answer at 'the model performs well on the test set' skips what Druva actually cares about: monitoring, retraining, failure modes, and how the system behaves with real enterprise data at scale.

Skipping clarifying questions. In system design rounds, jumping straight into a solution without asking about scale, latency, and label availability is a red flag that candidates frequently cite as feedback they received.

Generic behavioral answers. Saying 'I improved the model' without context or a concrete result makes it impossible for interviewers to assess your actual contribution. Be specific, and hedge the numbers honestly if you need to.

Not connecting to the domain. Candidates who treat the Druva interview like any generic ML role and never reference data protection, enterprise customers, or cloud-scale constraints miss an easy opportunity to stand out from equally skilled candidates.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Druva ML Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report a process that typically runs 4-5 rounds. This usually includes a recruiter screen, a coding or take-home round, an ML concepts discussion, a system design round, and a final conversation with a hiring manager or senior team member. The exact structure can vary by team and seniority level, so ask your recruiter at the start what to expect from the full process.

What programming language and tools should I prepare in?

Python is standard for ML roles at most product companies, and Druva is no exception based on candidate reports. Be ready to write clean Python for data manipulation, feature engineering, and model implementation. Familiarity with scikit-learn, pandas, and at least one deep learning framework (PyTorch or TensorFlow) is useful. Knowledge of cloud data tools like Spark or AWS services is a bonus given Druva's cloud-native stack.

Is there a take-home assignment in the process?

Some candidates report receiving a take-home ML case study, while others go straight to a live coding round. When a take-home is given, it typically involves a real-world or synthetic dataset and asks you to build and explain a model end-to-end. Treat it as an opportunity to show your production thinking, not just your modeling skill. Write clean code, add brief comments where your reasoning is not obvious, and include a short write-up of your approach and the trade-offs you considered.

What salary can I expect for this role in India?

Druva does not publish salary bands publicly. Based on Glassdoor and levels.fyi figures commonly cited for cloud-security product companies of similar scale, ML Engineer compensation in Bangalore is generally competitive with the broader product-company market. Your specific offer will depend on your experience level, interview performance, and how you negotiate. Research current market data on Glassdoor before your offer discussion so you can go in informed.

How important is prior experience in data protection or security?

You do not need a background in backup or data protection to clear the interview. Candidates who show they have spent even a few hours understanding Druva's product, what ransomware detection means in a backup context, and what multi-tenant cloud constraints look like tend to report a stronger interview experience. Spend some time on Druva's blog and product pages before your first round. It signals genuine interest and makes your answers more grounded than generic ML responses.

How long does the full process take from application to offer?

Candidates typically report a timeline of 3-6 weeks from the first recruiter call to an offer, though this can stretch if team schedules are busy or if a take-home round is included. Following up with your recruiter after each round is good practice. If you have a competing offer with a deadline, let the recruiter know early so the team can try to align their timeline.
