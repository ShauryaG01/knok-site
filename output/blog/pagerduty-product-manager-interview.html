01 Overview

Overview

PagerDuty is a US-listed operations cloud company whose core product helps engineering and IT teams manage incidents, on-call rotations, and service reliability at scale. For a Product Manager here, that means owning features used by SREs, DevOps engineers, and IT operations managers at some of the world's largest enterprises.

As of mid-2026, knok jobradar shows 25 open PM roles at PagerDuty. The interview process typically runs across several rounds, and candidates report a strong emphasis on product sense for developer-facing tools, data reasoning, and cross-functional influence.

PagerDuty PMs sit at the intersection of reliability engineering and business outcomes. Interviewers test whether you can speak the language of SREs (MTTR, on-call fatigue, alert noise) and also translate that into business value for enterprise buyers. If you are new to incident management as a domain, plan to spend time with PagerDuty's own product documentation, their publicly available 'State of Digital Operations' reports, and community discussions on DevOps forums before your first round.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Practice these out loud. Each uses the STAR structure.

---

Q: How would you approach reducing alert fatigue in PagerDuty's AIOps product?

*Situation:* At my previous company, our on-call team was dealing with a high volume of pages nightly. Industry surveys commonly cite alert noise as the top driver of on-call burnout, and engineers were starting to ignore low-severity pages.

*Task:* As PM, I was asked to reduce noise without missing genuine incidents.

*Action:* I segmented alerts by source, service, and time-of-day to find the noisiest offenders. I partnered with the data science team to build a suppression model trained on several months of historical alert data, flagging alerts that had never led to a real incident. I then ran a controlled pilot: a subset of engineers got the suppression layer while the rest did not. I tracked false-negative rate and engineer-reported fatigue via weekly pulse surveys.

*Result:* After six weeks, the pilot group reported meaningfully lower after-hours interruptions and the false-negative rate held flat. We rolled out broadly. The framing I would bring to PagerDuty: suppression is a trust problem, so you ship it incrementally and let engineers audit what was suppressed.

---

Q: How do you decide which new integration to build next?

*Situation:* At a B2B SaaS company I managed, we had a large backlog of requested integrations from customers and prospects.

*Task:* I needed a repeatable framework to prioritise without simply going by the loudest customer voice.

*Action:* I built a scoring model across three dimensions: reach (how many active customers use the tool we would integrate with), strategic fit (does this pull us upmarket or into a new segment), and engineering effort (T-shirt sized by the eng lead). I also layered in lost-deal data from sales: which integrations appeared most often in lost-deal notes. I presented the top candidates to leadership with the data and got alignment on the first two.

*Result:* The first integration shipped in one quarter and became one of our top trial-to-paid conversion drivers that year. The framework became the team's default for integration backlog reviews.

---

Q: Tell me about a product decision you had to make with incomplete data.

*Situation:* We were deciding whether to sunset a legacy API that a meaningful share of active accounts still used, according to our telemetry.

*Task:* Engineering wanted to deprecate it to cut maintenance burden, but the data showed usage without explaining the reason behind it.

*Action:* I ran a short sprint: customer interviews with several accounts flagged as heavy users, a survey to the broader segment, and a review of support tickets mentioning the API. I found that most usage was not intentional dependence but old automation scripts customers had forgotten about. I recommended a phased sunset with a migration guide and one-click upgrade tool.

*Result:* Most affected accounts migrated before the deadline with no support escalations. Engineering shipped the deprecation on schedule. The lesson: 'usage' in telemetry and 'dependency' in business context are different things, and qualitative research is the only way to tell them apart.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Having a go-to framework for each question type makes your answers sharper and easier to follow.

For prioritisation questions (feature or integration backlogs), score across reach (how many users or accounts are affected), impact (what outcome does it drive), and effort (engineering cost). Add a strategic overlay: does this help PagerDuty move upmarket or defend against a competitor?

For metrics questions, anchor on a North Star metric first, then break it into leading indicators. For an incident response product, a reasonable North Star is something like 'mean time to resolution per incident, per team.' Leading indicators could include alert-to-page conversion rate, escalation rate, and post-incident review completion rate.

For strategy questions, use a simple adjacency map: core product, adjacent use cases, and transformational bets. PagerDuty's core is incident response. Adjacent bets include change management, service dependency mapping, and operations analytics. Interviewers want to see you think in layers, not just name features.

For behavioural questions, use STAR but do not skip the 'So what' at the end. PagerDuty interviewers typically probe for what you learned and how you would do it differently. Have a few stories ready that you can adapt: one about shipping under constraints, one about influencing without authority, and one about a decision that did not go as planned.

For pricing or packaging questions, show that you understand value-based pricing. Enterprise buyers of PagerDuty are paying to reduce downtime, which has a quantifiable cost. Anchoring your pricing rationale to 'cost of one hour of downtime avoided' is more compelling than 'per seat per month.'

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

PagerDuty PMs typically need to satisfy two very different audiences: technical users (SREs, DevOps engineers) and economic buyers (VPs of Engineering, CIOs). Interviewers look for candidates who can move fluently between both.

Domain empathy for on-call teams. You do not need to have been an SRE, but you should be able to articulate what a bad on-call week feels like and why it matters commercially. Candidates who speak only in abstract product terms, without connecting to the daily reality of a paged engineer, tend to struggle.

Data reasoning without data dependency. Interviewers will often give you thin or ambiguous data and watch how you structure your thinking. They want to see hypothesis-first reasoning: 'I would expect X because of Y, and I would test it by looking at Z.'

Cross-functional influence. PagerDuty's products touch security, IT ops, engineering, and business operations. PMs here have to align stakeholders with very different incentives. Expect questions about how you bring along teams that do not report to you.

Customer obsession with a commercial lens. PagerDuty is a B2B company with enterprise contracts. Interviewers want PMs who care about customer outcomes and can also explain how those outcomes translate into retention, expansion, and net revenue retention.

Communication clarity. Because the product is technical, interviewers pay attention to whether you can explain complex concepts simply. Practice explaining 'what PagerDuty does' to a non-technical person in two sentences before your first round.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused two-to-three week plan that candidates typically find effective.

Week 1: Know the product and the domain.
Sign up for PagerDuty's free tier and use it. Set up a test service, trigger a test incident, and go through the on-call workflow yourself. Read the 'State of Digital Operations' report that PagerDuty publishes publicly each year. Review their product changelog and release notes to understand what they have shipped recently. Study the basics of incident management: MTTR, MTTA (mean time to acknowledge), escalation policies, and alert grouping.

Week 2: Prepare your stories and frameworks.
Write out five STAR stories from your past. Aim for: one about prioritisation under constraints, one about defining and tracking a key metric, one about influencing a stakeholder, one about a failure or course-correction, and one about shipping under time pressure. Map each story to the question types listed above. Practice the North Star metric framework and the RICE scoring approach until they feel natural in conversation.

Week 3: Mock interviews and company research.
Do at least two timed mock interviews out loud, ideally with a peer who can give feedback. Research PagerDuty's recent news: acquisitions, partnerships, product launches. Look up their earnings calls or investor materials for publicly reported revenue and growth context. Prepare a few sharp questions to ask your interviewers, focused on the team's roadmap and how PM success is measured.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Candidates who have interviewed at PagerDuty commonly flag these as areas where they underperformed.

Talking about the product without using it. Interviewers can tell quickly if you have not used PagerDuty. Set up the free trial and go through real workflows before any round.

Generic prioritisation answers. Saying 'I would talk to customers and use data' is not a framework. Name the specific signals you would use (support ticket volume, deal win/loss data, usage telemetry) and explain how you would weigh them against each other.

Ignoring the buyer versus user split. On-call engineers use PagerDuty, but VP-level buyers purchase it. Missing this distinction in strategy or pricing questions is a common gap.

Underselling your metrics instinct. PagerDuty is a data-rich environment. If your STAR stories do not include specific outcomes (even directional ones like 'reduced escalation rate' or 'improved trial conversion'), they land weaker than they should.

Not preparing questions to ask. Candidates report that PagerDuty interviewers give real weight to the questions you bring. Shallow questions ('what does a typical day look like?') signal low engagement. Specific questions about roadmap tensions or how the team measures PM impact signal genuine interest.

Confusing incident management with ITSM. PagerDuty is not ServiceNow. If you conflate the two in your answers, it signals you have not done enough research on how PagerDuty positions itself and who its real competitors are.
