02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Based on what candidates report, VIEWS PM interviews focus on product sense, metrics, and cross-functional collaboration. These are the questions that come up most often:

  1. Walk us through a product you have shipped end-to-end. What was your role and what trade-offs did you make?
  2. How would you prioritise a feature backlog if engineering capacity is limited for the next quarter?
  3. Tell us about a time you disagreed with an engineering lead or designer. How did you resolve it?
  4. If user engagement on our core product dropped noticeably, how would you investigate the root cause?
  5. Describe a data-driven decision you made. What metric did you track and what did you find?
  6. How would you define success for a new feature launch? What does your go-to launch checklist look like?
  7. You have three equally important features to build but capacity for only one. How do you decide?
  8. Tell us about a product you use daily that could be improved. What would you change and why?
  9. How do you gather user feedback when you do not have a dedicated research team?
  10. Describe a situation where a product you owned missed its target metrics. What did you learn?
  11. How do you work with sales or customer success to bring field feedback into your roadmap?
  12. If you were joining VIEWS as a PM on day one, what would you focus on in your first month?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through a product you have shipped end-to-end. What was your role and what trade-offs did you make?

*Situation:* I was the sole PM for a mobile notification system at my previous company. Push notification open rates were declining despite a growing user base.

*Task:* My goal was to improve open rates without increasing notification volume, since data showed users were already fatigued.

*Action:* I ran a segmentation analysis and found that users who received notifications in the morning opened them at a significantly higher rate. I proposed a 'smart send time' feature, worked with engineers through several sprints to build a simple time-preference model, and coordinated with design to add a notification preferences screen.

*Result:* Open rates improved meaningfully in the weeks following launch, and the preferences screen also reduced opt-outs. It taught me to look at timing and context before volume.

---

Q: If user engagement on a core product dropped noticeably, how would you investigate the root cause?

*Situation:* At a previous company, we saw a sudden drop in daily active users on our web app.

*Task:* I had to find the cause quickly before leadership escalated, since the drop coincided with a competitor launch.

*Action:* I broke the metric down by cohort (new vs. returning users), by platform (mobile vs. desktop), and by geography. The drop was concentrated in returning desktop users in Tier 2 cities. I then checked if a recent deployment had changed the desktop UI and found a layout shift was hiding the core action button on smaller screens.

*Result:* We rolled back the layout change quickly and engagement recovered. The investigation took less than a day because I had pre-defined dashboards ready for exactly this kind of triage.

---

Q: Describe a situation where a product you owned missed its target metrics. What did you learn?

*Situation:* I owned a referral programme that was projected to bring in a certain number of new signups in its first quarter.

*Task:* Halfway through the quarter, it was clear we would miss the target by a large margin.

*Action:* I did a root cause analysis and found that the referral incentive was not compelling for users in our main demographic, even though it had tested well in a different segment. I quickly A/B tested a revised reward structure and updated the copy.

*Result:* We recovered some ground in the back half of the quarter but still missed the original goal. I learned to validate assumptions in the specific user segment that matters most, not just whichever segment responds fastest to surveys.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions. Structure every behavioural answer as: Situation (the context), Task (your specific responsibility), Action (what you personally did, not the team), Result (a measurable or observable outcome). Keep Situation and Task brief. Spend most of your time on Action and Result.

Problem framing for product design questions. When asked to design or improve a product, start by clarifying the goal and the user. A simple sequence: (1) confirm who the user is, (2) identify their core pain, (3) brainstorm solutions, (4) prioritise one solution with clear trade-offs, (5) define success metrics. Interviewers at VIEWS, candidates report, value structured thinking over a single 'right' answer.

Metric breakdown for analytical questions. If a metric drops or you are asked to define success, split it into components first. For example, 'engagement' can be broken into frequency, depth, and breadth. This shows you think in systems, not just symptoms.

Prioritisation frameworks. RICE (Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort) and ICE (Impact, Confidence, Ease) are both widely used. Pick one and stay consistent. More important than the specific framework is explaining why you ranked something higher, using data or user evidence rather than gut feel.

First-month plan. If asked what you would do early in the role, structure your answer in three phases: listen and learn (speak to users, read existing docs, understand the current roadmap), form hypotheses (identify a few areas with the biggest potential), and propose one small win (a quick action with low risk and visible impact).

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Candidates who have interviewed at VIEWS typically describe interviewers as focused on clarity of thought and product intuition rather than domain expertise.

Structured thinking over fluency. Interviewers prefer a candidate who pauses, asks a clarifying question, and then gives a structured answer, over someone who rushes into a confident but vague response. Saying 'let me frame this first' signals confidence, not hesitation.

Evidence from your own work. VIEWS interviewers reportedly probe for specifics. If you say 'we improved retention,' expect a follow-up like 'how did you measure that?' Have real numbers or directional outcomes ready. If your company had confidentiality constraints, say so and explain the direction of the result.

Ownership and cross-functional skill. Based on publicly available VIEWS job descriptions, PMs work closely with engineering, design, and business stakeholders. Interviewers want to see you can hold a position under pushback and bring others along without relying on hierarchy.

User empathy grounded in data. Qualitative insight ('users told me') combined with quantitative validation ('and the funnel data confirmed it') is the pattern that lands well. Pure gut-feel without data, or pure data without user context, both fall short.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Understand the company and the role.
Read everything publicly available about VIEWS: their product, recent news, any engineering or design blog posts. Look at the open PM roles to understand which teams are hiring and what skills appear repeatedly. Build a brief answer to 'why VIEWS?' that is specific, not generic.

Week 2: Practise product sense and analytical questions.
Pick a few products you use regularly and practise improving them using the problem-framing sequence in the Answer Frameworks section. For analytical questions, practise metric breakdowns out loud, not just in your head.

Week 3: Behavioural preparation.
Write out several STAR stories covering: a product you shipped, a conflict you resolved, a failure you learned from, a data-driven decision, a time you influenced without authority, and a time you prioritised under constraints. Practise telling each story concisely.

Week 4: Mock interviews and logistics.
Do at least a couple of mock interviews with someone who can give honest feedback. Review your stories for vague language ('we did something,' 'the results were good') and replace with specifics. Prepare a few thoughtful questions to ask your interviewers. Candidates report that VIEWS interviewers appreciate questions about product strategy and team structure more than questions about perks.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Answering before clarifying. Product design and strategy questions are intentionally open-ended. Jumping to an answer without asking 'who is the target user?' or 'what is the primary goal?' signals poor product instincts. Always take a moment to clarify before you start.

Vague results in STAR answers. Saying 'the product did really well' without supporting evidence weakens your story. Even directional data ('retention went up over the next quarter') is better than nothing. Prepare your outcomes in advance.

Ignoring the 'why VIEWS' question. Many candidates treat this as a formality. Interviewers use it to test whether you understand the company direction and are genuinely interested. Generic answers ('great product, good culture') are easy to spot.

Overcomplicating the framework. Candidates sometimes stack multiple frameworks into a single answer to appear thorough. This often makes the answer harder to follow. Pick one structure and execute it cleanly.

Not asking questions at the end. Ending the interview with 'I think I covered everything' is a missed signal. Prepare a few specific questions about the team's current roadmap challenges or how success is measured for new PMs. It shows you are already thinking like someone who works there.

Treating every question as a product design question. When asked a behavioural question, answer with a STAR story, not a framework. Mixing the two confuses interviewers about what you actually did versus what you theorise you would do.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the VIEWS PM interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically runs across multiple rounds, commonly covering a recruiter screen, one or two product and analytical rounds, a behavioural interview, and a final discussion with senior leadership. The exact number varies by team and level. Confirm the structure with your recruiter at the start so you can plan your preparation accordingly.

Does VIEWS give a product case study or take-home assignment?

Some candidates report receiving a short take-home case, while others describe an in-round product design problem instead. This varies by team and seniority level. Prepare for both formats so you are not caught off guard. Practise structuring your answer clearly under time pressure regardless of the format.

What salary can I expect for a PM role at VIEWS?

Based on knok job radar data, PM salaries in India typically range from 24-40 LPA for mid-level roles and 40-60 LPA at the senior level. For VIEWS specifically, cross-check with Glassdoor or levels.fyi and factor in the level you are interviewing for before you negotiate. Compensation can also vary by team, city, and total comp structure including equity and bonuses.

How important is domain expertise for a PM role at VIEWS?

Candidates typically report that VIEWS values structured product thinking and problem-solving over domain-specific experience. That said, if the role sits in a specific vertical, showing familiarity with that space adds credibility to your answers. Focus on fundamentals first, and layer in domain knowledge where it genuinely strengthens your examples.

How long does the VIEWS hiring process take from first screen to offer?

Candidates report timelines vary, but the process from first screen to offer commonly takes a few weeks. Following up with your recruiter after each round is a reasonable and accepted practice. Having competing offers or a deadline can sometimes accelerate decisions, but be transparent about it rather than bluffing.

Should I prepare for metric and SQL questions at VIEWS?

Some VIEWS PM roles lean more analytical, especially for growth or data-focused product areas. Candidates for those roles report being asked to walk through a metric analysis or interpret a dataset. Even if you do not write SQL, practise explaining how you would approach a funnel analysis or a cohort breakdown in plain terms. Check the job description for signals like 'data-driven' or 'analytical mindset' to gauge how much to prepare.
