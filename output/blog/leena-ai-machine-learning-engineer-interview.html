01 Overview

Overview

Leena AI is a Bangalore-based enterprise software company that builds conversational AI products for HR automation, covering employee onboarding, HR ticketing, policy queries, and leave management. As a Machine Learning Engineer there, you will typically work on NLP pipelines, intent and entity models, LLM fine-tuning, and the infrastructure that keeps a real-time chatbot running reliably at enterprise scale.

Candidates report the interview process typically covers a few rounds: an initial HR or recruiter call, one or two technical rounds on ML fundamentals and Python coding, a system design round focused on conversational AI or NLP systems, and a final culture or leadership discussion. Some candidates report a short take-home NLP task as part of the process. Leena AI currently has 8 open Machine Learning Engineer roles, so the team is actively hiring and rounds tend to move at a reasonable pace.

The role suits engineers who are comfortable working close to a real product. You are not running experiments in isolation; your models are in production, fielding questions from enterprise employees every day. That product context shapes everything Leena AI looks for in interviews.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

  1. How would you build an intent classification system for HR queries when you only have a small set of labeled data to start with?
  2. Walk through how you would fine-tune a pre-trained language model (such as BERT or a smaller LLM) for a domain-specific HR task.
  3. How do you design a multi-turn dialogue system that tracks conversation context reliably across several messages?
  4. What is your approach to detecting out-of-scope or unanswerable queries in a chatbot, and how do you handle them gracefully?
  5. How would you extract HR-specific entities (leave types, employee IDs, policy names) from free-form employee messages?
  6. What metrics would you track to evaluate a conversational AI model both offline (before deployment) and in production?
  7. You need to serve LLM responses in near-real-time. What techniques would you use to reduce inference latency?
  8. Design a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pipeline that answers employee questions about company HR policies. What are the key components and failure points?
  9. How do you handle noisy, ambiguous, or code-switched (Hinglish) inputs in an NLP pipeline serving Indian enterprise users?
  10. How would you set up A/B testing for two versions of an NLP model in a live product without disrupting user experience?
  11. How do you monitor a deployed ML model for performance drift, and at what point do you trigger retraining?
  12. Leena AI's product connects to enterprise HRMS systems like Workday or SAP. How would you design an ML layer that personalises responses based on an employee's role and conversation history?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How would you build an intent classification system for HR queries when you only have a small set of labeled data?

*Situation:* At my previous company, we launched an internal HR chatbot with a very small set of labeled conversation examples spread across multiple intent categories.

*Task:* I needed a classifier accurate enough to go live without waiting months to collect more data through normal usage.

*Action:* I started with a pre-trained multilingual sentence transformer to handle Hindi-English mix, and fine-tuned it on our small labeled set using a few-shot learning approach. I added data augmentation via back-translation and paraphrasing with a smaller generative model to artificially expand training variety. I then set up an active learning loop so that low-confidence predictions flagged real conversations for human review each week, steadily growing the training set over time. I also added an 'unknown intent' bucket with a confidence threshold so the bot would escalate to a human agent rather than guess.

*Result:* We reached solid validation accuracy within a few weeks of launch, and the escalation-to-human-agent rate dropped noticeably in the first month of production, measured against our pre-launch internal baseline.

---

Q: How do you monitor a deployed ML model for performance drift and decide when to trigger retraining?

*Situation:* A conversational intent model I owned had been in production for several months at a SaaS company when new HR policy terminology started appearing in employee messages after a policy overhaul.

*Task:* I needed a monitoring strategy that would catch drift early, rather than waiting for support tickets to tell us the model had degraded.

*Action:* I set up three layers of monitoring. First, I tracked the daily share of low-confidence predictions as a leading indicator of concept drift. Second, I ran a weekly sample of production conversations through a human labeling queue and computed accuracy on that gold set. Third, I watched downstream product metrics (ticket escalation rate, thumbs-down feedback) in our analytics dashboard. I defined a retraining trigger: if any two of those three signals crossed their thresholds in the same week, an automated retraining pipeline kicked off with the freshly labeled data.

*Result:* The system caught a drift event caused by a company-wide policy rename before any customer complaint was filed. Retraining with the updated data resolved the accuracy drop within days of detection.

---

Q: Design a RAG pipeline that answers employee questions about company HR policies.

*Situation:* I worked on a proof-of-concept for an IT firm that wanted employees to ask natural-language questions about leave, reimbursement, and benefits policies stored across several PDF documents.

*Task:* I had to design an end-to-end pipeline that was accurate, cited sources, and could be refreshed whenever policies changed.

*Action:* I chunked the policy PDFs into overlapping paragraphs and embedded them using a sentence transformer model, storing vectors in a lightweight vector database. At query time, I retrieved the most relevant chunks using cosine similarity, then passed those chunks plus the user question to an LLM with a system prompt instructing it to answer only from the provided context and to reply 'I am not sure' when the context was insufficient. I added a metadata filter so that queries from employees in a specific location only retrieved policy chunks tagged for that region. A weekly refresh job re-embedded any policy document that had changed.

*Result:* In user testing the pipeline answered HR policy questions accurately on most test queries, and the fallback response prevented hallucinated policy details from ever reaching employees.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For technical questions, use a three-part structure: clarify the constraints first (data size, latency budget, accuracy target), then describe your approach step by step, then discuss trade-offs and how you would validate results. Interviewers at product-focused AI companies like Leena AI care as much about your reasoning as your final answer.

For system design, think in layers: data layer (collection, labeling, storage), model layer (architecture, training, evaluation), and serving layer (API, latency, monitoring). Walk through the pipeline verbally before diving into any one component. Always ask clarifying questions about scale and latency requirements before you start designing, not midway through.

For behavioral questions, use STAR: Situation (a sentence or two of context), Task (what you were personally responsible for), Action (what you did, using 'I' not 'we'), Result (a concrete outcome tied to a measurable baseline). Keep the full answer focused and to the point.

For 'tell me about a failure', treat it as STAR but put extra weight on what you learned and what you would do differently. Interviewers value genuine self-awareness over a polished story where everything worked out.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Depth in NLP and conversational AI. Leena AI's core product is a conversational HR bot, so candidates who can discuss intent classification, dialogue management, entity extraction, and LLM fine-tuning at a practical level stand out. Surface-level answers about 'training a model' are not enough.

Production mindset. Knowing how to build a model is table stakes. Interviewers want to hear how you monitor it, retrain it, and handle edge cases in a live system used by enterprise employees every day.

Comfort with limited data. Enterprise NLP often starts with small labeled datasets. Show that you know techniques like transfer learning, few-shot learning, active learning, and data augmentation, and know when to reach for each one.

Understanding of the HR domain. You do not need to be an HR expert, but knowing that employees ask questions in a mix of languages, use informal phrasing, and need precise answers on sensitive topics (pay, leave, policy) shows you have thought about the actual product rather than just the technology.

Clear communication. Candidates report that Leena AI values engineers who can explain their work to non-technical stakeholders. Practice articulating your thought process out loud during prep, not just arriving at the right answer silently.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Core ML and NLP fundamentals. Review classification, evaluation metrics (precision, recall, F1), and transformer architecture basics including attention mechanisms and how BERT fine-tuning works. Solve a few LeetCode medium problems in Python daily to stay sharp on coding.

Week 2: Conversational AI depth. Study dialogue systems: intent detection, slot filling, context tracking, and response generation. Read up on RAG pipelines and vector databases. Build or revisit a small NLP project you can walk through in detail during an interview.

Week 3: System design and production ML. Practice designing ML systems end-to-end: data pipeline, model training, serving, and monitoring. Study latency reduction techniques for LLMs (quantization, caching, batching). Review how you would detect model drift and set up retraining triggers in a real product.

Week 4: Company-specific prep and mock interviews. Read Leena AI's public blog posts and product announcements to understand their engineering challenges and product roadmap. Prepare a handful of STAR stories covering impact, failure, and cross-functional collaboration. Do at least a couple of full mock interviews (one technical, one system design) with a peer or via an online platform.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating it like a generic ML interview. Leena AI builds a specific product. Candidates who give textbook ML answers without connecting them to conversational AI or NLP use cases often do not progress past the first technical round. Tie your examples to dialogue, text, or language tasks wherever you can.

Proposing large models for every problem. Interviewers want to see engineering judgment. If a task can be solved with a lightweight classifier and active learning, defaulting to a large LLM every time signals poor cost and latency awareness. Show that you weigh trade-offs deliberately.

Stopping at model training. Describing how you would train a model is only half the answer. Candidates report that interviewers consistently probe what comes after: how do you monitor it, retrain it, and handle failures? Get into the habit of completing the production story in every answer.

Vague STAR answers. Saying 'we improved accuracy a lot' is weak. Anchor results in something concrete, such as a percentage change relative to a baseline or a measurable downstream metric, even if you note it as an internal measurement rather than a publicly reported figure.

Skipping clarifying questions in system design. Starting to design without asking about scale, latency requirements, or data constraints is a red flag interviewers notice quickly. Show that you scope a problem carefully before you solve it.
