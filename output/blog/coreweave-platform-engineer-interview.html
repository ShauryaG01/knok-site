01 Overview

Overview

CoreWeave has grown into one of the leading GPU-accelerated cloud platforms, powering some of the largest AI training and inference workloads in the industry. As of July 2026, the company has 309 open roles, with Platform Engineer being a core hire as they scale their compute infrastructure.

Platform Engineers at CoreWeave build and operate the foundational systems: bare-metal provisioning, Kubernetes clusters optimized for GPU workloads, high-performance networking, and the internal developer tools that allow AI teams to ship faster. The work is deeply technical, operating at a scale that most cloud engineers do not encounter elsewhere.

Candidates report that the interview process typically spans four to six rounds, covering a recruiter screen, technical phone interviews, a take-home or live coding exercise, a system design round, and a final loop with senior engineers or hiring managers. The process is known for being rigorous on Kubernetes internals, distributed systems, and networking fundamentals.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

CoreWeave's interviews focus on GPU infrastructure, Kubernetes depth, and large-scale systems thinking. These are the questions candidates report most frequently:

  1. Walk us through how you would design a multi-tenant Kubernetes cluster optimized for GPU workloads.
  2. How have you handled GPU resource scheduling using Kubernetes device plugins or custom schedulers?
  3. Describe your experience building or maintaining a bare-metal provisioning pipeline at scale.
  4. How does RDMA over Converged Ethernet (RoCE) or InfiniBand work, and when would you use one over the other?
  5. How would you implement GitOps for infrastructure changes across a large fleet of nodes?
  6. Walk us through debugging a performance bottleneck in a distributed AI training job.
  7. How would you design a highly available, high-throughput storage layer for ML model checkpoints?
  8. Describe how you have set up observability (metrics, logs, traces) for a Kubernetes-based platform.
  9. How would you approach capacity planning for a GPU cluster that must support both training and inference workloads?
  10. How have you managed secrets, RBAC, and network policies in a multi-tenant environment?
  11. How would you implement autoscaling for GPU-based inference endpoints with unpredictable traffic spikes?
  12. Tell us about a time you improved the reliability or SLA of a critical platform service.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through how you would design a multi-tenant Kubernetes cluster optimized for GPU workloads.

*Situation:* At my previous company, we ran a shared ML platform where multiple internal teams needed isolated GPU resources on a single cluster.

*Task:* I had to design a namespace-level isolation model that prevented noisy-neighbour problems while keeping GPU utilisation high.

*Action:* I implemented namespace-level resource quotas with LimitRange objects capping per-pod GPU requests. I used taints and tolerations to dedicate specific node pools to high-priority training jobs. For scheduling, I extended the default scheduler with a custom plugin that respected GPU topology (NVLink locality) to minimise cross-socket traffic. I also enforced NetworkPolicy rules to prevent cross-tenant traffic by default.

*Result:* GPU utilisation improved noticeably, scheduling conflicts dropped, and teams reported faster job completion times for large training runs. The pattern was later adopted as the company standard for new clusters.

---

Q: Describe your experience building a bare-metal provisioning pipeline.

*Situation:* We were onboarding new GPU servers every week as the company expanded its compute capacity.

*Task:* The manual provisioning process was error-prone and took a full day per rack. I was asked to automate it end-to-end.

*Action:* I built a pipeline using iPXE for network boot, cloud-init for OS configuration, and Ansible for post-boot hardening. I stored all node profiles in Git, so every provisioning run was version-controlled and reviewable. I also added automated validation steps that ran benchmark jobs after provisioning to confirm GPU and network health before the node joined the cluster.

*Result:* Provisioning time dropped significantly per rack, error rates fell, and the team could onboard new hardware without senior engineer involvement.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you improved the reliability of a critical platform service.

*Situation:* Our internal model registry, used by every ML team to store and retrieve model artifacts, had a pattern of going down during peak hours due to storage backend saturation.

*Task:* I was asked to own the incident and make the service reliable enough to remove it from the critical-alert rotation.

*Action:* I started with a post-mortem to identify the root cause: a single NFS mount that could not handle concurrent reads during peak traffic. I migrated the hot-path artifact reads to an object storage backend with a read-through cache, updated the service to use connection pooling, and added circuit breakers so a slow storage backend would not cascade into full service unavailability. I also set up SLO-based alerting so we could catch degradation before users noticed.

*Result:* The service went from multiple incidents per week to zero incidents over the following two months. It was removed from the P1 alert rotation, freeing the on-call team from weekend pages.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Most CoreWeave interview questions are behavioural-technical hybrids: they ask for a real past experience but want to hear deep technical reasoning, not just outcome storytelling. Use the STAR framework as your spine but expand the 'Action' section significantly.

STAR for Platform Engineering: Keep Situation and Task brief (two to three sentences each). Spend most of your time on Action: what specific technologies you used, the trade-offs you weighed, and why you made the choices you did. Result should be concrete where possible, describing what changed operationally even if you cannot share exact metrics.

For system design questions, use a structured opening: clarify requirements and constraints first, then talk through components (compute, network, storage, control plane), then discuss failure modes and how you would handle them. CoreWeave engineers care deeply about failure modes at scale, so spend at least a third of your time on 'what breaks and how do you recover.'

For debugging questions, walk the interviewer through your mental model: start from symptoms, form hypotheses, describe the tools you would use (perf, eBPF, tcpdump, kubectl debug, DCGM for GPU metrics), and explain how you would isolate variables. Thinking aloud is valued more than jumping straight to an answer.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

CoreWeave Platform Engineers operate at the intersection of cloud, HPC, and AI infrastructure. Interviewers are typically senior engineers who work on these systems daily, so they can immediately tell whether your experience is hands-on or surface-level.

Kubernetes depth beyond basics. Knowing how to deploy workloads is table stakes. They want to see that you understand the scheduler, the kubelet, device plugins, CRI and CNI internals, and how Kubernetes behaves under resource pressure and eviction.

GPU and HPC awareness. You do not need to be an ML engineer, but you should know what a GPU device plugin does, why GPU topology matters for training jobs, and what DCGM is used for. Familiarity with InfiniBand or RoCE networking is a strong signal.

Systems thinking at scale. Answers that work for a small cluster should also address what breaks at CoreWeave's scale. Interviewers look for candidates who naturally think about consistency, failure domains, and blast radius.

GitOps and automation culture. CoreWeave operates at a pace where manual changes are a liability. Show that your default is infrastructure-as-code, version-controlled, and peer-reviewed.

Clear communication under pressure. The interview panels are senior and move fast. Candidates who pause to clarify requirements, think aloud, and explain trade-offs are rated higher than those who rush to a solution.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Staying at the surface level of Kubernetes. Many candidates can describe Deployments and Services but stumble when asked about scheduler internals or what happens when a node runs out of memory. Prepare to go three layers deep on any topic you list on your resume.

Ignoring GPU-specific knowledge. Platform Engineers at CoreWeave work directly with GPU infrastructure. Saying 'I have not worked with GPUs but I can learn' is a red flag. Even basic familiarity with DCGM, device plugins, and why GPU memory bandwidth matters for training jobs sets you apart.

Vague answers on reliability work. Saying 'I improved system reliability' without specifics reads as padding. Even if you cannot share exact numbers, describe the failure mode, the root cause, and the architectural change you made.

Skipping the failure mode discussion in system design. Candidates often design the happy path and wait to be asked about failures. At CoreWeave, interviewers expect you to proactively raise failure scenarios. Build it into your answer structure from the start.

Not asking clarifying questions. Jumping into a system design answer without scoping requirements signals that you would do the same on the job. Spend the first two to three minutes asking about scale, SLAs, and constraints. Interviewers reward this.

Treating the recruiter screen as low-stakes. Candidates report that the initial recruiter call at CoreWeave sometimes includes light technical questions to filter for basic fit. Prepare a crisp two-minute summary of your infrastructure background before your first call.

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the CoreWeave Platform Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report a process of roughly four to six rounds, though this can vary by team and level. Typically you will go through a recruiter screen, one or two technical phone interviews, a system design round, and a final panel or hiring manager interview. Some candidates also report a take-home or live infrastructure exercise. The process is known to move quickly once it starts.

For a reference point, publicly reported figures on Glassdoor and levels.fyi for senior platform or infrastructure engineer roles at US-headquartered AI cloud companies vary widely by level and location. It is worth asking the recruiter directly about the band early in the process.

Does CoreWeave hire Platform Engineers remotely from India?

As of July 2026, knok's job radar shows CoreWeave has 309 open roles globally, but Platform Engineer listings are concentrated in certain locations based on available data. Remote eligibility for India-based candidates depends on the specific team and role. It is best to check the current job description and ask the recruiter directly about remote or hybrid options for your location.

What Kubernetes knowledge is most important to review before a CoreWeave interview?

Focus on areas beyond basic workload management. Interviewers commonly ask about the Kubernetes scheduler (how it selects nodes, custom scheduling), device plugins (especially GPU device plugins), kubelet internals, CRI and CNI, and how Kubernetes handles resource pressure and eviction. You should also be comfortable explaining multi-tenant isolation using namespaces, RBAC, NetworkPolicies, and resource quotas. Candidates who can explain not just 'what' but 'why' at each layer do significantly better.

Is experience with GPUs or AI infrastructure required for a Platform Engineer role at CoreWeave?

Given that CoreWeave's entire business is GPU cloud infrastructure for AI workloads, some familiarity with GPU infrastructure is strongly expected, even for generalist platform roles. You do not need to be an ML engineer or write CUDA code. But understanding GPU device plugins in Kubernetes, DCGM for monitoring, GPU topology and NVLink, and why high-speed networking matters for distributed training jobs will make a clear difference in your interview performance.

How should I prepare for the system design round specifically?

Practice designing systems that match CoreWeave's context: large GPU clusters, multi-tenant workloads, high-throughput networking, and strict SLA requirements. Pick two or three design problems (a distributed model checkpoint store, a GPU cluster autoscaler, a multi-tenant Kubernetes platform) and practice walking through them aloud within the allotted time. Prioritise discussing failure modes proactively rather than waiting to be asked. Interviewers consistently note that candidates who raise 'what breaks at scale' on their own score higher than those who only design the happy path.
