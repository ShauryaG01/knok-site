01 Overview

Overview

Mirus Opportunities is a staffing and recruitment firm that places Business Analysts across client organisations in sectors such as IT services, consulting, and operations. As of mid-2026, they carry 5 open Business Analyst roles, which points to steady active hiring across their client portfolio.

Candidates report a process that typically runs 2-3 rounds. The first is usually an HR screening call covering your background, current notice period, and salary expectations. One or two functional rounds follow, where interviewers assess your requirements-gathering skills, process analysis experience, and stakeholder communication. Some candidates also report a short written case study or take-home exercise to test structured thinking before the final discussion.

Because Mirus works as a placement firm, interviewers are evaluating you for fit across multiple potential client environments, not just a single team. Adaptability and domain-agnostic problem-solving carry extra weight here. Practical project experience matters more than certifications, though familiarity with Agile or Waterfall delivery adds credibility to your answers.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly in Mirus Opportunities BA interviews, based on publicly reported candidate experiences. Prepare a concrete example from your own work for each one.

  1. Walk me through how you gather requirements when stakeholders have conflicting priorities.
  2. Describe your experience with business process mapping and the tools you use.
  3. How do you handle a stakeholder or client who keeps changing requirements mid-project?
  4. What is the difference between functional and non-functional requirements? Give a real example from your own work.
  5. How do you decide which features to prioritise when the development team has limited bandwidth?
  6. Tell me about a time you spotted a process inefficiency and drove a fix for it.
  7. How do you confirm that the solution delivered actually solves the original business problem?
  8. What data tools have you worked with (Excel, SQL, Power BI, or similar)? Walk me through a specific analysis.
  9. How do you communicate technical details to a business audience with no technical background?
  10. Describe a project that went off-track or did not deliver as expected. What did you learn?
  11. How do you build trust with stakeholders who are resistant to process changes?
  12. What delivery methodologies have you worked in (Agile, Waterfall, or hybrid), and what was your specific BA role in each?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a time you gathered requirements when stakeholders disagreed with each other.

*Situation:* At my previous company, two department heads had opposing views on how a new internal reporting system should work. Finance wanted detailed cost breakdowns and Operations wanted a high-level summary dashboard.

*Task:* My job was to produce a single, agreed requirements document that the development team could actually build from, without the project stalling on internal politics.

*Action:* I held separate discovery sessions with each team first, so both sides felt fully heard before anyone had to defend a position in front of the other group. I then mapped both sets of needs onto a single process flow diagram and identified where the underlying goals actually overlapped. In a joint workshop, I framed the conversation around 'what does each of us need to make a decision quickly' rather than 'whose design is correct.' We aligned on a layered dashboard with a summary view for Ops and a drill-down section for Finance.

*Result:* Requirements were signed off in one revision cycle rather than the usual three, and the delivered product had no change requests from either team after go-live.

---

Q: Describe a time you identified a process inefficiency and drove a fix.

*Situation:* Our sales team was manually copying data from the CRM into a spreadsheet every morning to prepare the daily pipeline report, consuming a large chunk of one person's morning each day.

*Task:* I was asked to look at reporting automation as part of a broader operations review.

*Action:* I documented the current process step by step and spoke to the people running it to understand every edge case and exception. I then worked with the IT team to build a scheduled export that fed directly into the report template. Before switching over, I ran two weeks of parallel testing to confirm the automated output matched the manual one exactly.

*Result:* The manual task was eliminated completely. The team redirected that time to pipeline follow-up, and report accuracy improved because human copy-paste errors were removed entirely.

---

Q: Tell me about a project that went off-track. What did you learn?

*Situation:* Early in my career I was the BA on a software rollout for a retail client. We had thorough requirements documents signed off by the project sponsor, but we had not done enough interviews with the store staff who would actually use the system daily.

*Task:* Midway through development, UAT revealed that the interface made sense to IT but was confusing to store managers with no technical background.

*Action:* I organised emergency workshops with store managers, documented the usability gaps against the original requirements, and worked with the project manager to reprioritise the development backlog. We moved non-critical features to a Phase 2 and focused remaining time on the usability fixes.

*Result:* We delivered on time with core features working well for end users. The bigger lesson was that sign-off from a project sponsor is not the same as validation with actual end users. I now always include at least one round of end-user walkthroughs before UAT on any project I work on.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use STAR for every behavioural question. STAR stands for Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep the Situation and Task combined to 2-3 sentences. Spend most of your answer on the Action, because that is where interviewers judge your thinking process. Always close with a concrete Result, even a qualitative one ('stakeholders aligned within a week', 'the project shipped on schedule').

For 'how do you approach X' questions, structure your answer in three parts: (1) what information or context you gather first, (2) who you involve and how, (3) how you confirm the output is correct. This signals structured thinking without sounding rehearsed.

For requirements conflict scenarios, frame your answer around finding the shared underlying goal rather than picking a side. Interviewers want to see that you can de-escalate and redirect a group toward the actual business problem.

For tool-based questions (SQL, Excel, Power BI), lead with the business question you were answering, then bring in the tool. 'I used SQL to find which customer segments were churning fastest' lands better than just listing the tool name.

On using numbers in your answers: even rough, honest figures ('cut the reporting cycle from about a week down to two days') are stronger than vague claims. Only share numbers you can confidently defend if the interviewer follows up.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Mirus interviewers are evaluating you as a placement candidate, which means they are thinking about how you will perform across different client environments, not just one fixed role. Based on candidate reports, these are the qualities that come up most often:

Structured communication. Can you explain a complex process to someone who has no background in it? Interviewers often add a 'now explain that to a non-technical person' follow-up to probe this directly.

Stakeholder management. BAs placed by Mirus typically work with clients who have strong opinions and limited patience for process overhead. Showing that you can manage expectations, handle pushback professionally, and keep a project moving without formal authority is critical.

Adaptability. Placements span multiple sectors, so candidates who show they have quickly picked up a new domain (banking, logistics, healthcare, e-commerce) carry a genuine advantage over those with deep but narrow experience.

Hands-on tool experience. Process mapping tools, basic SQL or Excel for data analysis, and at least one project or ticketing tool (Jira, Confluence, or similar) are expected at most experience levels.

Ownership mindset. Interviewers respond well to candidates who do not just gather requirements and hand them off, but who actively track whether the delivered solution actually solved the original business problem.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Build your answer bank. Write down 5-6 projects you have worked on. For each, note the business problem, your specific actions, and the measurable outcome. These become the raw material for almost every interview question.

Step 2: Practise STAR answers out loud. Reading an answer is different from saying it. Practise with a friend or record yourself. Aim for answers that run 2-3 minutes, not longer.

Step 3: Refresh your tools knowledge. If you have not used SQL queries or Excel pivot tables recently, spend a few hours on a small practice dataset. Interviewers at Mirus often ask you to walk through an analysis you have actually done.

Step 4: Research Mirus Opportunities specifically. Check their LinkedIn page and active job postings to understand which sectors their clients are currently active in. Where possible, align your project examples to those sectors.

Step 5: Prepare smart questions for the interviewer. Ask about the specific client engagement, the team structure, or the tools used in the role. This signals genuine interest and gives you useful information for the placement discussion.

Step 6: Sort your setup. Mirus interviews are typically conducted online. Test your audio and video the day before and keep your resume open on screen so you can reference it quickly.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Vague answers without real examples. Saying 'I am good at stakeholder management' without backing it up with a specific situation tells the interviewer nothing. Every claim needs a story.

Describing the team's work as your own. Interviewers probe with follow-up questions. If your answers are built around 'we did this,' be ready to explain exactly what your individual contribution was.

Ignoring the business outcome. Many BA candidates describe their process in detail but forget to explain whether it actually worked. Always close with what the result was for the business.

Over-claiming tool expertise. If you list SQL or Power BI on your resume, assume you will be asked to walk through a real example. Do not list tools you have only observed others using.

Not asking any questions at the end. Candidates who ask nothing are often seen as less engaged. Prepare at least two specific questions about the role or the client context.

Talking only about technical requirements, ignoring the human side. A BA role is as much about people and communication as it is about documentation. If all your answers focus on tools and templates, interviewers may doubt your stakeholder skills.
