01 Overview

Overview

The Walt Disney Company currently has 24 Engineering Manager openings in India, making this a genuinely active hiring window. Disney's tech operations in India are anchored by the Disney+ Hotstar platform, which runs some of the most-watched live streaming infrastructure in the country, serving massive concurrent viewership during live cricket and major content launches.

The Engineering Manager role here sits at the intersection of people leadership, technical architecture, and business outcomes. You will lead teams building consumer-facing features, backend services, or platform infrastructure at scale. The interview process typically spans several rounds, and candidates report a mix of behavioural leadership discussions, system design, and cross-functional collaboration scenarios.

Across the broader India market, there are 975 Engineering Manager roles open as of early July 2026, with Bangalore leading at 182 openings. Salaries, based on knok jobradar data, range from 35-60 LPA at Manager level, 55-90 LPA at Senior Manager level, and 90-150+ LPA at Director level.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions reflect what candidates report being asked in Engineering Manager interviews at Disney, particularly for its India tech operations.

  1. Tell us about a time you led an engineering team through a high-traffic live event. How did you prepare, and how did you handle incidents on the day?
  1. Reliability is critical when serving large concurrent audiences. How do you build a culture of operational discipline and fast incident response within your team?
  1. Describe how you have managed a team with a mix of senior engineers and newer engineers. How did you keep both groups engaged and growing?
  1. Tell us about a time you had to make a technical trade-off between shipping speed and long-term system health. How did you arrive at your decision?
  1. How have you handled a situation where business stakeholders pushed for a feature your engineering team felt was not feasible on the given timeline?
  1. How do you keep your engineering team focused when there are competing priorities from multiple product teams across a large organisation?
  1. Describe a time you had to hire or grow your team quickly. How did you maintain quality while moving fast?
  1. How do you approach setting technical direction for a team when the problem space is still ambiguous and requirements are not fully defined?
  1. Tell us about a time you had to give difficult feedback to a strong performer. What happened and what did you learn from it?
  1. How do you measure the health and productivity of your engineering team without relying only on delivery velocity?
  1. Describe a cross-functional conflict you navigated as an engineering manager. How did you bring both sides to a resolution?
  1. Disney is a brand built on storytelling and user experience. How does that kind of brand identity shape the way you think about engineering decisions?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use these as a starting point. Replace the situations with your own experience before the interview.

Q: Tell us about a time you led an engineering team through a high-traffic live event.

*Situation:* My team owned the video delivery pipeline for a major live sports broadcast. The event was expected to drive very high concurrent viewership, and any degradation would have been immediately visible to users and widely reported.

*Task:* My responsibility was to make sure the team was operationally ready, technically prepared, and able to respond quickly if anything went wrong during the event window.

*Action:* We ran a structured pre-event review covering capacity planning, dependency health checks, and a dry run of our incident runbook. I set up a dedicated war room channel, assigned clear on-call leads for each service layer, and made sure every engineer knew their escalation path. I also negotiated a feature freeze with the product team for the two days leading up to the event.

*Result:* The event ran without a major incident. We caught one upstream latency spike early through monitoring and rolled back a dependency before it affected end users. The team's confidence in handling live events improved noticeably, and we codified the runbook as a standing practice for all future events.

---

Q: How have you handled a situation where business stakeholders pushed for a feature your engineering team felt was not feasible on the given timeline?

*Situation:* A product director wanted a personalised content recommendation feature shipped in two weeks ahead of a major content launch. My team had estimated the full build at closer to six weeks for a production-quality implementation.

*Task:* I needed to bridge the gap between business urgency and engineering reality without simply saying no or committing the team to a timeline we could not meet.

*Action:* I asked the product director to walk me through which parts of the feature drove the most value for launch day. Together, we identified that a simpler trending-content module would capture most of the user benefit with far less complexity. I proposed a phased approach: ship the simplified version for launch, then build the full recommendation engine over the following sprint cycles. I was transparent with both the stakeholder and my team about what each phase would and would not deliver.

*Result:* The product director agreed to the phased plan. We shipped the simplified feature on time, it received good user feedback, and the full recommendation engine followed in the next cycle with real usage data informing the design.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you gave difficult feedback to a strong performer.

*Situation:* One of my best engineers was consistently delivering excellent individual work but was missing estimates and not flagging blockers early. The pattern was affecting sprint planning for the whole team.

*Task:* I needed to address this directly without damaging a relationship I valued or making the engineer feel their contributions were not recognised.

*Action:* I set up a dedicated 1:1 and opened with specific examples rather than general observations. I explained the downstream impact on the team and asked what they felt was getting in the way. It turned out they were absorbing scope increases silently because they did not want to appear slow. We agreed on a check-in cadence, and I encouraged them to flag scope changes as soon as they spotted them rather than absorbing them quietly.

*Result:* The engineer's estimate accuracy improved over the following sprints. More importantly, they started speaking up during planning sessions, which made the whole team's forecasting more reliable. They later told me it was the most useful feedback conversation they had had with a manager.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions. Most Disney EM questions begin with 'Tell us about a time...' or 'Describe a situation...' Structure every answer as: Situation (brief context), Task (your specific responsibility), Action (what you did, in detail), Result (what happened, including what you learned). Keep Situation and Task short. Spend most of your time on Action.

SBI for feedback questions. If asked how you give feedback, use Situation-Behaviour-Impact: describe the specific situation, the observable behaviour rather than the personality, and the impact on the team or product. Interviewers respond well to answers that show both directness and empathy.

Trade-off framing for technical decisions. When asked about system design or technical direction, show you think in trade-offs: speed vs reliability, build vs buy, short-term delivery vs long-term maintainability. Name the trade-off explicitly, explain how you weighted it against business context, and describe who you involved in the decision.

Stakeholder mapping for cross-functional questions. For questions about alignment or conflict, describe who the stakeholders were, what each party needed, and how you found common ground. Disney operates across many business units, so the ability to hold multiple perspectives is valued.

Data plus narrative for team health questions. Do not rely only on metrics. Show you use both qualitative signals (1:1 conversations, team sentiment, attrition risk) and quantitative ones (deployment frequency, incident resolution time, delivery against commitments) to understand how your team is doing.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Disney Engineering Manager interviews, candidates report, tend to test for a specific combination of traits.

Operational maturity. Disney+ Hotstar runs at a scale where outages have immediate, public consequences. Interviewers want to see that you have managed high-stakes production environments and that you treat reliability as a first-class engineering concern, not an afterthought.

People-first leadership. Disney places real emphasis on culture and team belonging. Expect questions that probe how you develop engineers, handle underperformance, and build psychological safety. Surface-level answers about holding 1:1s will not be enough. You need to show what you actually do inside those conversations.

Business partnership. Engineering Managers at Disney are expected to be genuine partners to product and business teams, not just technical gatekeepers. They want to see that you can push back in a way that moves things forward rather than stalling them.

Adaptability to scale. Disney's India tech teams have grown rapidly. Interviewers look for candidates who have scaled teams, processes, and systems, and who can operate in ambiguity without waiting for complete direction.

Consumer empathy. Disney is a consumer company at its core. Candidates who connect engineering decisions back to user experience tend to stand out. You do not need to be a UX designer, but you do need to show that the end user is part of how you think.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know the company. Spend time understanding Disney's India tech footprint. Disney+ Hotstar is the centre of gravity. Read publicly available information about the platform's capabilities, its approach to live streaming, and recent product developments. Understand how technology enables the business model.

Week 1: Map your stories. List the six to eight most significant situations from your career that demonstrate leadership, technical decision-making, conflict resolution, team building, and operational excellence. Write out the STAR structure for each. Look for situations where the stakes were high and your specific actions made a measurable difference.

Week 2: Practise out loud. Do not plan your answers only in your head. Record yourself answering the 12 questions listed above and listen back. Notice where you are vague, where you lose the thread, or where you say 'we' when the question is asking about you specifically. Practise with a peer if possible.

Week 2: Prepare your questions. Disney interviewers typically invite you to ask questions. Prepare thoughtful ones: about the team's current challenges, how engineering and product collaborate, what success looks like in the first few months, and how the team handled a recent major incident. Good questions signal serious interest and prior research.

Before each round: Re-read the specific job description. Match your strongest stories to the competencies called out in the posting. If the role emphasises platform reliability, lead with your best incident-management story. If it emphasises team building, lead with your strongest hiring or coaching example.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Saying 'we' instead of 'I.' A very common pattern is describing every achievement as 'we did X.' Interviewers are assessing you, not your team. Use 'I' when describing your decisions and actions. Credit your team in the Result section, but own the leadership choices in the Action section.

Generic answers with no Disney context. Candidates who treat this as a standard EM interview and give answers that could apply anywhere miss a real opportunity. Anchoring your answers to streaming scale, live event complexity, or consumer experience shows you understand what makes Disney's engineering context specific.

Underestimating the people leadership bar. Some engineering managers prepare heavily for system design and neglect behavioural depth. Disney typically weights people leadership heavily. Have strong stories ready about developing engineers, handling difficult feedback, and managing team dynamics.

Overpromising to seem agreeable. In hypothetical scenarios, candidates sometimes commit to unrealistic timelines to avoid conflict. Disney interviewers often probe specifically for how you push back and negotiate. Saying no clearly and constructively is part of the job.

Arriving without questions. Not having thoughtful questions signals low engagement. Prepare at least three specific questions and use them. Building on something that came up in the conversation is even better than a pre-planned question.

Skipping the 'what I learned' part of STAR. Results matter, but so does reflection. Interviewers note whether you extract lessons from difficult situations. End your answers with what you would do the same way or differently next time.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Disney Engineering Manager interview typically have?

Candidates report that the process typically involves several rounds covering behavioural leadership, system design, and cross-functional collaboration. There is often a hiring manager conversation early in the process followed by a panel or loop of rounds. The exact structure varies by team and role, so ask your recruiter at the start what to expect for the specific position you applied for.

What salary can I expect as an Engineering Manager at Disney India?

Based on knok jobradar data, Engineering Manager roles in India broadly range from 35-60 LPA, Senior Manager roles from 55-90 LPA, and Director-level roles from 90 LPA upward. For current Disney-specific benchmarks, Glassdoor and levels.fyi have community-reported data. Total compensation at this level typically includes variable pay and other components beyond base salary.

Does Disney ask system design questions in Engineering Manager interviews?

Candidates report that system design does come up, though the framing tends to be leadership-oriented rather than purely technical. You may be asked how you guided your team through a complex architecture decision or how you would approach a particular design problem, rather than being asked to whiteboard a low-level solution from scratch. Be ready to discuss distributed systems, streaming at scale, and reliability patterns at a conceptual level.

How important is streaming or media industry experience for this role?

You do not need prior streaming industry experience to be considered. What matters more is demonstrable experience leading teams that build and operate consumer-facing products at scale. Candidates who understand the challenges of high-concurrency systems or live event delivery will find it easier to give grounded answers to Disney's context-heavy questions, but strong general engineering leadership experience transfers well.

Which city has the most Engineering Manager openings in India right now?

Based on knok jobradar data as of early July 2026, Bangalore leads the Engineering Manager market in India with 182 openings across all companies, out of 975 total openings nationally. Disney currently has 24 open Engineering Manager roles in India. Bangalore is also typically the centre of Disney's India tech operations, making it the most active location to target.

How far in advance should I start preparing for a Disney EM interview?

Most candidates find two to three weeks of focused preparation sufficient if they are already working as an Engineering Manager. If you are stepping up from a senior individual contributor role, allow more time to develop strong leadership stories and to get comfortable framing decisions in terms of team and business impact. The preparation plan above gives a structured week-by-week approach you can follow.
