01 Overview

Overview

Apartment List is a US-based rental marketplace that connects renters with apartment listings and helps landlords fill vacancies. Their product team is known for a data-driven, user-research-first culture, and design work sits at the intersection of search, personalisation, and conversion. The company currently has 17 open roles on knok jobradar. Across the broader Indian market, there are 393 Product Designer openings, with Bangalore leading at 62, Delhi at 33, and Mumbai at 13.

Candidates report that Apartment List interviews test three things above all: your ability to frame design decisions in business and user outcomes, your comfort working through ambiguity, and how well you collaborate across product, engineering, and data. The process typically runs across four to five touchpoints, starting with a recruiter conversation, then a portfolio review with a designer or design manager, followed by cross-functional rounds with a PM and an engineer, and ending with a values or behavioral conversation.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

  1. Walk us through a project where you improved a search or discovery experience. What was your process, and how did the design perform after launch?
  1. Apartment List serves two audiences: renters looking for a home and landlords trying to fill units. How do you handle design decisions when their needs conflict?
  1. Tell us about a time you used data or user research to challenge an assumption your team held. What happened?
  1. How would you approach redesigning the Apartment List listing page to increase the number of renters who schedule a tour?
  1. Describe a project where you worked closely with engineers. How did you maintain design quality as the feature moved through development?
  1. Tell us about a situation where you had to cut scope in a design. How did you decide what stayed and what went?

  1. Describe a project that shipped and underperformed. What did you learn, and what would you do differently?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through a project where you improved a search or discovery experience.

*Situation:* I was a mid-level designer at a B2C platform where our search results page had a high drop-off rate, confirmed by session recordings and exit surveys showing users felt overwhelmed by the number of filter options available.

*Task:* My goal was to redesign the filter and sort system to reduce drop-off and increase the share of searches that led to a listing or product page visit.

*Action:* I ran five moderated usability tests with target users, mapped the filter taxonomy to what users actually cared about, and introduced progressive disclosure so advanced filters were tucked behind a 'More filters' option. I worked closely with engineering during QA to catch visual regressions, and I set up an A/B test with the PM before we rolled out fully.

*Result:* The A/B test ran for three weeks and showed a statistically significant improvement in search-to-detail-page rate, per our analytics dashboard. The variant became the new default, and I presented the findings to the wider product team.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you used data to challenge an assumption your team held.

*Situation:* Our team assumed that renters wanted more listing photos above anything else on the detail page, because that was the loudest piece of feedback showing up in app store reviews.

*Task:* Before committing six weeks of engineering work to a photo gallery redesign, I wanted to validate whether photos were actually the primary driver of tour bookings.

*Action:* I pulled heatmap and scroll data from our analytics tool. The data showed that users who read the neighbourhood details section converted to tour bookings at a higher rate than users who only browsed photos. I also ran a short survey with a small cohort of users and flagged the findings as directional given the sample size. I then proposed a quick experiment: a version of the page that elevated neighbourhood content alongside photos.

*Result:* The experiment ran for four weeks. The combined layout outperformed the photo-only variant on tour bookings, per our product analytics. The team shifted the roadmap to improve neighbourhood content alongside the photo work, rather than treating photos as the single priority.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you had to cut scope in a design. How did you decide what stayed and what went?

*Situation:* I was designing a renter onboarding flow. Three weeks before the planned launch, the engineering lead told me we had capacity for only half the screens we had designed.

*Task:* I needed to decide which parts of onboarding to keep, while still collecting enough data to personalise results on a user's first load.

*Action:* I listed every screen and sorted each into one of three categories: 'must have for personalisation to work,' 'nice to have for engagement,' and 'can be deferred to post-signup.' I shared this framework with the PM and engineer so the cut was transparent, not arbitrary. We agreed to ship the core preference-capture screens and move the preference-refinement step to a post-signup nudge instead.

*Result:* We shipped on time. The post-signup nudge showed solid early engagement in usability sessions, and the deferred screens became a follow-on project the next quarter.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For portfolio walkthroughs: Use the structure: problem, users affected, key constraints, your process, the one decision that mattered most, and the outcome. Candidates report that Apartment List interviewers push hardest on 'the decision that mattered most' because it reveals how you think, not just what you made. Keep the Situation brief and spend most of your time on Action and outcome.

For 'how would you redesign X' questions: Resist jumping to solutions. Start by naming what you would first need to learn: who the user is, what they are trying to do, and where the current experience is failing. Then offer one or two design directions with clear trade-offs. Interviewers are testing your process, not whether your answer is correct.

For behavioral questions: Use the STAR structure (Situation, Task, Action, Result). Keep Situation and Task short. Spend the bulk of your answer on Action and Result. Be specific: name the tool, the metric, the team member's role. Vague answers like 'I collaborated with the team' are, per recruiter feedback shared publicly, among the most common reasons candidates do not move forward.

For marketplace-specific questions: Apartment List is a two-sided marketplace. When discussing any design decision, name which side of the marketplace you were optimising for and acknowledge the trade-off for the other side. This signals product maturity that generic portfolio candidates often miss.

For metrics questions: You do not need to cite Apartment List's internal numbers. What matters is naming the right category of metric (conversion rate, retention, task completion time) and explaining why that metric reflects user value, not just business output.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Outcome orientation over craft pride. Apartment List's design team, candidates report, wants to see that your decisions connect to measurable results. A beautiful portfolio with no 'before and after' on key metrics is harder to evaluate than a simpler portfolio that shows clear impact.

Comfort with ambiguity. Rental search is complex and high-stakes. Interviewers want to see that you can scope a problem, make a call with incomplete information, and adjust when new data arrives. Candidates who wait for perfect requirements before starting design work tend not to advance, per shared interview experiences.

Cross-functional fluency. Product Designers at Apartment List work closely with PMs and engineers. Interviewers look for candidates who can describe how their process changes depending on who they are working with, and who have real examples of pushing back respectfully and reaching a better outcome.

User empathy grounded in research. Renting a home involves real stress: budget limits, moving timelines, unfamiliar cities. Interviewers want to see that you design for emotional as well as functional needs, and that you use concrete methods (usability testing, surveys, session recordings) to support your understanding of users.

Self-awareness about failure. Most interview loops include at least one question about a project that did not go as planned. Candidates who answer this honestly and show what they changed as a result tend to stand out from those who deflect or externalise blame.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Know the product. Spend at least one hour on Apartment List as a renter. Go through the search, filter, listing detail, and contact flow. Note where you feel friction or delight. Be ready to talk about what you observed and what you would explore if you joined the team.

Step 2: Select three portfolio case studies. Choose projects that show: (a) a complex search or discovery UX, (b) a data-informed design decision, and (c) a cross-functional collaboration story. For each, prepare a five-minute verbal walkthrough using the structure in the Answer Frameworks section above.

Step 3: Prepare STAR answers for eight to ten behavioral questions. Use the most-asked questions list above as your starting point. Write out full answers, then practise saying them out loud. Aim for two to three minutes per answer, not longer.

Step 4: Practise a live design exercise. Candidates report that some rounds include a live design prompt on a whiteboard or shared tool. Practise narrating your thinking as you sketch. Silence during a live exercise reads as uncertainty, even when you are thinking carefully.

Step 5: Prepare questions for your interviewers. Ask how the design team measures its own impact, how design decisions get escalated, and what the biggest open design challenge is right now. These questions show genuine interest and product maturity.

Step 6: Research Apartment List's recent direction. The company has evolved significantly in recent years. Showing awareness of their current strategy signals that you have done your homework and are not applying without thought.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating the portfolio walkthrough as a presentation. Interviewers want a conversation. Pause and invite questions. Candidates who talk for ten minutes without stopping often lose the interviewer's attention before reaching the most important parts of their work.

Skipping the problem framing. Jumping straight to your solution without explaining the problem, the users, and the constraints makes your design look like decoration rather than problem-solving. Always start with 'here is what we were trying to solve and for whom.'

Claiming impact without specifics. Saying 'the design improved conversion' without explaining what you measured, how, or what changed reads as unverified. If you do not have hard numbers, say the data was directional or from a small cohort. Honesty is more credible than vague credit-claiming.

Not knowing Apartment List's product. Interviewers notice immediately if you have never used the product. Set aside one hour before your interview to go through the renter experience end to end.

Jumping to solutions in 'how would you redesign X' questions. Lead with what you would need to learn before designing anything. Interviewers at product-led companies like Apartment List typically give lower marks to candidates who skip straight to solutions without first establishing the problem.

Underestimating the behavioral rounds. Candidates report that behavioral interviews carry significant weight at Apartment List. Weak answers can cost you the offer even if your portfolio is strong. Prepare specific stories, not general principles.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Apartment List Product Designer interview typically have?

Candidates report a process of typically four to five touchpoints: an initial recruiter conversation, a portfolio review with a designer or design manager, one or two cross-functional rounds with a PM and an engineer, and a final values or behavioral conversation. The exact structure can vary by team and role level. Always confirm the format with your recruiter at the start of the process so you can prepare accordingly.

Is there a take-home design assignment?

Some candidates report receiving a take-home case study prompt, while others go through a live design exercise instead. The format seems to depend on the specific team and role level. Ask your recruiter early whether there will be a take-home component so you can plan your time. Either way, practise narrating your design thinking out loud before any round that involves a design problem.

What salary can I expect as a Product Designer at Apartment List in India?

Apartment List is US-headquartered, and compensation for India-based or remote roles can vary significantly from local market bands. Based on knok jobradar data for the Indian market, mid-level Product Designers (3-5 years experience) typically see ranges of 14-24 LPA, while senior designers (6-9 years) see 26-40 LPA. For company-specific data points, check Glassdoor and levels.fyi, which carry user-reported salaries for Apartment List roles.

How important is domain knowledge in real estate or rentals?

You do not need prior experience in real estate or property tech. What matters is that you have used the Apartment List product, understand the two-sided marketplace dynamic (renters and landlords), and can empathise with a high-stakes user decision like choosing a home. Interviewers value curiosity and quick ramp-up speed over prior experience in this specific vertical.

Should I include side projects in my portfolio if my professional work is limited?

Yes, side projects are a good option if your professional work has gaps or if a personal project better demonstrates a relevant skill like search UX or mobile onboarding. Be ready to talk about the constraints and trade-offs you faced, even in personal work. A well-explained side project showing clear design thinking can be more compelling than a professional project presented without any context or outcome.
