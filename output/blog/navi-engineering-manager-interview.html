03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a time you had to rebuild trust with an underperforming engineer.

*Situation:* At my previous company, a senior engineer was missing deadlines and pulling back from team discussions. The rest of the team was quietly absorbing the extra load.

*Task:* I needed to address the performance issue without losing the engineer, who had strong domain knowledge, and without burning out the rest of the team.

*Action:* I set up a one-on-one to listen first rather than deliver feedback immediately. I discovered the engineer was dealing with a health issue and felt overwhelmed by unclear expectations. I worked with them to define a focused set of short-term goals, temporarily reduced their sprint commitments, and introduced weekly check-ins. I gave the team a brief, honest explanation that the workload redistribution had a defined plan and timeline behind it.

*Result:* Within two months the engineer was back to full capacity and went on to lead a critical payment module. The team's confidence in how leadership handled difficulty visibly improved.

---

Q: Navi moves fast and priorities shift. How do you keep your team focused during a major pivot?

*Situation:* At a previous fintech startup, our company pivoted from a consumer lending product to a B2B infrastructure product in the span of a few weeks, triggered by a regulatory change.

*Task:* I needed to re-align a team that had spent months building features that were now deprioritized, without losing morale or key people.

*Action:* I held a candid team meeting, explained what changed and why, and made space for frustration before moving to solutions. I then ran a skills-mapping exercise showing how each person's existing work translated directly into the new direction. I involved the team in breaking down the new roadmap rather than presenting a finished plan from above.

*Result:* We retained the full team through the pivot. Delivery on the new direction started within weeks, and the team's ownership of the roadmap made execution faster than a top-down plan would have.

---

Q: How do you manage technical debt while still delivering features at speed?

*Situation:* At my previous role we inherited a monolithic codebase that was slowing down feature delivery. Business pressure was high and there was no appetite for a pause on feature work.

*Task:* I needed to pay down debt incrementally without asking for dedicated debt-reduction sprints, which leadership would not approve.

*Action:* I introduced a practice where any engineer touching an existing module was expected to leave it measurably better than they found it, documented in the pull request. I created a lightweight debt register the whole team could contribute to, giving visibility without adding bureaucracy. I also negotiated a standing allocation in each sprint for debt work by framing it as a reliability and velocity investment rather than a cost.

*Result:* Over two quarters, deployment frequency improved and the incident rate on legacy modules dropped, based on team-tracked metrics. Leadership extended the allocation because they could see the velocity gain in delivery data.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for leadership and behavioral questions. Every leadership question at Navi can be structured as Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep the Situation brief (one or two sentences), spend the most time on the Action (what you specifically did, not what the team did), and close with a concrete Result, even if it is qualitative rather than numerical.

'Why, What, How' for strategy and decision questions. When asked about a decision you made, structure your answer around: why you identified the problem, what options you considered, and how you chose your path. This signals structured thinking without sounding scripted.

'People, Process, Product' as a completeness check. For questions about team health or delivery, interviewers want to see that you think across all three dimensions. Answering only on process (standups, sprint planning) without touching people (motivation, career growth) or product (clarity of outcome) leaves your answer feeling thin.

Calibration for the fintech context. Navi operates in a regulated industry where downtime and data errors have direct consequences for customers. When you answer questions about speed or delivery, add a sentence about how you maintained quality or compliance guardrails. Interviewers notice when candidates talk about velocity without acknowledging risk.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on candidate reports, Navi EM interviewers typically look for a consistent set of qualities.

Ownership without hand-holding. They want to see that you identify problems yourself, form a plan, and execute without waiting for direction from above. Stories where you spotted a gap the business had not yet named tend to resonate strongly.

People-first, but delivery-focused. Navi runs lean teams, so every engineer matters. Interviewers want to see that you invest in people's growth, but they also expect you to hold a high bar and not let chronic underperformance go unaddressed.

Comfort with ambiguity. Navi is a growth-stage company where processes change and priorities shift. Candidates who describe building structure from scratch, rather than applying a fixed playbook, tend to do better than those who rely on process familiarity alone.

Technical credibility. You do not need to write code day-to-day as an EM, but interviewers expect you to challenge technical decisions, understand system design trade-offs, and have a clear point of view on architecture. Vague or passive answers on technical topics are a red flag.

Regulatory and customer sensitivity. Navi serves retail financial customers. Interviewers look for candidates who understand that downtime, data issues, or compliance lapses affect real people, not just internal SLA dashboards.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Know the company before round one. Read Navi's public blog, any available product announcements, and publicly reported news about their product lines. Understand the engineering challenges across their core offerings: home loans, personal loans, health insurance, and mutual funds. Fintech engineering involves payments infrastructure, credit decisioning, regulatory reporting, and fraud controls. Know this landscape at a surface level before you walk in.

Build your story bank. Map your experience to five or six core themes: ownership, people leadership, technical decision-making, handling ambiguity, managing conflict, and delivery under pressure. Write each story in STAR format and practice it out loud, not just in your head.

Practice the hard questions. The questions in this guide reflect what candidates commonly report from Navi interviews. Practice each one with a target of two to three minutes per answer. Get feedback from someone who will tell you when your answer is vague or too long.

Review the JD before each round. Candidates report that Navi interviewers sometimes ask directly about responsibilities listed in the job description. Know which parts of your background map most clearly to each requirement before you join the call.

Prepare questions for the interviewer. Have two or three genuine questions ready about team structure, current engineering challenges, or how success is measured in the first six months. Candidates who have nothing to ask come across as disengaged.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Describing what the team did instead of what you did. Interviewers are assessing you. Use 'I' clearly when describing your decisions and actions, and reserve 'we' for context only.

Generic answers with no fintech context. Answers that could apply to any industry miss an opportunity. Navi deals with lending, insurance, and investments, which carry reliability and compliance expectations different from consumer apps or enterprise software. Bring that context into your answers wherever it fits naturally.

Sanitizing conflict out of your stories. Many candidates smooth over disagreements to avoid sounding difficult. Interviewers at growth-stage companies want to see that you can hold a position, push back when needed, and navigate disagreement without burning relationships. A story with real friction is more credible than one where everything went smoothly.

Dropping the result from your STAR answer. Answers that end without a clear result feel incomplete. Even if the outcome was mixed, own it and explain what you changed based on what you learned.

Over-indexing on process names. Mentioning agile, OKRs, or specific frameworks is fine, but interviewers want to see judgment, not a toolbox. Show that you know when a process is the right tool and when it just adds overhead.

Not asking questions at the end of a round. Candidates who have nothing to ask the interviewer come across as unprepared or disengaged. Prepare specific, genuine questions about the team, current challenges, or what the first few months look like.
