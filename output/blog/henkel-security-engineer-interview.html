01 Overview

Overview

Henkel is a German multinational known for adhesives, sealants, and consumer goods, with a significant manufacturing and R&D presence in India. Security Engineers at Henkel typically work across enterprise IT, cloud infrastructure, and operational technology (OT) environments. The company takes information security seriously given its global supply chain, regulated markets, and digital transformation agenda.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

The following questions are commonly reported by candidates interviewing for Security Engineer roles at Henkel. Exact questions vary by team and level, but these themes recur.

  1. Tell us about your experience with network security and segmentation in an enterprise environment.
  2. How have you handled a security incident from detection through to resolution?
  3. Walk us through your approach to third-party or vendor risk assessments.
  4. Which security frameworks or control standards have you worked with, and how did you apply them in a real project?
  5. Describe your experience with cloud security, particularly around misconfiguration detection and access control.
  6. How do you approach securing industrial control systems or OT environments?
  7. What is your process for conducting a vulnerability assessment and prioritizing remediation findings?
  8. How do you stay current with the threat landscape relevant to manufacturing or consumer goods companies?
  9. Describe a time you had to convince a business team to adopt a security control they initially resisted.
  10. How would you build a security awareness program for a workforce that is not primarily technical?
  11. What security tools or platforms have you used for SIEM, endpoint protection, or identity and access management?
  12. How do you balance security requirements with business continuity in a production or plant environment?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR method (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for behavioral questions. Here are three examples structured for Henkel interviews.

Q: How have you handled a security incident from detection through to resolution?

*Situation:* At my previous company, our SIEM flagged unusual lateral movement inside the corporate network late on a Friday evening.

*Task:* I was the on-call security engineer and needed to assess whether this was a real breach, contain it quickly, and communicate status to the CISO without causing unnecessary panic.

*Action:* I immediately isolated the affected VLAN using network segmentation controls already in place, pulled endpoint logs to identify the source process, and confirmed the activity was a compromised service account. I revoked the account credentials, patched the misconfigured service, and drafted a full incident timeline for the CISO before the shift ended.

*Result:* We contained the incident with no data exfiltration confirmed. The post-incident review led to tighter privilege controls on service accounts across the environment.

---

Q: Describe how you have dealt with a cloud security misconfiguration.

*Situation:* During a routine cloud posture review, I discovered a storage bucket in our dev environment had been left publicly accessible by a developer testing an integration.

*Task:* I needed to remediate the exposure immediately, check whether sensitive data had been accessed, and put a process in place to prevent recurrence.

*Action:* I locked down the bucket permissions promptly and enabled access logging. I then pulled the access logs to verify whether any unauthorized reads had occurred before the fix. I worked with the DevOps team to add a policy-as-code check in the CI/CD pipeline so public storage buckets would be flagged before deployment.

*Result:* No external access to sensitive data was confirmed. The pipeline check has since caught several similar misconfigurations automatically in the months that followed.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you had to convince a business team to adopt a security control.

*Situation:* A plant operations team was resisting a multi-factor authentication rollout because they felt it would slow down shift handovers on shared workstations.

*Task:* My job was to get MFA adopted without disrupting production workflows.

*Action:* I met with the plant manager and floor supervisors to understand exactly where the friction was. I proposed a phased rollout using hardware tokens for shared terminals rather than phone-based MFA, and ran a two-week pilot on a single production line. I collected feedback and adjusted the process before the full rollout.

*Result:* MFA was deployed across all plant workstations within the quarter with minimal complaints. The plant manager later cited the pilot approach as the reason the team trusted the change.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For behavioral questions: the STAR method works well. Candidates report that Henkel interviewers appreciate specificity: name the tool, the team, the outcome. Vague answers like 'I handled many incidents' do not land as well as a focused story with a clear result.

For technical questions: use an 'assess, plan, act, verify' flow. Explain how you would first understand the scope of the problem, outline your approach before diving into details, describe the specific actions you would take, and then explain how you would confirm the fix or control is working.

For framework or compliance questions: name the control family or standard clearly, for example NIST's Cybersecurity Framework or the CIS Critical Security Controls. Describe a real project where you applied it, and connect it to a business outcome such as reduced audit findings or faster incident response. Avoid reciting definitions. Interviewers at large enterprises typically want to know you have used these in a real environment, not just read about them.

For stakeholder questions: structure your answer around the gap between the business perspective and the security requirement, explain how you bridged that gap, and end with a measurable or observable outcome. This matters especially at Henkel, where security engineers work closely with plant managers, procurement teams, and global compliance functions.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Henkel is a manufacturing-first company, so security engineers are expected to understand both the IT and OT sides of the business. Based on what candidates report, interviewers look for the following qualities.

Practical incident response depth. Theoretical knowledge of frameworks is not enough. Interviewers want to hear how you have actually detected, contained, and documented incidents in a real environment.

Enterprise governance experience. Henkel operates across many countries and regulated sectors. Candidates who understand how security policies, vendor risk programs, and audit cycles work in a large organization stand out.

Communication with non-technical stakeholders. Security at a company like Henkel involves significant cross-functional work with plant managers, procurement teams, and business unit leads. Candidates who frame security in terms of business risk, not just technical controls, tend to resonate more with interviewers.

Cloud and identity security skills. Hands-on experience with cloud posture management, IAM, and endpoint protection tools is commonly expected. Be ready to name the specific platforms you have used and describe what you did with them.

OT or industrial security awareness. For roles supporting manufacturing plants, familiarity with industrial control systems and the specific challenges of securing them is a clear advantage. For corporate IT roles, this is more of a bonus than a hard requirement.

Cultural alignment. Henkel's stated values emphasize sustainability and responsibility. Candidates who position security as enabling business trust, rather than just blocking threats, tend to connect better with interviewers.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused two-to-three week plan covers the key areas for this role.

Week 1: Refresh core security knowledge. Review network segmentation, identity and access management, vulnerability management, and cloud security fundamentals. Revisit major control frameworks such as NIST's Cybersecurity Framework and the CIS Critical Security Controls. Be ready to describe how you have applied them in real projects, not just name them.

Week 2: Research Henkel and build your stories. Read Henkel's publicly available annual report and sustainability disclosures to understand how the company talks about digital trust and data protection. Look up publicly reported news about cybersecurity trends in the manufacturing sector to give your answers relevant context. Prepare two or three past-project examples that map to the common questions listed above.

Week 3: Practice out loud. Run through your STAR answers with a friend or record yourself. Tighten each answer to under three minutes. Prepare at least three genuine questions to ask the interviewer, such as how the security team is structured across global and India operations, which tools are currently in use, and what the biggest security challenges are for the team right now.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

  1. Focusing only on IT security. Candidates who do not acknowledge OT or industrial environments can come across as underprepared for a manufacturing company like Henkel. Even a brief mention of awareness around securing plant systems shows you understand the broader context.
  1. Naming frameworks without applying them. Saying you 'know' a control standard is far weaker than describing a specific project where you used it. Always follow a framework mention with a concrete example from your experience.
  1. Vague incident response answers. Interviewers want specifics: what you detected, how you contained it, and what changed afterward. Generic answers like 'I followed the incident response plan' do not demonstrate actual competence.
  1. Ignoring stakeholder communication. Security at an enterprise like Henkel involves significant cross-functional work. Candidates who only discuss technical controls and never mention how they communicated with business teams miss an important dimension that interviewers actively probe.
  1. Not asking questions at the end. Candidates who have no questions for the interviewer can appear disengaged. Prepare at least two genuine questions about the team's priorities, tooling, or structure before your interview.
  1. Overselling certifications without depth. Listing credentials is fine, but candidates report that interviewers quickly probe deeper. Be ready to back up any certification with practical examples from your work experience.

What is the typical interview process for a Security Engineer at Henkel?

Candidates report the process typically includes an initial HR screening call, followed by one or two technical rounds with the security team, and a final conversation with the hiring manager or a senior leader. Candidates report that scripting questions do come up, typically around Python or PowerShell for tasks like log parsing, alert triage, or automation of security checks. It depends on the specific team and the job description.
