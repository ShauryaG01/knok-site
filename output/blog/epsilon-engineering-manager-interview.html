03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a time you scaled an engineering team while maintaining delivery quality.

*Situation:* My team of six engineers was asked to absorb a new data ingestion service after an internal reorganisation. We needed to integrate a legacy system while continuing existing delivery commitments.

*Task:* I had to bring in three additional engineers within a quarter and ensure they could contribute quickly without slowing down ongoing sprints.

*Action:* I split the work into two parallel streams, each anchored by a senior engineer, so new hires could onboard without blocking delivery. I introduced structured onboarding with paired coding sessions, clear documentation sprints, and a weekly 'definition of done' review to keep quality consistent as the team grew.

*Result:* We completed hiring within the quarter and hit our delivery milestones on schedule. The integration shipped without a rollback, and team throughput improved noticeably in the following sprint cycle.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you pushed back on a business requirement.

*Situation:* The product team wanted to add a real-time personalisation feature two weeks before a major client demo. My team's sprint was already at full capacity, and the feature required changes to a core data model.

*Task:* I needed to communicate the risk clearly without creating friction, and find a path that protected both the demo and system stability.

*Action:* I prepared a short written risk assessment and shared it with the product manager and the VP of Engineering before any verbal escalation. I proposed a lightweight, read-only version of the feature for the demo, with the full implementation planned for the next sprint. I walked through exactly what a rushed build would risk: potential data inconsistencies and a likely hotfix cycle during the client visit.

*Result:* The product team accepted the alternative. The demo went well, the client was satisfied, and the full feature shipped cleanly in the following sprint with no rollbacks.

---

Q: How do you handle a situation where a high-performing engineer wants to leave?

*Situation:* One of my strongest backend engineers came to me after two years on the team to say she had received an external offer and was seriously considering it.

*Task:* My goal was to understand her real reasons for considering a move and see if there was a genuine path forward, without making promises I could not keep.

*Action:* I had a candid one-on-one focused not on matching the offer but on what she felt was missing. She wanted more architectural ownership and felt the role was too execution-focused. I proposed a formal tech lead scope expansion within her current role, connected her with the internal staff engineer pathway, and brought her into the next roadmap planning cycle as a full contributor.

*Result:* She chose to stay. Over the following months she led two major architecture decisions, and her engagement in quarterly pulse checks improved noticeably. She later became a strong internal advocate for the team.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Three frameworks cover most Engineering Manager interview questions at Epsilon.

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the workhorse for behavioural questions. Keep your Situation brief (two to three sentences), spend most of your time on Action (what you personally decided and did, not what the team did), and always close with a concrete Result. If you cannot share exact figures from a previous employer, describe the outcome in observable terms.

SCR (Situation, Complication, Resolution) is stronger for conflict and disagreement questions. Start with stable context, name the complication clearly, then explain your resolution. This structure signals that you understood the full picture before acting, which candidates report Epsilon interviewers typically respond to well.

Why, What, How works well for technical strategy and roadmap questions. Start with the business reason (why), then your decision (what), then execution and trade-offs (how). Epsilon interviewers often probe whether you can connect technical choices back to business outcomes, particularly client impact, so this structure signals the right instinct.

For questions about data platforms or marketing technology, be ready to speak to scale in terms of data volumes, latency requirements, or pipeline complexity. If you cannot share proprietary figures from a previous employer, frame the scale in relative terms or reference publicly reported benchmarks from comparable systems.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Candidates and hiring managers at Epsilon typically describe evaluation across four dimensions.

People-first leadership. Can you grow engineers, address underperformance with empathy, and retain talent in a competitive market? Epsilon teams value internal mobility and long tenure, so expect detailed questions about how you have developed individual careers, not just delivered projects.

Data fluency. Epsilon's core business is data-driven marketing at scale. You do not need to write code in the interview, but you should discuss data pipeline design, privacy compliance, and real-time processing trade-offs with confidence. Candidates who speak the language of data infrastructure tend to stand out.

Cross-functional collaboration. Candidates report that Epsilon places heavy weight on how engineering managers work with product, data science, and client-facing stakeholders. Come prepared with stories about navigating disagreements across teams and aligning on priorities when there is no obvious right answer.

End-to-end delivery ownership. Epsilon values managers who take accountability from planning through deployment and incident response. Stories where you personally owned the outcome, not just the process, resonate strongly with Epsilon interviewers.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused two to three week plan, based on what candidates typically report about the Epsilon interview process.

Week 1: Understand Epsilon's business and technology context. Read about Epsilon's core offerings: their loyalty and CRM platforms, their data network, and their work on personalisation at enterprise scale. Understand where engineering sits in their business model. Identify two to three strong stories from your past experience in data-heavy or platform-scale environments.

Week 2: Practise your leadership stories. Write out answers to the 12 questions above using the STAR format. Focus especially on stories about hiring, conflict resolution, technical roadmaps, and talent development. Practise saying them out loud so they sound natural rather than scripted.

Week 3: Technical and system design revision. Epsilon Engineering Manager interviews typically include a round where you discuss the architecture of systems your previous teams built. Revise distributed systems concepts, data pipeline design, API design, and observability at a conceptual level. Focus on trade-offs rather than implementation syntax.

Before each round: Prepare two to three questions for your interviewer. Ask about the team's current technical challenges, how success is measured for a new Engineering Manager in the first six months, or what the roadmap looks like for the next two quarters.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Using 'we' instead of 'I'. The most common mistake Engineering Manager candidates make is describing what the team did rather than what they personally decided. Interviewers are evaluating your judgement, not your team's output. Use 'I decided', 'I pushed back', 'I restructured the sprint.'

Skipping the result. Many candidates narrate a situation in detail but trail off before stating the outcome. Always close your story. If the result was mixed or partial, say so honestly and add what you learned from it.

Underselling people management skills. Candidates who came up through the individual contributor track often over-index on technical stories. Epsilon interviewers want to see that you can grow people and handle difficult conversations. Balance your technical stories with at least two strong people management examples.

Assuming you know the process. Epsilon's interview structure reportedly varies by team, location, and hiring manager. Do not assume a fixed number of rounds or a specific order. Confirm the current format with your recruiter before each step.

Generic answers without grounding. Responses like 'I always prioritise communication' without a real example do not hold up under follow-up questions. Every answer should be anchored in a specific situation from your own experience.
