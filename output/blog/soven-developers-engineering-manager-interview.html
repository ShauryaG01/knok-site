02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions reflect patterns seen across Engineering Manager interviews at growth-stage tech companies. Candidates report that panels of this type combine behavioural and situational questions, with a strong emphasis on delivery ownership and people management.

  1. Walk us through a time you delivered a complex project under a tight deadline. What did you protect and what did you cut?
  2. How do you manage an engineer who is consistently missing expectations? Walk through your process step by step.
  3. Soven is scaling quickly. How would you hire and ramp up three new engineers in 60 days without dropping delivery velocity?
  4. Describe a situation where engineering and product disagreed sharply on priority. How did you resolve it?
  5. How do you balance shipping new features against clearing technical debt? Give a specific example.
  6. Tell us about a production incident you owned as an engineering manager. What did you do first and what changed afterward?
  7. How do you run 1-on-1s? What do you track and what do you follow up on?
  8. Describe the most technically complex system your team has owned. How did you stay credible in architecture reviews without becoming a bottleneck?
  9. How do you build a culture of quality and ownership in a team that is moving quickly?
  10. Tell us about a time you gave difficult feedback to a senior engineer. How did you prepare and how did they respond?
  11. How do you approach engineering roadmap planning? How far ahead do you plan and how do you communicate changes to stakeholders?
  12. What metrics do you use to measure the health of your engineering team and your delivery pipeline?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use these three examples as a starting template. Replace the details with your own experience and keep the same structure.

Q: How do you handle an underperforming engineer?

*Situation:* 'I had a mid-level engineer who had been strong for two years but started missing deadlines and disengaging from code reviews over several weeks.'

*Task:* 'My job was to find the root cause, be direct about the impact, and give this person a genuine chance to turn things around before escalating.'

*Action:* 'I set aside time in our next 1-on-1 and named the specific pattern, not a vague 'I have concerns' conversation. It turned out the engineer was overwhelmed by an ambiguous project scope and felt too senior to ask for help. We rewrote the project brief together, set fortnightly check-ins, and I connected them with a staff engineer for pairing on the hardest parts.'

*Result:* 'Delivery was back on track within about six weeks. That engineer later became one of the strongest advocates for psychological safety on the team. The takeaway: underperformance is almost always a signal, not a character flaw.'

---

Q: Tell us about a production incident you owned as an EM.

*Situation:* 'Our payments service went down during a high-traffic period. I was the on-call engineering manager that week.'

*Task:* 'Keep the team calm, coordinate the response, keep stakeholders informed, and fix the root cause rather than just patching the symptom.'

*Action:* 'I opened a dedicated incident channel immediately. I split the team: two engineers on root cause, one assessing customer impact, one drafting stakeholder updates. I gave business leaders plain-English status updates at regular intervals so they were not pulling engineers into side conversations. Once we identified a misconfigured timeout on a third-party integration, I had the engineer walk me through the fix before we deployed it.'

*Result:* 'Service was restored and we ran a blameless post-mortem two days later. We added a canary deployment step that caught several similar misconfigurations in the following quarter.'

---

Q: How do you balance technical debt against feature delivery?

*Situation:* 'At my previous company, accumulated debt in our notification service was causing weekly minor incidents and adding meaningful overhead to every new feature. Leadership wanted three features shipped that quarter.'

*Task:* 'Make the case for allocating some sprint capacity to debt without delaying the committed roadmap.'

*Action:* 'I quantified the cost of the debt in engineering hours over the previous quarter and translated that into equivalent sprint capacity we had lost. I proposed a fixed sprint allocation to debt reduction alongside the feature work, and showed that the net effect would speed up feature delivery by mid-quarter. Critically, I named two specific debt items rather than asking for vague cleanup time.'

*Result:* 'Leadership approved the plan. We hit all three feature commitments and incident frequency dropped significantly by end of quarter. That approach became our standard template for debt conversations.'

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Two frameworks work well for Engineering Manager interviews. Use them as a thinking structure, not a rigid script.

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result), for behavioural questions

Situation: Set context briefly, in one or two sentences. Task: State what you were specifically responsible for. Action: Spend most of your time here. Be specific about what YOU decided and did, not what the team did. Use 'I' not 'we' when describing decisions. Result: Describe the outcome concretely. If you have real metrics, use them. If not, describe the qualitative change clearly.

The most common trap: candidates spend most of the answer on Situation and rush through Action. Interviewers are evaluating how you think and decide, not just what the context was.

Situation-Complication-Resolution, for strategic or process questions

Useful when the question is 'how do you approach X' rather than 'tell me about a time.' Start with the normal state (Situation), introduce the constraint or tension that made it hard (Complication), then walk through your reasoning and decision (Resolution). This format shows you understand trade-offs, not just outcomes.

A note on level calibration: For Manager-level roles (35-60 LPA on current market data), interviewers expect team-level ownership examples. For Senior Manager roles (55-90 LPA), they expect examples spanning multiple teams or cross-functional decisions. Calibrate the scope of your stories to the level you are targeting.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Engineering Manager interviews at companies like Soven Developers typically look for four qualities, roughly in this order of weight.

Delivery credibility. Can you ship? Have you owned a roadmap, managed cross-team dependencies, and hit commitments under real constraints? Interviewers want specific examples, not process descriptions. If you only describe what you would do rather than what you have done, you will not clear this bar.

People leadership depth. Have you coached someone through a real performance problem? Have you hired and built a team, not just inherited one? Shallow answers here are among the most common rejection reasons at this level.

Technical credibility. You do not need to write production code in the interview, but you must hold a system design or architecture conversation with conviction. Interviewers want evidence that your team trusts your technical judgment.

Stakeholder communication. Engineering Managers at this level interact regularly with product, business, and sometimes customers. Interviewers will notice whether you communicate trade-offs clearly or retreat into jargon.

A pattern candidates report from similar panels: how you talk about your team matters as much as what you say. Managers who claim sole credit for team wins, or who deflect blame entirely onto others, raise red flags about self-awareness.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused two-week plan for Soven Developers Engineering Manager interview preparation.

Week 1: Build your story bank

Write out five to seven specific stories from your career covering: a delivery under pressure, a direct performance conversation, a technical decision you influenced, a stakeholder conflict you navigated, and a team-building win. Map each story to the STAR structure and know the key facts and outcome cold. Do not memorise scripts.

Week 2: Practise and research

Do two or three mock interviews with a peer or mentor who will interrupt and ask 'what did YOU specifically decide?' That question exposes gaps in most EM stories. Look up Soven Developers on LinkedIn and Glassdoor to understand their current team structure, recent hires, and any published engineering content. Note their open roles and think about what problems those hires are meant to solve.

Before the interview

Prepare three questions for the panel. Strong choices: 'What does success look like for this role in the first 90 days?', 'What is the biggest engineering challenge the team is navigating right now?', and 'How does engineering leadership work with product at Soven?' These show you think like a leader, not just a candidate.

On the day

Candidates report that EM panels at companies of this type often ask for specific examples early. Anchor your first answer in a real situation from the first sentence rather than waiting to be pushed.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

These mistakes come up repeatedly in Engineering Manager interviews.

Talking about the team instead of yourself. 'We shipped on time' tells the interviewer nothing about your contribution. Name your specific decisions and actions.

Giving process answers instead of experience answers. 'I would hold a retrospective' is not a story. 'Here is what happened when I held a retrospective after a missed deadline' is. Always use the real example.

Skipping the result. Many candidates give a rich Situation and Action but trail off at the end. Interviewers use results to calibrate scope and impact. Even a qualitative result is better than none.

Overclaiming technical depth. If you have been out of hands-on coding for a few years, own that clearly. Interviewers at Manager level are looking for technical credibility in system design and judgment, not live coding ability. Claiming you still write production code when you do not will unravel during the technical discussion.

Being unprepared on compensation. Know your number before the interview. The current market for Engineering Manager roles runs 35-60 LPA at the Manager level on current market data. Anchor to your target range confidently and connect it to your scope and team size.

Asking no questions. Candidates who skip the closing question round signal low engagement. Three prepared questions (see preparation plan above) change the tone of the conversation.
