01 Overview

Overview

Toast is a US-based restaurant technology company whose Android apps power point-of-sale terminals, handheld ordering devices, and kitchen displays in restaurants across North America. Android Engineers at Toast work on software that runs in demanding, real-world environments where reliability is not optional: a crash during the dinner rush has immediate business impact.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates report Toast interviewers focus heavily on real-world reliability, offline behavior, and hardware integration. These are the questions most commonly discussed in prep communities and interview debriefs.

  1. Walk me through how the Android activity and fragment lifecycle works, and describe a lifecycle-related bug you have fixed.
  2. How would you architect an offline-first feature for a restaurant POS app where network connectivity is unreliable?
  3. Describe your approach to handling background work and data synchronization in Android (WorkManager, coroutines, etc.).
  4. How have you optimized an Android app running on constrained hardware with limited RAM?
  5. Explain how you would integrate with a hardware peripheral (like a card reader or receipt printer) from an Android app.
  6. Describe a production crash you debugged from end to end. What tools did you use and what did you find?
  7. How do you write and structure unit tests and UI tests for Android? What is your philosophy on test coverage?
  8. Walk me through your experience with Kotlin coroutines or RxJava for asynchronous operations.
  9. How do you handle state management across configuration changes and process death?
  10. Describe a time you had to push back on a technical decision. How did you handle the disagreement?
  11. How do you keep up with Android SDK changes, new APIs, and deprecations in a fast-moving codebase?
  12. If a restaurant staff member reports that the app 'freezes during peak hours,' how would you investigate and fix it?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for behavioral and technical-experience questions. Here are three model answers tailored to Toast's context.

Q: Describe a production crash you debugged from end to end.

*Situation:* On a food-delivery app I worked on, users reported the app crashing on a specific device model during checkout. Crashlytics showed the crash was happening consistently but only on devices running a particular OEM skin.

*Task:* I was responsible for identifying the root cause and shipping a fix quickly because the crash was blocking purchases.

*Action:* I used Crashlytics to isolate the stack trace, then reproduced the issue locally using an emulator with that OEM configuration. I found that the OEM had overridden a system animation setting in a way that conflicted with our custom transition. I replaced the custom transition with a standard one and added a device-capability check so the fallback applied automatically.

*Result:* The crash rate in Crashlytics dropped to near zero within one release cycle, and we added the OEM check to our device compatibility checklist so the team would catch similar issues earlier.

---

Q: How would you architect an offline-first feature for a POS app?

*Situation:* At a previous company, we built a mobile ordering feature for a retail client whose store Wi-Fi was inconsistent. Orders needed to work even without connectivity.

*Task:* I led the design of the offline sync layer.

*Action:* I proposed a local Room database as the source of truth, with a sync queue for pending operations. When the device reconnected, WorkManager would process the queue in order, using exponential backoff on failures. I also added conflict resolution logic so server changes took precedence over stale local data.

*Result:* The feature handled network drops gracefully in QA testing and received positive feedback from store staff during the pilot, who noted they no longer had to restart transactions when Wi-Fi dropped.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you pushed back on a technical decision.

*Situation:* My team lead proposed caching all product images in memory to speed up a catalog screen. I was concerned this would cause out-of-memory crashes on lower-end devices.

*Task:* I needed to raise the concern without blocking the sprint.

*Action:* I put together a quick comparison: in-memory caching versus using Glide's disk cache with a size limit. I showed the team how the disk-cache approach gave similar perceived performance on our target devices while avoiding memory pressure. I framed it as a tradeoff discussion rather than a veto.

*Result:* The team adopted the disk-cache approach. We saw no OOM crashes in the subsequent release, and the lead appreciated that I brought data rather than just an opinion.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For system design questions, structure your answer in four steps. First, clarify constraints: device type, connectivity assumptions, expected data volume. Second, define the data layer (local database, sync strategy). Third, describe the UI layer (how state flows to the screen). Fourth, explain failure handling (what happens when sync fails or the device restarts mid-operation). Toast's hardware context means always discussing offline behavior, even when the question does not mention it explicitly.

For Android fundamentals questions, show depth by going beyond the 'what' to the 'why.' If asked about ViewModels, explain not just that they survive configuration changes, but why that matters on a device that might be rotated or handed between staff members mid-shift.

For debugging questions, follow a structured narrative: symptom, reproduction, root cause, fix, prevention. Mention specific tools you used (Crashlytics, Android Profiler, Logcat, Systrace) rather than speaking generically.

For behavioral questions, use STAR cleanly. Keep the Situation short (one or two sentences), spend most of your time on Action (what you personally did, not what 'we' did), and make the Result concrete even if you cannot cite exact figures.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Reliability mindset. Toast apps run in restaurants where downtime has real consequences. Interviewers want to see that you think about edge cases, error states, and graceful degradation, not just the happy path.

Hardware awareness. Toast builds on dedicated Android hardware. Candidates who understand constraints like limited RAM, fixed screen sizes, and peripheral integration stand out over those who only know consumer app development.

Ownership. Candidates report that Toast values engineers who take end-to-end responsibility, from writing the code to monitoring it in production. Mention how you have used crash reporting, profiling, or analytics to validate your work after shipping.

Clear communication. Restaurant technology serves non-technical users (servers, cooks, managers). Interviewers often probe whether you think about the end user's experience, not just the code architecture.

Collaborative pushback. Based on publicly available engineering blog posts, Toast's engineering culture values respectful disagreement. Showing that you can advocate for a technical position while remaining open to other views is a positive signal.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Android core review

Revisit activity and fragment lifecycle, ViewModel, LiveData or StateFlow, and Room. Build one small offline-first demo app from scratch. Practice explaining your decisions out loud as you build, as if talking through them with an interviewer.

Week 2: System design and hardware context

Study common POS and offline-sync patterns. Read Android's official guide on data and file storage, background processing, and WorkManager. Research how Android apps communicate with USB and Bluetooth peripherals. Prepare a system design answer for 'design an offline order-taking app.'

Week 3: Behavioral prep and mock interviews

Write out five STAR stories covering: a production incident, a technical disagreement, a performance optimization, a cross-team collaboration, and a feature you are proud of. Record yourself answering them to check for filler words and clarity.

Week 4: Company research and final practice

Search for 'Toast engineering blog' to read about their product thinking. Understand their product suite: POS, handheld ordering, kitchen display systems. Do at least two timed mock interviews, ideally with a peer who can give feedback. Review your weakest area from Weeks 1 and 2.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Ignoring offline scenarios. Many candidates design features assuming reliable connectivity. For Toast specifically, this is a significant gap. Always address what happens when the network drops.

Vague debugging answers. Saying 'I looked at the logs and fixed it' is not enough. Interviewers want to hear which tools you used, what the stack trace told you, and how you confirmed the fix worked.

Skipping the Result in STAR answers. Candidates often describe what they did but not what happened afterward. Even a qualitative result ('the team adopted the approach' or 'no further crashes were reported') is stronger than no result at all.

Over-engineering system design. Toast interviewers, based on candidate reports, prefer practical solutions over elaborate distributed architectures. Start simple, then add complexity only when the interviewer asks for it.

Not asking clarifying questions. Jumping straight into an answer without clarifying the problem scope reads as impulsive. Ask one or two focused questions before you start designing or coding.

Generic Android answers. Answers that could apply to any consumer app do not stand out. Tie your experience to reliability, hardware constraints, or user environments similar to restaurants whenever you can.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many interview rounds does Toast typically have for Android Engineers?

Candidates report the process typically includes a recruiter screen, one or two technical rounds covering coding and Android fundamentals or system design, and a behavioral round. The exact number of rounds can vary by team and location. Remote and international candidates sometimes have an additional round to assess communication and async collaboration style.

Does Toast ask live coding questions or take-home assignments?

Candidates report both formats have been used, depending on the team and the interviewer's preference. Live coding rounds typically focus on data structures, algorithms, or Android-specific problems. Take-home assignments, when given, typically involve building a small Android feature or debugging a provided codebase. Confirm the format with your recruiter so you can prepare accordingly.

What Android topics should I prioritize for a Toast interview?

Based on candidate reports, the most important areas are Android lifecycle management, offline-first architecture (Room, WorkManager, sync strategies), Kotlin coroutines, and performance profiling. Because Toast runs on dedicated hardware, understanding memory constraints and peripheral integration is a bonus that not all candidates bring to the table.

Is Kotlin required, or will Java answers be accepted?

Kotlin is the standard for Android development and candidates report Toast interviews expect Kotlin fluency. You can mention Java experience for context, but your code examples and explanations should be in Kotlin. If you are stronger in Java, spend time getting comfortable with Kotlin coroutines and extension functions before your interview date.

How important is system design for an Android Engineer role at Toast?

Candidates report system design questions appear more frequently at mid-to-senior levels. For junior roles, the focus is more on Android fundamentals and coding. For senior roles, expect to design an entire feature including the data layer, sync strategy, and error handling. Practice designing offline-first systems since that maps directly to Toast's product context.
