03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a time you diagnosed and fixed a critical production issue under pressure.

*Situation:* At my previous company, an order-processing service began throwing server errors shortly after a deployment, blocking customers from completing purchases.

*Task:* I was on call and needed to identify the root cause quickly and either roll back or patch the service.

*Action:* I pulled the application logs and noticed that a configuration value for a downstream payment API endpoint had not been updated in the environment variables during the deployment. I confirmed the issue in the staging environment, updated the config, redeployed, and monitored the error rate in our alerting dashboard until it returned to a healthy baseline.

*Result:* The service recovered within a short window. I then added a pre-deployment config validation step to our CI pipeline so the same class of error would surface before reaching production in future.

---

Q: Describe a time you significantly improved the performance of a slow database query.

*Situation:* Our product listing page was loading slowly and user complaints were escalating to the support team.

*Task:* I was asked to investigate the backend and reduce the response time for that endpoint.

*Action:* I used the database query explain plan to find a full table scan on an orders table with no index on the filter column. I added a composite index on the columns used in the WHERE and ORDER BY clauses, tested on a copy of the production dataset, and deployed during a low-traffic window.

*Result:* Query execution time dropped by a large margin, bringing page response time within an acceptable range. I documented the index strategy in our internal wiki so the team could apply the same approach to other slow queries.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you had to pick up a new technology quickly to deliver a project.

*Situation:* Our team decided to migrate a batch job from a cron script to a managed workflow orchestration tool. No one on the team had used it before.

*Task:* I volunteered to lead the migration and had two weeks to deliver a working prototype.

*Action:* I spent the first few days reading the official documentation and running the quickstart examples. I then mapped our existing job steps to the new tool's DAG structure, wrote unit tests for each task, and set up a local development environment so teammates could review the code easily.

*Result:* The prototype was ready on time and passed code review with minor feedback. The migration reduced manual intervention in our batch pipeline and made failed steps much easier to retry without re-running the entire job.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For system design questions: Start with requirements (who uses the system, what scale is expected, what consistency guarantees matter). Sketch the major components, explain data flow, then discuss trade-offs. For a health-tech company like PlatinumRx, always mention how you would handle prescription data sensitivity and audit logging.

For behavioural questions: Use the STAR format. Situation (one or two sentences of context), Task (what you were responsible for), Action (specific steps you took), Result (what changed). Keep it first-person and concrete. Avoid saying 'we did everything' without explaining your specific contribution.

For debugging questions: Walk through your mental model: reproduce the issue, isolate the component, read the logs, form a hypothesis, test it, fix, verify, prevent recurrence. Interviewers want to see a structured process, not just the lucky answer.

For trade-off questions: State the options, list pros and cons of each, then explain which you would choose given the constraints. Showing you can hold two valid approaches in mind at the same time signals seniority.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Candidates for health-tech backend roles typically need to show three things.

Domain awareness. PlatinumRx deals with prescriptions, regulated data, and order fulfilment. Interviewers want to see that you understand why data integrity, audit trails, and reliability matter in this space, even if you have not worked in pharma before.

Engineering fundamentals. A solid grasp of REST API design, relational databases, indexing, and service communication patterns is expected. Candidates report that system design questions often involve order workflows or notification pipelines.

Ownership mindset. Health-tech systems affect real patients. Interviewers look for engineers who think about failure modes, write tests, set up monitoring, and care about what happens after the code ships.

Collaboration and communication. With 5 open roles, PlatinumRx is growing its engineering team. Expect questions about how you have worked across teams, handled disagreements, and onboarded to new codebases.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Coding and fundamentals

Practise data structures and algorithms on a platform like LeetCode. Focus on arrays, hashmaps, trees, and graph traversal, as these are commonly cited in health-tech backend interviews. Revisit SQL: joins, aggregations, indexing, and query explain plans.

Week 2: System design

Study common backend patterns: rate limiting, idempotency, event-driven architecture, and distributed transactions. Practise designing an order management system or a notification service end to end. Read about how e-commerce and pharmacy platforms typically handle inventory and prescription workflows.

Week 3: Behavioural and company research

Prepare four to six STAR stories covering: a production incident you handled, a performance improvement you drove, a technical disagreement you resolved, and a time you learned something new quickly. Research PlatinumRx's app and products and any public engineering content they have published.

Days before the interview

Review your resume line by line. Every project or metric you have listed is a question target. Practise talking through your last two roles in two minutes each. Confirm the interview format with the recruiter so there are no surprises on the day.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping to solutions in system design. Candidates who start drawing architecture without clarifying requirements often design for the wrong problem. Spend the first few minutes asking about scale, consistency needs, and constraints.

Vague STAR answers. Saying 'I worked with my team to fix a bug' is not a STAR answer. Interviewers want to know what you specifically did, not what the team did collectively.

Ignoring failure handling. In health-tech, a system that does not account for partial failures (a payment gateway going down, a prescription service timing out) is a red flag. Always mention retries, fallbacks, or dead-letter queues where relevant.

Not asking questions at the end. Candidates who have no questions signal low interest. Prepare two or three genuine questions about the team's tech stack, on-call culture, or how engineering collaborates with product.

Underestimating the HR round. Culture and values questions are not a formality at companies in growth mode. Prepare honest, specific answers about why you want to work in health-tech and what kind of team environment brings out your best work.
