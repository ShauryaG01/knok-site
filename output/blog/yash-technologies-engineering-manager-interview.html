03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use STAR format for every behavioural question: lay out the Situation, Task, Action, and Result clearly. Keep each answer to two or three minutes when spoken aloud.

Q: How do you handle an underperforming engineer on a client-facing project?

*Situation:* At a previous employer, a senior developer on a banking integration project was missing sprint commitments and producing code with frequent defects. The client was flagging concerns in weekly status calls.

*Task:* I needed to close the performance gap without losing the engineer's domain knowledge mid-project, while protecting the client relationship and team morale.

*Action:* I had a private one-on-one to understand the root cause. It turned out to be a skill gap with a new REST API stack the team had recently adopted. I paired the engineer with a more experienced colleague for focused knowledge transfer, set clear short-term milestones, and held weekly check-ins to track progress. I gave the project sponsor a general risk flag without naming the individual.

*Result:* Output improved steadily over the following sprints. The project delivered on schedule, and the engineer later became the team's go-to person for that integration stack.

---

Q: Describe a time scope creep threatened a project.

*Situation:* On an ERP rollout for a manufacturing client, the client began requesting additions after the statement of work was signed. Each request felt small individually, but collectively they were putting the timeline and team bandwidth at serious risk.

*Task:* I had to protect the original delivery commitment while keeping the client engaged and the relationship intact.

*Action:* I documented every new request in a shared change log with an estimated impact on timeline and effort. For each request, I gave the client two clear options: defer it to a Phase 2 scope, or approve a formal change order with adjusted timelines and cost. I set up a standing scope review call with the client's project sponsor to keep the conversation structured rather than ad hoc.

*Result:* Core scope delivered on time. Several deferred items became the basis for a follow-on engagement, which benefited both the client and the business.

---

Q: Tell us about a conflict between your engineering team and a client stakeholder.

*Situation:* My team proposed refactoring a legacy component into a service-based architecture. The client's IT director pushed back, citing concerns about operational complexity and risk to ongoing support.

*Task:* I had to find a path that respected my team's technical judgment and the client's legitimate risk concerns, without the conversation becoming adversarial.

*Action:* I proposed a joint working session where the team walked through a phased approach: deliver the immediate feature on the existing architecture, then pilot the new design on one low-risk module. I also prepared a short written summary of the risks and benefits for the client director, framed around their specific concerns.

*Result:* The client approved the pilot. It ran without issues, and the director later approved full migration. The team felt their recommendation was taken seriously, and the client felt their concerns were respected.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Most Engineering Manager interview questions at Yash fall into three types, and a different framing works best for each.

Behavioural questions ('Tell me about a time...') respond best to STAR: Situation (one or two sentences of context), Task (what you personally were responsible for), Action (the specific steps you took, not what 'the team' did), Result (a concrete outcome, even if qualitative). Spend the most time on Action. Interviewers at IT services firms like Yash are particularly interested in how you navigated client relationships and resource constraints, so work those details into your Situation and Action.

Situational or hypothetical questions ('What would you do if...') are best answered by briefly stating your principle, then walking through the steps you would take. Ground it in a real past experience if you can: 'I would approach it the way I did on a similar situation at...' This shows judgment rather than just theory.

Technical and process questions ('How do you run delivery at scale?') benefit from a structured problem, approach, trade-offs format. Name the approach you favour, explain why it fits the IT-services delivery model, and acknowledge what it does not solve. Interviewers at firms like Yash value pragmatism over textbook methodology.

Across all formats: use 'I' not 'we' when describing your actions, keep results honest (qualitative outcomes are fine), and connect your answers to the kind of work Yash does: enterprise programs, long client relationships, and distributed delivery teams.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates typically report from Yash Technologies EM interviews, the panel looks for a specific combination of qualities.

Delivery credibility. Can you show that you have shipped complex projects in a services or consulting environment? Interviewers want concrete evidence: programs you owned end to end, risks you caught early, and clients you kept satisfied under pressure. Theoretical answers about frameworks matter less than proof you have done the work.

People leadership depth. Yash runs large delivery teams. They want to see that you have had hard conversations with engineers, made calls on performance, and helped people grow. Generic answers about 'creating a positive culture' do not land well. Specific stories about individuals do.

Client and stakeholder management. In an IT services firm, the EM role is often as much about managing upward and outward as it is about managing the team. Interviewers test whether you can hold a client relationship together when things go sideways, and whether you know how to escalate without creating panic.

Process maturity without rigidity. They look for candidates who can set up reliable delivery rhythms but adapt them when the client context demands it. Candidates who are dogmatic about a single methodology tend to struggle in services environments where client contracts and culture vary widely.

Communication clarity. Your ability to explain complex technical trade-offs to non-technical stakeholders comes up in almost every round. Practise framing technical decisions in terms of business risk, timeline, and cost.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused plan for Yash Technologies Engineering Manager interviews, typically covering two to three weeks:

Build your story bank first. Before doing anything else, write out five to seven stories from your career that demonstrate delivery leadership, people decisions, client management, and handling failure. Each story needs a clear situation, a decision point, the specific action you took, and the outcome. These stories are the raw material for almost every behavioural question you will face.

Research Yash Technologies specifically. Look at their publicly available case studies, LinkedIn posts from Yash leaders, and any recent coverage of their service lines. Understanding their focus areas (SAP, Oracle, managed services, digital transformation) lets you connect your experience to their work rather than speaking in generic IT terms.

Practise out loud, not just in your head. STAR answers that feel clear when you think them often run too long or lose structure when spoken. Record yourself answering the top questions from the list above, play it back, and cut anything that takes more than three minutes.

Prepare questions for the panel. Ask about team size and structure, how the EM role interfaces with client delivery leads, what a strong first few months looks like for a new hire, and what the biggest delivery challenges the team is currently navigating. Good questions signal genuine interest and help you evaluate fit.

On the day. Arrive or join a few minutes early. If a question catches you off guard, it is perfectly fine to say 'let me think about that for a moment' before answering. Interviewers at this level expect composure under uncertainty.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

These are the mistakes that most often cost candidates Engineering Manager offers at IT services firms like Yash.

Speaking in 'we' instead of 'I'. It is natural to talk about team achievements, but interviewers need to understand your specific contribution. Replace 'we delivered' with 'I set up the process that allowed the team to deliver.'

Skipping the result. Many candidates give a detailed Situation and Action but trail off before the outcome. Even a qualitative result ('the client extended the contract,' 'the engineer was promoted the following cycle') is far stronger than leaving the story unfinished.

Generic answers about methodology. Saying 'I use agile' or 'I believe in servant leadership' without a specific example signals that you are describing an aspiration rather than a practice. Every methodology claim needs a real story attached.

Underplaying conflict and failure. Interviewers at this level are not looking for a perfect track record. They want to see how you handle adversity. Candidates who only describe successes come across as either risk-averse or less than candid. Prepare at least one story where things went wrong and you had to course-correct.

Not asking questions at the end. Candidates who have nothing to ask signal low engagement. Prepare two or three genuine questions about the team, the work, or the challenges the role is expected to solve.

Treating every round the same. An HR screener wants a career summary and salary alignment. A delivery-focused panel wants project depth. A senior leader wants strategic thinking and client instincts. Adjust your emphasis based on who is in the room.
