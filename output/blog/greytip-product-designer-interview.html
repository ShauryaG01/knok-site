01 Overview

Overview

Greytip Software is a Bengaluru-based HR-tech company best known for greytHR, a payroll and HR management platform used by small and mid-sized businesses across India. With 78 open roles as of July 2026, greytip is one of the more active product employers in the Indian HR-tech space.

Product Designer interviews at greytip typically span multiple rounds: a portfolio review, a design task or challenge, and a final conversation with the team or hiring manager. Candidates report that greytip places strong weight on your ability to design for non-technical users, since most greytHR users are HR managers, payroll admins, and employees accessing self-service features on everyday devices under deadline pressure.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

The questions below reflect the kinds of problems greytip designers solve daily, based on candidate reports. Treat them as a strong starting point.

  1. Walk us through a project where you improved a complex workflow for a non-technical user.
  2. How do you approach designing for users with low digital literacy or limited tech comfort?
  3. greytHR is used by HR admins who run payroll every month. How would you redesign the payroll run flow to reduce errors?
  4. Tell me about a time you pushed back on a stakeholder request because it conflicted with user needs.
  5. How do you prioritise features when engineering bandwidth is tight?
  6. Describe your user research process when time and budget are limited.
  7. How would you measure the success of a redesign after it shipped?

  9. How do you handle design feedback you disagree with from a senior colleague?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through a project where you improved a complex workflow for a non-technical user.

*Situation:* At my previous company, an HR admin dashboard had a payroll reconciliation flow that users were completing incorrectly or abandoning partway through.

*Task:* I was asked to redesign the flow to reduce completion errors before the next product release.

*Action:* I ran a jobs-to-be-done exercise with several HR managers, mapping the steps they actually took versus what the product expected. I found they were routinely switching to a separate spreadsheet to cross-check figures. I redesigned the screen to surface discrepancy flags inline, with plain-language explanations instead of cryptic error codes, so users never had to leave the app.

*Result:* Usability test participants completed the task much faster in testing, and the team saw a clear drop in support tickets related to that flow in the weeks after launch.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you pushed back on a stakeholder request.

*Situation:* A product manager wanted to add a prominent upsell banner to the employee self-service home screen.

*Task:* I was designing the feature but had concerns it would interrupt employees who just needed to find their salary slip.

*Action:* I ran a quick guerrilla test showing both versions: one with the banner, one without. I presented the findings to the PM, showing that users in the banner version took noticeably longer to reach their primary task and expressed frustration during think-aloud sessions.

*Result:* The PM agreed to move the upsell to a less prominent spot in the settings menu. The next usability round showed no friction on the home screen, and the PM later credited the test with saving a round of post-launch fixes.

---

Q: Tell me about a design decision that turned out to be wrong.

*Situation:* I designed a multi-step onboarding wizard for a new HR feature, assuming users would complete it in one sitting.

*Task:* My goal was to help new users configure the feature correctly on first use.

*Action:* I built a linear wizard with several steps but left out a save-and-resume option. After launch, session recordings showed a large share of users dropping off midway and not returning.

*Result:* I added a save-and-resume path and a short follow-up nudge for users who had not finished setup. Completion rates improved noticeably in the following cycle, and I now treat 'resumability' as a default checklist item for any multi-step flow.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Jobs-to-be-Done for workflow questions. When asked to improve a greytHR flow, start by naming the job the user is trying to get done (for example: 'run payroll without errors before the 5th'). Then describe what gets in the way. This frames your answer around user value, not UI polish.

STAR with a metrics coda. Use the Situation, Task, Action, Result structure for behavioural questions, but end with what you would measure differently next time. Greytip interviewers typically want to see that you reflect on outcomes, not just process.

Constraint-first for prioritisation questions. When asked how you prioritise, name the constraints first (engineering capacity, release date, user impact), then explain how you rank against them. Vague answers like 'I talk to stakeholders' without explaining what you do with that input tend to fall flat.

Observe-infer-propose-validate for critique questions. For 'what would you improve in greytHR?' questions, use this structure: observe (what you see in the current product), infer (what user need is unmet), propose (one specific change), validate (how you would test it). This shows rigour without sounding like you are criticising the team's past work.

Evidence framing for pushback questions. For conflict or disagreement questions, the phrase 'I ran a quick test to bring data into the conversation' lands better than 'I disagreed with them.' It reframes your position as evidence-based rather than personal.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Greytip designers work on a B2B HR platform where users are not choosing the product for fun. They are using it because their employer requires it. Interviewers look for designers who understand that context.

Empathy for reluctant users. Can you design for someone who uses your product once a month, under deadline pressure, on a mid-range phone, possibly in a second language? If your portfolio only shows consumer apps with young, tech-savvy users, expect probing questions about how you would handle this shift.

Comfort with complexity without adding complexity. greytHR covers payroll, compliance, leave, attendance, and more. Interviewers want to see that you can simplify dense information without hiding it entirely, since many HR admins genuinely need the underlying detail.

Collaboration signals. Candidates report that greytip values designers who can describe how they work with PMs and engineers, not just what they deliver. Be ready to talk about a moment of disagreement and how it was resolved constructively.

Ownership of outcomes. Generic answers about following a design process are not enough. Interviewers typically want to hear what happened after your design shipped: did it work, how did you know, what did you change?

Awareness of greytHR. Candidates who can reference a specific screen or flow show genuine interest and are easier to picture as a team member. A short hands-on session with the product before your interview is one of the highest-value things you can do.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Know the product first. Sign up for a greytHR trial or watch walkthrough videos. Pick two or three flows that feel clunky and prepare a structured critique using the observe-infer-propose-validate framework. Note the different user types: HR admins, payroll processors, and employees on self-service.

Audit your portfolio. Select two or three projects that involve complex workflows or B2B contexts. If your portfolio is all consumer work, prepare a bridge statement connecting the skills you built there to what HR software users need.

Practise behavioural questions out loud. Run through the questions in the previous section and time yourself. Answers that run very long in conversation tend to lose the interviewer before you reach your result.

Prepare a design challenge mindset. Greytip reportedly gives take-home or live design tasks. Practise narrating your thinking as you work: 'I am starting with the job to be done, which is...' This signals structured thinking even when your output is rough.

Research the team. Look up greytip's recent product announcements and any public posts from their design team. Prepare two or three questions about the team's current priorities. It signals genuine interest, not just job-hunting.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating greytHR like a consumer app. Candidates who open with 'the first thing I would do is simplify the UI' often lose interviewers quickly. greytHR users need access to detail. Simplification without understanding their workflow can mean hiding information they rely on.

Portfolio without B2B or enterprise context. If every project you show is a consumer mobile app, expect questions about whether you can handle HR software complexity. Prepare a framing that connects your consumer work to enterprise user needs.

Vague research claims. Saying 'I did user research' without describing who you spoke to, what you learned, and how it changed your design is a common weakness. Greytip interviewers reportedly push hard on this point.

Ending the case study at 'we shipped it.' Interviewers want to hear what happened next. If you do not have hard data, describe qualitative observations and what you would measure if you could go back.

Not knowing the product. Walking into a greytip interview without having used greytHR is the single most avoidable mistake. Even a short session with the trial gives you specific, credible things to say.

Over-designing in a live task. Candidates sometimes spend too long on visual polish during a live challenge. Greytip interviewers typically care more about your reasoning and trade-off decisions than pixel-perfect mockups.
