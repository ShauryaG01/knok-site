02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates who have interviewed at Airbnb for DevOps roles report questions across a few consistent themes: incident ownership, CI/CD pipeline design, Kubernetes at scale, infrastructure-as-code, and cross-team collaboration. Here are 12 questions commonly cited by candidates:

  1. Walk me through how you designed or significantly improved a CI/CD pipeline. What bottlenecks did you remove and how did you measure the result?
  2. Airbnb runs a globally distributed system. How would you design multi-region failover for a stateful service?
  3. Describe a production incident you owned end-to-end. How did you detect it, contain it, and prevent recurrence?
  4. How do you decide what to alert on versus what to log and review later? How do you keep on-call fatigue low for your team?
  5. Tell me about running Kubernetes in production. What edge cases or failure modes surprised you that the documentation did not warn you about?
  6. How would you design a secrets management system for a company running hundreds of microservices?
  7. Airbnb has many independent product teams. How do you enforce infrastructure standards without becoming a bottleneck to those teams?
  8. Describe your infrastructure-as-code approach. How do you detect and handle drift between your IaC definitions and the live environment?
  9. A deploy just went out and latency spiked within minutes. Walk me through your debugging process, step by step.
  10. How have you built or improved on-call runbooks so that an engineer unfamiliar with a service can resolve common issues without escalating?
  11. How do you approach cloud cost optimisation without hurting reliability or developer velocity?
  12. Tell me about a time you pushed back on an engineering team's request because it would have introduced operational risk. How did you handle the conversation?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a production incident you owned end-to-end.

*Situation:* At my previous employer, a payment processing service began throwing errors on a Friday evening. Monitoring alerts fired within minutes and paged the on-call engineer, which was me that week.

*Task:* I needed to identify the root cause quickly, keep stakeholders informed, and restore service with minimal customer impact.

*Action:* I pulled the recent deploy history immediately and spotted a configuration change that had gone out a couple of hours earlier. I confirmed with the backend team lead that a targeted config rollback would be safe, coordinated a war-room call to keep the team aligned, and issued an internal status update. I chose a targeted rollback rather than a full service restart to limit blast radius. Once traffic normalised, I led the post-mortem: a detailed timeline, a map of contributing factors, and concrete action items including a pre-deploy config validation step, a synthetic monitor for the payment flow, and a new runbook entry for this failure pattern.

*Result:* Service recovered within the target recovery window. The config validation step was adopted as a standard pipeline gate across related services. The post-mortem was shared company-wide and became a training reference for the on-call rotation.

---

Q: How do you enforce infrastructure standards across many independent engineering teams without becoming a bottleneck?

*Situation:* At a previous company, we had grown to many product teams, each deploying independently. Standards were informal and inconsistency was causing incidents.

*Task:* As the lead DevOps engineer, I needed to raise the baseline without creating a centralised approval gate that would slow teams down.

*Action:* I mapped every manual review gate and replaced the slowest checks with automated policy enforcement using Open Policy Agent integrated into the CI pipeline. Teams received instant, actionable feedback on policy violations before code ever reached staging. For standards that were harder to automate, I ran short enablement sessions with each team lead rather than issuing mandates. I also created a shared Terraform module library so teams could self-serve compliant infrastructure patterns. The DevOps team became a platform team, not a gatekeeper.

*Result:* Policy violation rates dropped measurably over the following quarter based on our internal dashboard. Engineer feedback was positive because teams could move quickly within guardrails. The module library was adopted across all product teams and became part of the new-hire onboarding guide.

---

Q: Tell me about running Kubernetes in production. What surprised you?

*Situation:* I joined a team that had recently migrated from VM-based deployments to Kubernetes on AWS EKS. The migration had gone smoothly on paper but we were seeing intermittent issues under load.

*Task:* My job was to stabilise the cluster and build team confidence in the platform before onboarding more teams.

*Action:* I reviewed resource requests and limits across all workloads and found that several services had no limits set, which caused noisy-neighbour problems during traffic spikes. I used VPA recommendations as a starting point, then tuned manually based on observed usage patterns. I also found that our pod disruption budgets were misconfigured, meaning rolling updates were occasionally taking services below minimum capacity. I fixed the PDB definitions, added a pre-deploy PDB health check, and improved the cluster autoscaler configuration to scale out faster during known traffic ramp periods.

*Result:* Node-related incidents dropped in the months that followed. Rolling update reliability improved substantially, and the team grew confident enough to onboard three additional product teams onto the cluster within the next quarter.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For technical DevOps questions, the STAR method (Situation, Task, Action, Result) gives your answers structure without making them feel scripted. Here is how to apply it specifically for Airbnb:

Situation and Task (keep brief). Spend a sentence or two at most. Interviewers want context, not backstory. Name the scale where relevant: how many services, what kind of traffic, what was at stake.

Action (this is the core). Go deep here. Walk through your actual decision-making. Why Kubernetes over ECS? Why Open Policy Agent over a manual review process? Airbnb interviewers are looking for engineering judgement, not just execution. Explain the trade-offs you weighed.

Result (specific and honest). Share a real outcome. If you have a metric from your internal dashboards, use it and say where it came from. If the outcome was qualitative (the on-call team stopped being woken up for this class of issue, teams adopted the tool voluntarily), state that directly. Honesty about data limitations reads far better than vague claims.

For system design questions, use a three-move structure: state your understanding of the constraints (global scale, spike traffic, stateful vs stateless), propose a high-level architecture, then drill into the pieces the interviewer asks about. Resist the urge to describe everything upfront before the interviewer has a chance to redirect you.

For behavioural questions about cross-team collaboration, Airbnb values transparency and moving with purpose. Frame your answers around sharing context early, making decisions with available data, and treating other teams as partners rather than obstacles to route around.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Airbnb DevOps interviewers are looking for a specific combination of technical depth and engineering mindset. Candidates commonly report that the following qualities stand out in successful interviews:

Reliability as a first principle. Airbnb's platform cannot afford extended outages. Interviewers want to see that you treat reliability as a design constraint from the start, not something you retrofit after launch. Show that you build for failure, not just for the happy path.

Automation-first thinking. If your answer to a reliability or consistency problem involves a human checking something manually on a recurring basis, that signals to the interviewer that you have not thought the problem through. Show that your instinct is to encode the check.

Structured post-mortem practice. Candidates who demonstrate blameless root-cause analysis and genuine follow-through on action items stand out. Airbnb engineering has publicly shared its investment in incident culture, so this topic is taken seriously in interviews.

Cross-team influence without authority. DevOps at Airbnb involves working with many independent product teams. Interviewers want to see that you raise standards through enablement, tooling, and direct conversation, not through mandates or gatekeeping.

Communication under pressure. Expect at least one scenario about an active incident. Interviewers want to see that you can communicate clearly to non-technical stakeholders while simultaneously debugging a technical problem.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A structured preparation approach commonly cited as effective by candidates who have gone through the Airbnb DevOps process:

Week 1: Incident and on-call readiness. Revisit your most complex production incidents. Write a short STAR narrative for each. Practice telling each story aloud, then cut it down further. Record yourself. Identify where you rush, where you are vague on the 'why', and where you use jargon a non-specialist would not follow.

Week 2: System design at scale. Study multi-region architecture patterns, especially for stateful services. Practice designing a global load balancing setup, a secrets management system, and a blue-green deploy pipeline. Draw diagrams and explain them aloud. The goal is fluency, not a perfect answer.

Week 3: Kubernetes and cloud platform depth. Review Kubernetes from first principles: scheduling, resource management, network policies, pod disruption budgets, and autoscaling. Do the same for AWS services relevant to infrastructure (EKS, IAM, VPC, CloudWatch). Read recent Airbnb Engineering blog posts to understand the problems the team is actively working on.

Week 4: Behavioural and culture fit. Airbnb is known for taking its culture values seriously. Prepare a handful of stories about collaboration, disagreement handled professionally, and decisions made with incomplete information. Read Airbnb's publicly available engineering and culture material before the final round.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping to solutions before stating constraints. For system design questions, candidates who begin drawing architecture before agreeing on requirements often head in the wrong direction. State your assumptions first, confirm them with the interviewer, then design.

Vague results in STAR answers. Saying 'the system became more reliable' tells the interviewer nothing useful. Even a qualitative outcome like 'the on-call team stopped being paged for this class of issue' is concrete and credible. Use internal dashboard metrics where you have them.

Overcomplicating the design. Airbnb values operational simplicity. A clean, maintainable architecture with known trade-offs is more impressive than a theoretically optimal system that is hard to debug at 2am by someone who did not build it.

Not asking clarifying questions. Airbnb interviewers expect candidates to probe requirements, especially in system design. Staying silent is often read as lack of depth, not confidence.

Treating the interviewer as an examiner. Airbnb interviews are designed to feel collaborative. If you get stuck, say so and think aloud. Interviewers consistently report preferring candidates who explore problems openly over candidates who freeze or give up.

Under-preparing for the behavioural round. Technical candidates often spend all their preparation time on infrastructure topics. The culture and behavioural round carries real weight at Airbnb. Prepare a handful of strong stories covering collaboration, conflict, and ambiguity before your interview day.

How many interview rounds does Airbnb typically have for DevOps roles?

Candidates commonly report a process spanning from a recruiter screen through several rounds, including one or two technical sessions, a scripting or coding assessment, and a final culture-and-depth round. Specific stage counts vary by team and level, so treat any account you read online as a guide, not a guarantee. Confirm the structure with your recruiter at the start of the process so you can prepare accordingly.

Does Airbnb ask live coding or scripting questions in DevOps interviews?

Candidates commonly report a scripting or automation task as part of the DevOps interview, usually in Python or Bash. These are typically practical scenarios: parsing logs, automating a deploy step, or checking infrastructure state. Pure competitive-programming style algorithm questions are less commonly reported for DevOps tracks, though some teams include a lighter coding round. Confirm the format with your recruiter early so you prepare for the right thing.

Airbnb has publicly reported using AWS as a primary cloud platform. Reviewing AWS services relevant to infrastructure (EKS, EC2, RDS, IAM, VPC, CloudWatch) will cover most scenarios you are likely to encounter. Kubernetes knowledge is strongly valued based on candidate reports. Multi-cloud or GCP knowledge is useful context but is not essential for most DevOps roles.
