01 Overview

Overview

Nurix is a clinical-stage biotechnology company working on targeted protein degradation therapies. Their engineering team supports research computing, clinical data pipelines, and regulated cloud infrastructure. A DevOps role here means working at the intersection of modern cloud practices and the compliance requirements of a life sciences company.

With 7 open DevOps roles at Nurix (per knok jobradar data as of July 2026), the company is actively hiring. That signals genuine team growth, though competition from candidates with biotech or pharma backgrounds is real.

Candidates report the process typically includes a recruiter screen, one or two technical rounds (sometimes with a take-home exercise or a live infrastructure walkthrough), and a final conversation with a hiring manager or senior engineer. The exact format can vary by role and team, so confirm the structure with your recruiter early.

What makes this interview different from a pure tech company: Nurix interviewers look for engineers who understand why audit trails, change control, and data integrity matter, not just how to build fast CI/CD pipelines. You do not need a compliance background, but showing awareness of GxP principles and validated systems will set you apart from candidates who treat this like any other cloud role.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions reflect patterns candidates commonly report from DevOps interviews at biotech and life sciences companies at a similar stage to Nurix. Expect technical depth and compliance-awareness questions to appear together.

  1. Walk me through how you have managed infrastructure in a regulated or compliance-heavy environment. What specific controls did you put in place?
  2. Describe your experience building or maintaining CI/CD pipelines for scientific or data-heavy workflows. How did you ensure reproducibility?
  3. How have you used containerisation (Docker, Kubernetes) in production? What was the hardest problem you solved with it?
  4. Tell me about a time you enforced secrets management and access control across a cloud environment. What was your approach and what gaps did you close?
  5. How would you design monitoring and alerting for a research platform where downtime directly impacts a team's experiments or deadlines?
  6. Describe a time you improved deployment reliability or reduced the frequency of production incidents. What changed and how did you measure success?
  7. How have you supported data engineering, ML, or bioinformatics teams with their infrastructure needs? What did you learn from working with scientists or analysts?
  8. Walk me through your experience with infrastructure as code. Which tools have you used and how did you handle drift between environments?
  9. How do you approach disaster recovery planning? Give me a concrete example of a backup or failover mechanism you designed and tested.
  10. Nurix operates in a regulated space. How familiar are you with GxP compliance, audit logging, or validated systems? Can you give a real example of applying this knowledge?
  11. Describe a time you had to balance delivery speed with security or compliance requirements. How did you resolve the tension?
  12. How do you keep cloud costs under control while supporting teams that need flexible, on-demand compute for large jobs?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format for all behavioural questions: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation brief, spend most of your time on Action, and make Result specific even if you have to say 'the team reported' or 'we estimated.'

---

Q: Describe your experience building CI/CD pipelines for data-heavy or scientific workflows.

*Situation:* A bioinformatics team at my previous company was running genome alignment jobs manually on shared servers. Every run required someone to SSH in, copy files, and trigger scripts by hand. Results were hard to reproduce and errors were frequent.

*Task:* I was asked to automate the entire process from code commit to validated output, while maintaining a full audit log of every run.

*Action:* I built a GitLab CI/CD pipeline that containerised each workflow step using Docker. I added input and output checksums at every stage so data corruption would be caught immediately. I connected the pipeline to an S3-compatible backend so every run's artifacts were automatically versioned and traceable. I also wrote documentation explaining how to reproduce any historical run.

*Result:* Manual errors dropped to near zero. Scientists could reproduce any past result by checking out the same commit and re-running the pipeline. The audit logs became useful during an internal review and required no extra work to produce.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you improved deployment reliability or reduced incidents.

*Situation:* Our team was deploying a critical data ingestion service roughly once a week, and a significant share of those deployments caused brief outages because of missed configuration changes between environments.

*Task:* I was responsible for redesigning the deployment process to eliminate these failures without slowing down release frequency.

*Action:* I introduced Terraform to manage all infrastructure configuration as code and added a pre-deployment validation step that diff-checked the intended state against the live environment. I also built a staging environment mirroring production so every change was tested there first. Finally, I wrote rollback runbooks so any on-call engineer could recover quickly without needing me available.

*Result:* Configuration-related outages stopped entirely over the following quarter. The team started releasing more frequently because confidence in the process was higher.

---

Q: How have you handled compliance or audit requirements in a cloud environment?

*Situation:* A previous employer was preparing for an external compliance audit of their cloud infrastructure. Auditors needed evidence that all production changes were tracked, reviewed, and approved.

*Task:* I needed to implement a change control workflow for our AWS environment that was rigorous enough to satisfy auditors but lightweight enough that engineers would actually follow it.

*Action:* I enabled AWS CloudTrail across all accounts and fed logs into a centralised SIEM. I enforced all infrastructure changes through pull requests in our IaC repository, requiring peer approval before merge. I created a change log template so every PR documented what changed, why, and what the rollback plan was. I also scheduled periodic IAM permission reviews to catch privilege creep before it became an audit finding.

*Result:* The audit passed with no major findings related to change management. The auditors specifically noted the pull-request-based workflow as a strong control. Engineers found it low-friction because it built on their existing Git habits.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For technical 'how would you' questions: Lead with your default approach, explain the trade-offs you considered, and close with a specific example or tool you have used. Interviewers want structured thinking, not a list of product names.

For behavioural questions: Use STAR. Keep Situation to one or two sentences. Spend the most time on Action, using 'I' not 'we.' Make Result concrete: a metric, a timeline, or a direct quote from a stakeholder. Vague results ('things improved') are the most common way candidates lose credit.

For compliance-related questions: Structure your answer around three things: what the risk or requirement was, what control you put in place, and how you verified it was working. Even without direct biotech experience, mapping your answer to this structure signals the right instincts for a regulated environment.

For 'under pressure' or incident questions: Describe what you did in the moment, then explain the systemic change you made afterward so the problem would not recur. Nurix interviewers want to see that you build resilience, not just survive crises.

On tool names: Mentioning specific tools (Terraform, Helm, Prometheus, Datadog, CloudTrail) is good. But always explain what problem the tool solved. At a company like Nurix, reasoning matters more than whether you have used their exact stack.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Core technical depth: Strong fundamentals in containers, CI/CD, infrastructure as code, and cloud platforms are table stakes. Expect questions that go one level deeper, for example, not just 'have you used Vault?' but 'how did you handle secret rotation and what happened when a key expired unexpectedly?'

Regulated environment instincts: Nurix is a clinical-stage company. Interviewers look for engineers who treat audit trails, reproducibility, and access control as natural parts of good infrastructure design, not as compliance overhead bolted on at the end. You do not need a certification, but you should be able to speak to GxP principles at a practical level.

Cross-functional collaboration: DevOps at Nurix means partnering with scientists, data engineers, and clinical operations teams who may not have infrastructure backgrounds. Candidates who can translate technical constraints into plain language and who ask good questions about what the science team actually needs tend to stand out.

Ownership mentality: Expect detailed questions about incidents and failures. Interviewers want to see that you take clear ownership, investigate root causes, and build systems that prevent recurrence. Being vague about your personal contribution or attributing problems entirely to external factors are common ways candidates lose points.

Security mindset: With clinical and research data potentially in scope, security is not optional. Be ready to discuss IAM least-privilege design, network segmentation, and how you have approached vulnerability management or patching in a production environment.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Understand Nurix's scientific context. Read their pipeline page and recent press releases before your interview. You do not need to understand the biology in depth, but knowing they are in clinical trials for protein degradation therapies helps you frame answers around the real stakes of downtime or data integrity failures.

Step 2: Review GxP and validated systems basics. Spend a few hours reading about what GxP compliance means for software and infrastructure: change control, audit trails, data integrity, and validation documentation. Being able to say 'I understand why this matters' with a concrete example is enough for most DevOps roles at this stage.

Step 3: Prepare your STAR stories. Write out at least five stories covering: a CI/CD pipeline you owned end to end, an incident you resolved and then prevented from recurring, a security or compliance challenge you navigated, a time you improved a system's reliability, and a time you worked closely with non-engineering stakeholders. Practice them out loud.

Step 4: Brush up on your IaC and cloud fundamentals. Terraform, Docker, Kubernetes, and AWS (especially IAM, CloudTrail, EKS, and S3) are the tools most commonly cited in biotech DevOps job descriptions. If there are gaps, hands-on labs before the interview will do more than reading documentation.

Step 5: Prepare thoughtful questions for them. Ask about their current stack, the biggest infrastructure challenge the team is solving in the next few months, and how DevOps collaborates with research and clinical teams. Good questions signal genuine interest and help you assess whether the role is the right fit.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Using 'we' throughout your answers: The most common mistake in DevOps interviews is describing what 'we' built or 'our team' deployed without clarifying your specific contribution. Interviewers probe this because they want to know what you can do independently, not what a team around you did.

Treating compliance as a checkbox: Candidates who say 'yes, we had audit logs' without explaining what they logged, why it mattered, or how they verified the logs were useful come across as having borrowed experience. Be ready to go one level deeper on any compliance claim you make.

Name-dropping tools without context: Listing Terraform, Kubernetes, Datadog, and ArgoCD in a single breath sounds impressive but tells an interviewer nothing. For every tool you mention, be ready to say what specific problem it solved and what trade-off you considered when choosing it.

Skipping the 'after' in incident stories: Candidates describe the incident and the immediate fix but forget to mention the systemic change they made so the same thing would not happen again. The 'after' is often what interviewers remember most from these answers.

Not asking questions at the end: Candidates who skip questions at the end of the interview miss a chance to show genuine interest in the team's actual challenges. They also miss information that could help them tailor their answers in a final round.

Assuming this is a pure tech company interview: Nurix is a biotech. Framing all your answers around shipping fast and moving quickly, without acknowledging the regulated context, can read as a mismatch to the hiring team. Speed matters, but so does traceability and reliability.
