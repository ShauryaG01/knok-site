02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates at Serving Skill typically report a mix of behavioral and technical questions across one or more rounds. Here are the questions that come up most often:

  1. Walk us through a project where you had to gather requirements from stakeholders who disagreed on priorities. How did you handle it?
  2. How would you document a new business process for a product on a skill-development platform?
  3. Describe a time you turned raw data into a recommendation that changed a business decision.
  4. A technical team tells you a requirement is not feasible. What do you do?
  5. How do you write a Business Requirements Document (BRD) or Functional Specification Document (FSD)? Walk us through your approach.
  6. Tell us about a time you identified a gap between what a client asked for and what they actually needed.
  7. How do you prioritize features when the backlog is long and the team has limited bandwidth?
  8. What BI tools or SQL experience do you have, and how have you used them in a BA role?
  9. How do you manage scope creep once a project has already started?
  10. Describe how you keep all stakeholders aligned from requirement sign-off through delivery.
  11. How would you approach creating user stories and acceptance criteria for a new feature?
  12. What does success look like for a BA in the first few months in a new role at a company like ours?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell us about a time you identified a gap between what a client asked for and what they actually needed.

*Situation:* I was assigned to a project where the client asked us to build a new reporting dashboard. They wanted a page listing every transaction from the past year.

*Task:* My job was to document requirements and hand them to the dev team. But before I did, I spent time with the client to understand how they would actually use the report.

*Action:* I asked questions like 'What decisions will you make using this data?' and 'How often do you look at historical transactions?' I found that the client only needed a weekly summary with a few key metrics, not a full transaction log. I put together a revised scope document, got the client's approval, and briefed the dev team on the updated requirements.

*Result:* The team built a focused dashboard in less time than the original scope would have taken. The client used it every week. The original request would have produced a report nobody opened.

---

Q: How do you handle a situation where the technical team says a requirement is not feasible?

*Situation:* On a previous project, a core feature I had documented was flagged as technically not feasible by the engineering team two weeks before the delivery date.

*Task:* I needed to find a path forward without losing the business value the stakeholder expected.

*Action:* I sat down with a developer to understand exactly what made it difficult. It turned out the constraint was a specific integration, not the feature itself. I went back to the stakeholder, explained the constraint in plain language, and proposed an alternative approach that delivered the same outcome differently. I documented the workaround as a formal requirement and got sign-off from both sides.

*Result:* The feature shipped on time using the revised approach. The stakeholder was satisfied because the underlying business need was met, even if the implementation looked different.

---

Q: How do you prioritize features when the backlog is large and resources are limited?

*Situation:* I joined a project mid-way where the backlog had grown large and the team was unsure what to build next.

*Task:* I had to help the product manager and stakeholders agree on a priority order before the next sprint planning session.

*Action:* I facilitated a prioritization workshop using the MoSCoW method (Must have, Should have, Could have, Won't have). I prepared a one-page summary of each item with estimated business impact and rough effort. We went through the backlog item by item, and I helped translate technical items into business language so stakeholders could vote meaningfully.

*Result:* We cut the active backlog significantly in one session. The team went into sprint planning with a clear, agreed-upon list. Stakeholders felt heard because they had participated in the decision.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the most important structure to learn. Almost every behavioral question at Serving Skill can be answered with it. Keep the Situation brief (a sentence or two), spend most of your answer on Action, and always close with a concrete Result.

MoSCoW for prioritization questions. If asked how you prioritize requirements, MoSCoW (Must have, Should have, Could have, Won't have) is a recognized framework that signals structured thinking. Explain it briefly and then give an example of using it.

Five Whys for root-cause questions. When asked how you diagnose a business problem, the Five Whys technique shows you go beyond the surface symptom. Say: 'I ask why something is happening, then why again, until I reach the root cause, usually within a few iterations.'

The user story format 'As a [user type], I want [goal], so that [reason]' is the standard structure for writing user stories. If asked about requirements or acceptance criteria, using this format shows you understand how business needs translate to development tasks.

Gap analysis framing works well for process improvement questions. Describe the current state, the desired state, and the gap between them. Then explain how your analysis identified which gap to close first.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Serving Skill interviewers are typically evaluating a few things in parallel:

Can you communicate with non-technical people? A BA at a skill-development company talks to trainers, sales teams, operations staff, and clients, not just developers. Interviewers want to hear that you can explain a technical constraint to a business person without jargon.

Do you ask the right questions? The best BAs are curious. Interviewers often look for candidates who, in their example answers, describe pausing to ask clarifying questions rather than immediately executing a request.

Are you data-oriented? Even if the role is not heavily technical, candidates who can pull a SQL query or build a pivot table to validate their analysis stand out. Mention specific tools you have used.

Can you manage stakeholders under pressure? Scope creep, conflicting priorities, and missed deadlines are everyday BA problems. Use your examples to show you have navigated these without losing the stakeholder's trust.

Do you understand the end user? At a company like Serving Skill, the end users are often learners or job seekers. Interviewers appreciate candidates who think about user needs, not just business requirements.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Research and company context first. Read everything public about Serving Skill, its products, and the role description carefully. Note what the job description says about tools, industries, and stakeholders. Prepare a two-minute answer to 'Tell me about yourself' that connects your background directly to what the role needs.

Build your story bank. List several projects or situations from your experience that demonstrate requirements gathering, stakeholder management, data analysis, process improvement, and conflict resolution. For each, write out the STAR structure in bullet points. You want to be able to draw from these stories for almost any question.

Practice out loud. Answering in your head is different from speaking. Record yourself or practice with a friend. Pay attention to whether your Result is clear and whether you are running too long on the Situation part.

Prepare your own questions. Good ones include: 'What does the BA team's day-to-day collaboration with product and tech look like?', 'What does success look like in the first few months in this role?', and 'What are the biggest challenges a BA faces here right now?' Asking these signals genuine interest and helps you evaluate the role.

Brush up on tools. If the job description mentions SQL, Excel, Power BI, Jira, or Confluence, spend time refreshing your knowledge. Even basic proficiency signals seriousness.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Giving vague, general answers. Saying 'I am good at stakeholder management' without a specific story tells the interviewer nothing. Every claim needs an example.

Skipping the Result. Candidates often describe what they did but never say what happened. Always close with an outcome, even if it is qualitative ('the client was satisfied', 'the project shipped on time').

Overloading the interviewer with context. Keep the Situation part of your STAR answer to a few sentences. If you spend several minutes on background before getting to what you actually did, the interviewer loses the thread. Your Action and Result should take up most of your answer.

Not asking questions at the end. Candidates who say 'No, I think I am covered' miss a chance to show curiosity and learn something useful about the role.

Using jargon without checking the audience. Terms like 'UAT', 'BRD', or 'sprint velocity' are fine if your interviewer is technical. If they are from HR or a business team, switch to plain language.

Memorizing scripts instead of stories. Scripted answers sound robotic and fall apart when the interviewer follows up. Know your stories deeply enough to answer follow-up questions naturally.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Serving Skill BA interview typically have?

Candidates typically report two to three rounds, though this varies by role and seniority. The first round is usually a screening call focused on your background and experience. Subsequent rounds are more detailed behavioral and technical discussions. Treat any information about round count as approximate until confirmed with your recruiter, as Serving Skill has not published a fixed interview process.

Is there a case study or take-home assignment?

Some candidates report receiving a short take-home assignment or case study, particularly for senior BA roles. It typically involves analyzing a business scenario and recommending a solution. Prepare by practicing structured problem-solving: state the problem clearly, identify stakeholders, list your assumptions, and justify your recommendation with whatever data you have.

What salary can I expect for a BA role at Serving Skill?

Serving Skill has not publicly disclosed salary bands for BA roles. For market context, platforms like Glassdoor and industry surveys are your best reference for BA compensation benchmarks in India. Discuss your expectations based on your years of experience and the role's location, and always research current market rates before entering salary negotiations.

Do I need SQL or data analysis skills for this role?

Many BA job descriptions at companies like Serving Skill mention SQL, Excel, or BI tools as preferred or required skills. Even if not strictly mandatory, being able to demonstrate basic data analysis signals analytical thinking and makes you a stronger candidate. Practice writing simple queries and interpreting data if you have not used these tools recently.

How should I answer 'Tell me about yourself' for this role?

Keep it to two to three minutes. Structure it as: where you started, what you have built in your BA career, and why this role at Serving Skill fits your next step. Avoid reciting your resume chronologically. Instead, pick one or two highlights that are directly relevant to what the job description asks for, and end with why you are interested in this specific company.

How much of the interview is technical versus behavioral?

For most BA roles, behavioral questions in STAR format make up the majority of the interview. Technical questions typically cover tools you have listed on your resume, SQL basics, or domain knowledge relevant to the industry. Candidates report that communication skills and structured thinking matter more than deep technical expertise for BA interviews at most companies.
