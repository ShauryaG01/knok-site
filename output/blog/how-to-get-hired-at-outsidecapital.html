03 Interview Process

Interview Process

The stages below reflect commonly reported patterns for financial services and capital markets firms of this type. Specifics can vary by role, seniority, and team, so treat this as a guide rather than a guarantee.

Application and resume screen. Your resume goes through a recruiter review or an initial filter. Relevant titles, tools, and domain experience should be easy to spot in the first half of the page. Mismatches here are the most common reason candidates do not hear back.

Recruiter or HR call. If your resume clears the screen, a recruiter typically reaches out for an introductory call. This covers your work history, what you are looking for, your notice period, and a first check of role fit. Keep your story concise and have a short 'walk me through your background' answer ready before you pick up the phone.

Technical or domain round. This stage depends on the function. Finance roles commonly involve modelling exercises, case discussions, or questions on valuation and market structure. Technology roles typically include a coding test or a system design session. Operations and client-facing roles may get a written task or a scenario-based discussion.

Hiring manager interview. A senior team member takes you deeper into your past experience, your decision-making approach, and how you handle ambiguity. Expect behavioural questions asking you to describe specific situations. The STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is a reliable structure to practise.

Final panel or leadership round. The last stage often involves a few senior stakeholders in back-to-back conversations or a panel setting. Cultural alignment, intellectual curiosity, and the quality of your questions to them all carry weight at this point. Come with two or three thoughtful questions prepared.

04 What They Look For

What They Look For

Financial services and capital markets firms consistently screen for a mix of technical depth and professional presence. Based on publicly reported patterns for similar firms, here is what likely matters at Outside Capital.

Domain knowledge. For finance and investment roles, a solid grasp of how capital markets work, how deals are structured, or how portfolios are managed is typically expected. For technology roles, clean code, system design thinking, and familiarity with data pipelines or APIs tends to be the benchmark.

Analytical thinking. Whether you are reading a balance sheet or debugging a data model, firms in this space expect you to work comfortably with numbers and draw structured conclusions from incomplete information. Show this through specific examples, not just claims.

Clear communication. You will often need to explain complex ideas to people who are not specialists in your area. Written clarity and concise verbal answers both matter, and interviewers notice quickly which candidates have this skill.

Ownership mindset. Growth-stage firms in particular look for people who take initiative and do not wait to be told exactly what to do. Coming into interviews with examples of problems you identified and solved proactively goes a long way.

Attention to detail. In capital markets environments, errors in numbers or documents can have real consequences. Precision in your resume, your test submissions, and your interview answers signals that you take quality seriously from day one.

05 How To Prepare

How To Prepare

Research the firm before every round. Understand what Outside Capital does: the asset classes it focuses on, the clients it serves, and any recent news or milestones. Candidates who show they have done this homework stand out in every stage, and those who clearly have not are easy to spot.

Tailor your resume to each role. With 92 open roles at the company, it can be tempting to use one resume for everything. Resist that. Adjust your headline and your top bullets to match the specific language in each job description you apply to.

Prepare your 'walk me through your background' answer. This question opens almost every interview. Keep it concise, focus on the thread connecting your career moves, and end at your current situation. Practise it out loud, not just in your head, so it sounds natural rather than rehearsed.

Run through role-specific preparation. For finance roles, brush up on valuation basics, financial statements, and any deal types relevant to the role. For technology roles, practise coding problems and review system design fundamentals. For client-facing roles, prepare examples of how you have managed stakeholder relationships under pressure.

Prepare sharp questions. Interviewers remember candidates who ask thoughtful questions. Ask about team structure, current challenges the team is solving, or how success is measured in the role in the first few months. Avoid questions whose answers are on the company website.

Follow up after each round. Send a brief thank-you note referencing something specific from the conversation. It leaves a professional impression and keeps you visible in the recruiter's pipeline.

06 Culture And Values

Culture And Values

Capital markets firms tend to attract people who are genuinely interested in how money moves and where opportunity hides. Outside Capital, given its name and the scale of its current hiring, appears to be building a team that values sharp thinking, professional discipline, and the drive to create results in a competitive environment.

Pace is likely fast. Investment and capital markets environments rarely slow down, and the people who thrive in them tend to be energised by deadlines, market movements, and complex problem-solving rather than drained by them. If you prefer a predictable, process-heavy routine, this type of firm is probably not the right fit.

Collaboration within clear structure is usually the cultural norm at firms like this. You are expected to work well with colleagues and clients, but also to take ownership of your deliverables without needing constant direction. The ability to figure things out independently is valued.

Professional growth in environments like this tends to be tied to performance and initiative rather than time served. Showing that you are learning fast, contributing beyond your role description, and bringing new ideas to the table is how people move forward.
