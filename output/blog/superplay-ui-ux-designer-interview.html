01 Overview

Overview

SuperPlay is a mobile gaming company focused on casual, social, and hyper-casual game experiences. As of July 2026, knok jobradar tracked 27 open roles at SuperPlay across India, reflecting an active hiring push. The UI/UX Designer role sits at the intersection of player experience, visual design, and product thinking.

Candidates report a process that typically includes a portfolio screening, one or two design rounds (a take-home task or live exercise), and a final discussion with the hiring manager or team lead. The exact structure varies by team and seniority, so ask your recruiter for the full format upfront.

SuperPlay looks for designers who understand what keeps players coming back: clear onboarding, satisfying feedback loops, and interfaces that feel effortless on a small mobile screen. If your portfolio skews toward enterprise or B2B apps, actively bridge the gap by connecting your work to consumer behaviour and engagement patterns.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up frequently in SuperPlay UI/UX interviews, based on what candidates report and the nature of mobile gaming product work.

  1. Walk us through your strongest game or consumer app UI project. What design decisions did you make and why?
  2. How do you design onboarding for a new player who has never played this type of game before?
  3. How do you balance visual richness with usability on a small mobile screen?
  4. Describe a time you simplified a complex user flow. What changed, and what was the result?
  5. How do you incorporate player feedback or analytics into your design iterations?
  6. Tell us about a design you were proud of that data showed was not working. What did you do?
  7. How do you collaborate with game designers, developers, and PMs to ship UI on tight timelines?
  8. What UI patterns do you think drive daily active use in casual games?
  9. How do you approach designing for multiple screen sizes and Android/iOS differences?
  10. Name a mobile game with UI you admire. What specifically works well and why?
  11. Describe a time a stakeholder pushed back on your design. How did you handle it?
  12. What is your design handoff process, and how do you make sure developers implement your vision accurately?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through your strongest game or consumer app UI project.

*Situation:* I was the lead designer on a casual puzzle game at my previous company. The game had strong core mechanics but players were dropping off in the first few sessions.

*Task:* My job was to redesign the onboarding flow and early level UI to improve day-one retention.

*Action:* I started by reviewing session recordings and exit-point data. I found players were getting confused at the currency system and the upgrade screen. I ran two rounds of user testing with players unfamiliar with the genre, simplified the currency display to a single resource for early levels, and redesigned the upgrade screen to show one clear action at a time. I worked closely with the developer to make sure animations matched the new layout without adding load time.

*Result:* After the redesign shipped, day-one retention improved noticeably according to publicly reported internal metrics shared at our team review. The onboarding path became a reference for two other games in the studio.

---

Q: Tell us about a design you were proud of that data showed was not working.

*Situation:* I had designed a social leaderboard feature for a word game. I was proud of the visual hierarchy and the animations.

*Task:* Post-launch analytics showed very low tap rates on the leaderboard tab compared to our expectations.

*Action:* I set aside my attachment to the visual and went back to basics. I ran a quick survey with a small group of players and found they did not feel the leaderboard was relevant because it showed global rankings rather than friends. I proposed switching to a friends-first view with a global tab as secondary. I iterated quickly, tested the revised layout with the same group, and got it into a build within the sprint.

*Result:* Engagement on the leaderboard feature increased in the following cycle, and the product manager noted the improvement in the next retrospective. I learned to separate pride in craft from attachment to outcomes.

---

Q: Describe a time a stakeholder pushed back on your design.

*Situation:* During a main menu redesign, I proposed removing a set of icons the marketing team had requested, because user testing showed players found them confusing.

*Task:* I needed to make the case for simplification without dismissing the marketing team's goals.

*Action:* I prepared a short presentation with session recordings where players hesitated or tapped the wrong icon. I offered an alternative: keeping the marketing messaging but moving it to a dedicated section accessible after players completed their first session. I framed it as 'show new players a clean path first, then introduce more.' I also invited the marketing lead to join a follow-up test session so they could hear player reactions directly.

*Result:* The marketing team agreed to the phased approach. The main menu shipped in the simpler form, and the secondary section was added in the next update. The collaboration also opened a standing monthly sync between design and marketing.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use STAR for experience questions. Situation sets the scene, Task explains your specific responsibility, Action is the bulk of your answer (stay here the longest), and Result closes with a real outcome. Keep Situation short so you spend most time on Action.

Use 'Show, Explain, Impact' for portfolio walkthroughs. Show the screen or flow, explain the specific decision you made (and what you rejected), then state the impact on the player or the product. Avoid narrating what is visually obvious. Interviewers want your reasoning, not a description of what they can already see.

Use 'Player First, then Business' for design philosophy questions. When asked how you balance competing priorities, anchor your answer in the player's goal first, then show how that also serves the business. This signals product maturity and aligns with how gaming studios think about retention and engagement.

Use 'Data plus Instinct' for process questions. Mobile gaming moves fast. Show that you use analytics and testing to validate, but that you can also make a confident call when data is thin or timelines are short. SuperPlay teams typically appreciate designers who do not get paralysed waiting for perfect data.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

A portfolio with mobile or consumer product work. Desktop or enterprise UI alone will raise questions. If your portfolio is mixed, lead with your most consumer-facing projects and draw explicit parallels to player experience.

Clear design thinking, not just aesthetics. Interviewers want to hear why you made each choice. Beautiful screens with no rationale behind them will not move you forward. Practice explaining trade-offs.

Collaboration signals. Game UI ships through writers, engineers, game designers, and PMs. Candidates who describe cross-functional work naturally tend to do better than those who present design as a solo activity.

Speed and adaptability. Casual gaming studios iterate quickly. Interviewers look for signs that you can produce solid work under time pressure and revise without drama when direction changes.

Genuine interest in games. You do not need to be a hardcore gamer, but you should be able to talk about mobile games you have actually played, what you noticed in their UI, and what you would do differently. If you have not explored SuperPlay's own titles before the interview, that gap will show.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know the product. Download and spend meaningful time with SuperPlay's games. Play through the onboarding of at least one title as if you were a brand-new player. Note what works, what confuses you, and what UI patterns repeat across their catalog. Prepare a few specific observations you could raise naturally in the interview.

Week 2: Prepare your portfolio. Select a few projects that show mobile or consumer product thinking. For each, write out the problem, your role, the key decisions, and the result. Practice walking through each briefly out loud. If you have no gaming work, prepare a short note on how your existing work relates to engagement-focused design.

Week 3: Practice answers and logistics. Run through all the questions in the 'Most Asked Questions' section using the STAR and 'Show, Explain, Impact' frameworks from the 'Answer Frameworks' section. Record yourself once to catch filler words and pacing. Confirm your portfolio is accessible on mobile, since interviewers may view it on a phone. Prepare a few thoughtful questions to ask the panel, focused on the team's current design challenges or how success is measured for this role.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Showing a portfolio without narration. Dropping a Figma link and expecting it to speak for itself is a common miss. Walk the interviewer through your thinking even if they can already see the screens.

Treating critique as an attack. SuperPlay interviews often include a design critique component where you are asked to find flaws in existing work, including theirs. Candidates who go defensive or overly diplomatic tend to lose points. Be specific and constructive.

Skipping player psychology. Answers that focus only on visual craft and ignore why players behave the way they do signal a surface-level understanding of game UI. Reference engagement loops, feedback mechanics, or onboarding psychology where relevant.

Not researching SuperPlay's games. Interviewers can immediately tell if you have not used the product. Generic answers about 'mobile-first design' land far weaker than observations tied to a specific flow in one of their titles.

Underselling cross-functional work. If you only talk about your own contributions without mentioning how you worked with developers or PMs, you may come across as someone who will be difficult to collaborate with.

Ignoring the brief in a design task. If given a take-home or live exercise, read the instructions carefully. Many candidates jump to solutions before fully understanding the constraint. Restate the goal before you start designing.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the SuperPlay UI/UX Designer interview typically have?

Candidates report a process that typically includes a portfolio screening, a design exercise (take-home or live), and one or two discussions with the design team and hiring manager. The exact number of rounds varies by team and seniority level. Ask the recruiter at the start of the process what the full format looks like so you can prepare accordingly.

Is there a design task or assignment in the process?

Most candidates report receiving some form of design exercise, either a take-home brief or a live whiteboard-style task. Typically this involves redesigning an existing screen or solving a specific player experience problem. Focus on explaining your process and trade-offs clearly, not just producing a polished final output.

What salary can a UI/UX Designer expect at SuperPlay?

SuperPlay has not publicly disclosed salary bands for this role, and knok jobradar does not have verified compensation data for it. For a realistic range, check Glassdoor and levels.fyi for UI/UX Designer salaries at comparable mobile gaming companies in India. Industry surveys suggest compensation varies considerably by years of experience and city.

Do I need gaming industry experience to apply?

Formal gaming industry experience is not a strict requirement, but genuine familiarity with mobile games helps significantly. Interviewers want to see that you understand player behaviour, engagement loops, and mobile UI conventions. If you come from a consumer app or e-commerce background, draw clear parallels to gaming-style interaction design in your answers.

Which cities have the most UI/UX Designer openings right now?

According to knok jobradar data from July 2026, Delhi has the most UI/UX Designer openings at 8, followed by Bangalore at 6 and Mumbai at 4. Hyderabad and Pune each had 1 opening. These numbers reflect the broader market across all companies, not just SuperPlay.

How should I prepare my portfolio for a gaming company interview?

Lead with your most consumer-facing or mobile-heavy projects, even if they are not games. For each project, prepare to explain the player or user problem, the specific design decisions you made, and the measurable result. If you have the chance, adding a short speculative redesign of one of SuperPlay's own screens can show initiative and genuine product interest.
