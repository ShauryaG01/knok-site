01 Overview

Overview

Cyera is a cloud-native data security platform that helps enterprises discover, classify, and protect sensitive data across cloud environments. The company has been expanding its engineering team aggressively, and as of mid-2026, knok jobradar tracked 120 open roles at Cyera across functions. Platform Engineer is one of the key technical hires in that mix.

A Platform Engineer at Cyera typically owns the infrastructure backbone the product runs on: Kubernetes orchestration, CI/CD pipelines, cloud cost management, and reliability engineering. Because Cyera's product sits at the intersection of data security and cloud infrastructure, platform work here carries real compliance weight. Interviewers care about whether you treat access control, audit logging, and data isolation as defaults, not afterthoughts.

The interview process typically spans several rounds. Candidates report an initial recruiter screen, a technical phone screen, one or two architecture or system design sessions, and a final hiring manager conversation. Some candidates mention a take-home or live coding exercise. Expect the technical rounds to mix standard platform and DevOps depth with Cyera-specific context around secure-by-default infrastructure thinking.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Based on candidate reports and Cyera's public engineering presence, these are the questions that come up most in the Platform Engineer interview:

  1. Walk me through how you would design a multi-tenant Kubernetes platform that isolates customer data at the namespace or cluster level.
  2. How have you handled secrets management in production? Which tools did you use and what were the trade-offs?
  3. Describe your experience with cloud-native observability. How did you decide what to instrument and what SLOs to set?
  4. Cyera's platform handles sensitive enterprise data. How do you think about building infrastructure that is secure by default?
  5. Tell me about a time you reduced infrastructure costs without sacrificing reliability. What did you measure, and what did you cut?
  6. How do you design CI/CD pipelines for a growing engineering team shipping daily? What quality gates do you put in place?
  7. How would you handle a cascading failure in a distributed system? Walk me through your incident response approach.
  8. How do you manage configuration drift across multiple cloud environments and keep your infrastructure-as-code state consistent?
  9. How have you implemented role-based access control at the infrastructure layer, and how did you audit it over time?
  10. What is your approach to capacity planning for a workload whose traffic patterns you cannot predict precisely?
  11. How do you balance developer velocity with platform stability? Give a concrete example where you had to make that trade-off.
  12. Describe a migration you led from one cloud provider, runtime, or orchestration system to another. What would you do differently next time?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How have you handled secrets management in a production environment?

*Situation:* At my previous company, secrets were scattered across environment variables, config files, and a mix of cloud-native stores with no consistent rotation policy. A compliance review flagged this as a critical gap.

*Task:* I was responsible for consolidating secrets management before an external audit and reducing the risk of secrets leaking through logs or config drift.

*Action:* I migrated all secrets to HashiCorp Vault with dynamic credentials for database connections, integrated Vault Agent as a sidecar in our Kubernetes pods, set up automatic rotation for all service accounts, and added a CI gate that failed builds if any hardcoded secrets were detected by a static analysis tool.

*Result:* We passed the audit with zero findings on secrets management. The blast radius for any single compromised credential dropped significantly, and the developer workflow got simpler because services no longer needed manual rotation steps.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you reduced infrastructure costs without sacrificing reliability.

*Situation:* Our staging environment was a near-identical mirror of production, running around the clock. The cloud bill had grown steadily over two consecutive quarters, and leadership asked engineering to address it.

*Task:* I was asked to reduce staging costs while keeping the environment available during core business hours for QA and developers.

*Action:* I implemented scheduled scale-down using Kubernetes CronJobs combined with cloud-provider instance schedules. I right-sized nodes by analysing actual CPU and memory utilisation over several weeks using Prometheus data, and moved non-critical workloads to spot instances with proper eviction handling and retry logic.

*Result:* Staging costs dropped meaningfully with no disruption the team noticed. We documented the approach so other non-production environments could follow the same pattern.

---

Q: Describe a migration you led from one orchestration system to another.

*Situation:* We ran a legacy ECS-based workload that the product team had outgrown. Service discovery, autoscaling, and deployments were all handled by custom scripts that were brittle and hard to debug.

*Task:* I led the migration of several dozen microservices from ECS to Kubernetes on EKS, with zero downtime and no disruption to a live B2B product.

*Action:* I ran both platforms in parallel, routing a small initial slice of traffic to Kubernetes first. I wrote migration runbooks for each service type, built Helm charts with sensible defaults, held weekly office hours so application teams could migrate their own services with support, and used feature flags at the load balancer layer to control traffic splits.

*Result:* The migration completed over several months. Deployment frequency increased because teams could use GitOps instead of custom deploy scripts, and on-call incidents related to deployments dropped noticeably in the quarter after cutover.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use STAR but keep it tight. Cyera interviewers typically move through several questions per round. Keep each answer to around two minutes. State the situation in one or two sentences, spend the most time on your actions, and always close with a concrete result, even if you cannot quote an exact number.

For system design questions, start by clarifying requirements: scale, latency targets, compliance constraints, and team size. At Cyera, explicitly calling out data isolation and access control early signals that you understand their domain. Walk through components one layer at a time rather than presenting everything at once.

For trade-off questions, name the tension before diving into your answer. Interviewers want to see that you recognise competing priorities, not that you found a magic solution. For example: 'Developer velocity and platform stability pull against each other, and here is where I drew the line in this case.'

For incident and on-call questions, use a before, during, after structure: what signals you used to detect the issue, the steps you took to mitigate it, and what you changed to prevent a recurrence. Mentioning a postmortem outcome shows operational maturity.

Connect your answers to Cyera's context. Their platform handles sensitive enterprise data. Weaving in phrases like 'given that this is customer-sensitive data' or 'we built the audit log from day one' shows you think like someone who fits a data security company.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Deep Kubernetes and cloud fluency. Cyera is cloud-native, and listing Kubernetes on your resume is not enough. Candidates report probing questions about network policies, admission controllers, resource quotas, and how the scheduler actually works, not just basic deployments and services.

Security-first thinking. This is the single biggest differentiator from a generic platform role. Interviewers probe whether your default instincts include least-privilege access, audit logging, network segmentation, and secrets hygiene. You do not need to be a security engineer, but you must speak the language of secure infrastructure naturally.

Operational ownership. Cyera wants engineers who own outcomes, not just tasks. They look for evidence that you have been on-call, written runbooks, and treated reliability as a shared responsibility with product teams rather than a separate team's concern.

Cross-functional communication. Platform engineers work closely with product and security teams at Cyera. Interviewers check whether you can explain infrastructure decisions to non-engineers and push back on unrealistic timelines with data rather than just opinions.

Comfort with ambiguity. The company is scaling quickly. Candidates who need fully defined requirements before starting work often struggle. Show that you can scope a problem, state your assumptions clearly, and iterate.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Core technical foundation.
Revise Kubernetes internals: the scheduler, controller manager, etcd, and how network policies are enforced in practice. Practice writing Terraform or Pulumi modules from scratch, including state management across environments. If you have limited hands-on experience with a dedicated secrets manager like HashiCorp Vault or AWS Secrets Manager, do a practical lab before the interview.

Week 2: Cyera-specific context.
Read everything publicly available about Cyera's product, their engineering blog posts, and any talks their team has given. Understand what Data Security Posture Management means at a product level, even though your role is infrastructure. Study how compliance certifications like SOC 2 affect infrastructure design in practice, specifically around logging requirements, encryption at rest and in transit, and access control audit trails.

Week 3: System design practice.
Practice designing a multi-tenant SaaS platform from scratch. Include network isolation, secrets management, CI/CD, and observability in every session. Time yourself and narrate your thought process out loud, since Cyera interviewers want to follow your reasoning, not just see a finished diagram.

Week 4: Behavioural stories and mock interviews.
Write out several STAR stories covering cost optimisation, migrations, incidents, and cross-team collaboration. Practice them with a peer or record yourself. Prepare a few thoughtful questions for the hiring manager about the team's current reliability challenges and what success looks like in the first few months.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Staying too shallow on Kubernetes. Listing it as a skill without being able to explain how a pod gets scheduled, how network policies are enforced, or what happens when etcd has issues is a common reason candidates do not progress past the technical screen.

Ignoring the compliance context. Platform engineers at Cyera are building infrastructure for a data security product. Candidates who treat the role like a generic cloud position and never mention access control, audit trails, or data isolation tend not to advance past the technical rounds.

Vague results in STAR answers. Saying 'things improved' or 'the team was happy' is not enough. Even without exact figures, describe what changed: 'deploy frequency went from weekly to daily' or 'on-call pages dropped in the month after the fix.'

Over-engineering system design answers. Adding every possible service to a design without justification reads as pattern-matching rather than engineering judgment. Cyera interviewers reportedly value clarity and reasoning over complexity. Explain why each component belongs there.

Not asking questions. Candidates who ask nothing at the end of a round signal low interest. Prepare questions about the team's current pain points, how platform decisions are made, and what the on-call load looks like today.

Talking only about tools, not outcomes. Mentioning Terraform, ArgoCD, and Prometheus is a baseline expectation. What stands out is why you chose them, what problems they actually solved, and what you would do differently given what you learned.
