01 Overview

Overview

Roku is a US-based streaming platform company, best known for the Roku OS that powers millions of smart TVs and streaming devices worldwide. Product Designers at Roku work on TV and mobile interfaces, the Roku Channel, remote control interaction patterns, advertising products, and cross-device experiences. As of July 2026, Roku has 253 open roles across functions, signaling active hiring. Candidates report that the interview process typically spans multiple rounds covering a portfolio presentation, behavioural questions, and often a live or take-home design exercise. The process places strong emphasis on how you communicate your design decisions, not just the quality of your final screens. If you are targeting a Product Designer role at Roku, expect deep focus on TV platform constraints, accessibility, and designing for lean-back (passive viewing) contexts.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

The following questions come up frequently in Roku Product Designer interviews, based on candidate reports and the nature of Roku's product.

  1. Walk us through a project in your portfolio where you solved a complex UX problem. What was your process from problem definition to final solution?
  2. How do you design for a 10-foot UI: a TV screen viewed from across the room? What constraints do you prioritise?
  3. Tell us about a time you had to push back on a product requirement. How did you handle it and what was the outcome?
  4. How do you approach accessibility in your design work, particularly on TV or large-screen platforms?
  5. Describe your experience contributing to or evolving a design system. What was your specific role?
  6. How do you balance user needs with business metrics when the two are in conflict?
  7. Walk us through how you would approach redesigning the Roku home screen for a brand-new user's first-time experience.
  8. Tell us about a time you worked closely with engineering to make feasibility trade-offs on a design.
  9. How do you conduct and synthesise user research when project timelines are tight?
  10. Describe a situation where quantitative data and qualitative user feedback pointed in opposite directions. What did you do?
  11. How do you ensure design consistency across multiple product surfaces such as TV, mobile, and web?
  12. What design trends in streaming and connected TV do you think will matter most over the next couple of years?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for behavioural questions. Keep Situation brief, spend most time on Action, and make Result specific.

Q: Walk us through a project where you solved a complex UX problem.

*Situation:* At my previous company, our video streaming app had a high drop-off rate at the content discovery stage on mobile, which the team first noticed in session analytics.

*Task:* I was asked to redesign the home feed to improve content discoverability without increasing the number of taps required to reach content.

*Action:* I ran several user interviews to understand how people mentally categorise content, mapped the current flow step by step, and identified that users were overwhelmed by rows of undifferentiated content. I proposed a personalised shelf system with clear category labels, collaborated with engineering to assess feasibility, and iterated through three rounds of low-fidelity to high-fidelity prototyping.

*Result:* After launch, the product team reported improved session duration in the quarterly review. The shelf pattern I introduced became a reference component adopted by two other product teams in the organisation.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you had to push back on a product requirement.

*Situation:* A PM wanted to place a persistent promotional banner for an advertising partner across the top of our home screen, which would visually obscure the primary navigation.

*Task:* My role was to design the placement, but I was concerned the implementation would harm usability for all users in order to benefit a single partner's visibility goal.

*Action:* I prepared a short written rationale, referenced publicly reported data on how intrusive banner placements had affected engagement for comparable streaming products, and proposed two alternative placements that met the advertising goal without covering navigation.

*Result:* The PM accepted one of my alternatives. The banner launched in a non-intrusive position, and post-launch usability testing returned positive feedback from participants.

---

Q: Describe your experience contributing to a design system.

*Situation:* Our design team had grown across several product areas, and the UI had become visibly inconsistent across screens.

*Task:* I was asked to audit the component library and propose a consolidation plan.

*Action:* I catalogued every component in active use, identified duplicates and one-off variants, facilitated a working session with the team to align on core principles, and drafted a tiered structure: core components, extended components, and platform-specific overrides. I wrote usage guidelines for each tier to reduce ambiguity for future contributors.

*Result:* The consolidated system reduced friction in design-to-engineering handoffs noticeably, and new designers reported onboarding to the system faster, which came up consistently in retrospectives.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Three frameworks cover most Roku Product Designer interview questions.

The 'Problem First' approach: Before showing any solution, state the problem clearly: who was affected, what constraint you were working within, and why it mattered to the business or the user. Roku interviewers consistently want to see rigorous problem framing, not just polished screens.

The 'Constraints and Trade-offs' approach: TV UI design carries hard constraints: focus-state navigation, no hover interactions, remote control as the primary input, variable screen sizes, and viewing distances of several feet. For any design question, explicitly name the constraints you worked within and the trade-offs you made. This signals platform-aware thinking and separates you from candidates who default to mobile patterns.

The STAR approach for behavioural questions: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation to a sentence or two. Spend the majority of your answer on Action (what you did and why you did it that way). Make Result as specific as you can: a metric from a published report, a qualitative outcome observed by stakeholders, or a decision made by the team based on your work.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Roku interviewers typically look for four qualities in Product Designer candidates.

Platform fluency: They want to see that you understand the unique challenges of designing for TV: focus-based navigation, remote control interaction, lean-back passive viewing, and accessibility for diverse audiences. Even if your portfolio has no TV work, you should be able to reason about these constraints out loud and apply your design thinking to them.

Clear design process: The portfolio presentation is not a gallery walk. Interviewers want to hear your process: how you defined the problem, what research informed your decisions, how you iterated, and what you would do differently. Candidates who skip straight to final screens typically do not progress to the next round.

Cross-functional collaboration: Roku teams work closely across product management, engineering, and data. Interviewers look for evidence that you handle disagreements constructively, understand technical feasibility, and can advocate for design decisions without alienating partners.

Communication and storytelling: Design decisions must be explained to stakeholders who are not designers. Candidates report that being able to articulate the 'why' behind each decision, clearly and concisely, is one of the most valued signals Roku interviewers look for.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Prepare in phases across the week or two before your interview.

Phase 1: Know the product. Use the Roku app or device if you can access one. Note what works well and what feels confusing from a design perspective. Have two or three specific observations ready, framed as a designer rather than a general user complaint.

Phase 2: Curate your portfolio. Select two or three projects that show your full process, not just final UI. For each case study, prepare to walk through your problem definition, research approach, key design decisions, and measurable or observable outcome. Practise narrating each case concisely out loud before the interview.

Phase 3: Build context on TV and streaming design. Read publicly available writing on 10-foot UI design, accessibility on TV platforms, and streaming UX patterns. You do not need prior Roku experience, but you should be able to discuss constraints such as focus states, remote input, and viewing distance with confidence.

Phase 4: Practise behavioural questions. Use the STAR format for questions about conflict, feedback, and cross-functional work. Prepare a handful of thoughtful questions to ask your interviewers, such as how design and product collaborate on roadmap decisions, or what current design challenges the team is focused on.

Phase 5: Day-of logistics. Candidates report that portfolio walkthroughs are central to Roku interviews. Have your portfolio link ready, test screen sharing before the call, and practise narrating your work without reading from slides. 07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping to solutions: Candidates who skip problem framing and go straight to showing screens often lose interviewers early. Always open with the context: what problem you were solving, for whom, and why it mattered.

Ignoring TV platform constraints: If you treat a Roku design exercise like a mobile app problem, you signal a gap in platform awareness. Mention focus states, remote navigation, and viewing distance even if the interviewer does not prompt you.

Vague outcomes in STAR answers: Saying 'the project went well' is weak. Tie your result to something observable: a metric from a published report, a qualitative outcome noted in user testing, or a decision made by stakeholders based on your work. If you have no number, describe specifically who noticed the change and what they said.

Weak portfolio narrative: Showing many screens without explaining the thinking behind key decisions is a common mistake. Practise narrating your process out loud so your reasoning is visible, not just your output.

Not preparing questions to ask: Candidates who have no questions at the end of a round can seem disengaged. Prepare genuine questions about team structure, the product roadmap, and the design culture at Roku.

Over-emphasising tools: Mentioning Figma, Maze, or other tools is fine, but spending significant time on tooling rather than thinking is a signal many interviewers flag. Lead with your process and reasoning; mention tools only briefly.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Roku Product Designer interview typically have?

Candidates report that the process typically includes a recruiter screen, a hiring manager conversation, a portfolio presentation round, and a final panel interview. The exact number of rounds can vary by team and seniority level. Confirm the current format with your recruiter at the start of the process, as interview structures do change over time.

Do I need TV or streaming design experience to interview at Roku?

Not necessarily, but you should be able to reason about TV platform constraints confidently. Interviewers typically look for strong design thinking and the ability to adapt quickly to new constraints. If your portfolio is focused on mobile or web, prepare to discuss how your approach would shift for a 10-foot UI context: focus states, remote navigation, and passive viewing behaviour.

What should I include in my portfolio presentation for Roku?

Focus on two or three projects that show your end-to-end process: problem definition, research, key decisions, iterations, and outcome. Roku interviewers typically want to understand your thinking, not just your visual output. Include at least one case study where you navigated a difficult constraint, a stakeholder disagreement, or a significant pivot during the project.

What salary can I expect as a Product Designer at Roku in India?

Based on knok jobradar data, Product Designer salaries in India broadly range from 6-12 LPA at entry level (0-2 years experience), 14-24 LPA at mid level (3-5 years), 26-40 LPA at senior level (6-9 years), and 36-55+ LPA at Lead or Principal level. For Roku-specific compensation figures, check Glassdoor or levels.fyi, as company-level data gives a more accurate picture than broad market ranges.

Is there a design exercise or take-home assignment in the Roku interview?

Candidates report that some Roku design interviews include a live whiteboard or design exercise, while others rely primarily on portfolio discussion and behavioural questions. The format can vary by team and level. Ask your recruiter early in the process whether to expect a design challenge, so you can set aside focused preparation time accordingly.

How do I stand out as a candidate for a Roku Product Designer role?

Demonstrate genuine curiosity about the Roku product by sharing specific, considered observations from using it yourself. Show that you can articulate design decisions clearly and connect them to user needs and business goals. Strong candidates also demonstrate comfort with ambiguity, cross-functional collaboration, and platform-specific constraints, not just visual craft.
