01 Overview

Overview

Modal is a New York-based infrastructure startup building serverless GPU compute for ML and data engineering teams. Their platform lets developers run Python functions on cloud GPUs without managing servers, and the company has been growing fast since its public launch.

Engineering Managers at Modal sit at the intersection of product engineering and deep infrastructure work. You need to be genuinely technical (comfortable discussing distributed systems, container orchestration, and developer experience) while also being a strong people leader who can hire, grow, and retain senior engineers.

As of July 2026, knok jobradar tracked 33 open roles at Modal, part of a broader market of 975 Engineering Manager positions across India. Candidates report the interview process is rigorous on both technical judgment and leadership depth, typically spanning multiple rounds covering system design, behavioral depth, and conversations with senior leadership. This guide covers what you are likely to face and how to prepare.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions are commonly reported by Engineering Manager candidates at Modal. The company values technical depth and startup ownership, so expect follow-ups that probe the 'why' behind your decisions.

  1. How do you stay technically sharp as a manager while trusting your team to own the implementation?
  2. Tell me about a time you led your team through a major infrastructure migration or platform change.
  3. How do you set priorities when engineering velocity and system reliability pull in opposite directions?
  4. Describe your hiring process for senior or staff engineers. What signals do you look for?
  5. Modal is remote-first. How do you build team cohesion and accountability without in-person time?
  6. Tell me about a time you had to make a difficult technical trade-off with incomplete information.
  7. How do you keep engineers aligned with company goals during a period of rapid growth or change?
  8. How have you handled a situation where a strong engineer was consistently missing delivery expectations?
  9. What experience do you have with developer tools, infrastructure products, or platform engineering?
  10. How do you create a culture where engineers take ownership and escalate problems early?
  11. Walk me through how you managed a critical production incident from detection to post-mortem.
  12. What metrics do you use to measure team health and engineering effectiveness?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a time you led your team through a major infrastructure change.

*Situation:* My team owned the data ingestion pipeline at a Series B startup. Our monolithic pipeline was causing deployment bottlenecks, and on-call burden had doubled over six months.

*Task:* I needed to migrate the team to a service-oriented architecture without disrupting ongoing feature work or increasing incident rates.

*Action:* I ran a two-week discovery sprint where engineers mapped dependencies and identified the three highest-risk seams. I set up a migration working group, established a 'strangle the monolith' pattern so we could ship incrementally, and held weekly syncs to surface blockers early. I also negotiated with the product team to protect dedicated sprint capacity for migration work each cycle.

*Result:* Over four months, we decomposed the pipeline into six independent services. On-call pages dropped significantly according to our PagerDuty data, and deployment frequency increased. The team reported higher morale because engineers could finally own their services end to end.

---

Q: How have you handled a situation where a strong engineer was consistently missing delivery expectations?

*Situation:* One of my senior engineers was technically excellent but regularly missed sprint commitments and did not communicate delays proactively. Other team members noticed and started losing confidence in sprint planning.

*Task:* I needed to address the performance gap clearly while retaining someone who had deep domain knowledge the team depended on.

*Action:* I set up a direct conversation where I shared specific examples, not vague feedback, and asked what was getting in the way. It turned out the engineer was taking on unplanned requests from a stakeholder group without telling me. We agreed on a written expectation: all scope changes go through me first, and commitments are updated in the tracker same day. I checked in weekly for two months.

*Result:* Delivery consistency improved within six weeks. The engineer later said the clarity actually reduced their stress. The team's confidence in planning recovered within one quarter.

---

Q: How do you balance technical involvement with trusting your team?

*Situation:* After being promoted to EM, I initially stayed too deep in code reviews and architecture decisions. Engineers started waiting for my sign-off instead of making calls themselves.

*Task:* I had to recalibrate: stay technically credible enough to hire and set direction, while genuinely empowering the team to own decisions.

*Action:* I introduced a simple decision framework. Engineers document decisions above a certain complexity threshold in a lightweight RFC, get async feedback from peers, and only escalate to me if there is genuine disagreement or cross-team impact. I also shifted from doing code reviews to doing periodic architecture reviews, asking questions rather than prescribing answers.

*Result:* Decision-making velocity improved noticeably over two quarters. Engineers reported in retros that they felt more ownership. I freed up time for hiring and cross-functional alignment, which had been neglected.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the baseline for behavioral questions. At Modal, push the Result step further: quantify what changed, name the system or process affected, and explain what you learned.

The 'Why I chose this' frame is useful for leadership philosophy questions. Lead with the outcome you were optimizing for, then explain your method, then show the trade-off you consciously made. Interviewers at infrastructure companies care that you reason clearly about trade-offs, not just that you followed a playbook.

Technical credibility anchors: When discussing past engineering decisions, name the technologies and constraints involved (latency targets, team size, cloud provider choices). You do not need to know Modal's exact stack in advance, but speaking concretely about infrastructure decisions signals you can earn your team's respect.

The 'What I would do differently' add-on: Modal is a startup that values learning loops. Ending a STAR answer with a clear, honest reflection on what you would change next time is often the detail that makes an answer memorable.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates report and Modal's public engineering culture, interviewers typically look for four things.

Technical depth that earns trust. Modal's engineers work on hard infrastructure problems. An EM who cannot follow a systems design discussion or ask probing technical questions will struggle. You do not need to write the code, but you need to understand it.

Startup ownership mindset. Modal moves fast and has a small team. Interviewers want evidence that you have operated with high autonomy, made consequential decisions without a committee, and taken responsibility for outcomes including failures.

Clear, direct communication. Modal is remote-first. Interviewers pay close attention to how well you structure your thinking out loud, because async communication is how the company runs. Rambling or vague answers are a red flag.

People leadership that scales. They want to see that you can hire senior engineers, give hard feedback, and build a team culture that does not depend on you being in every room. Evidence of team members you have grown into larger roles is a strong signal.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Research and positioning. Read Modal's engineering blog, their public GitHub repositories, and recent technical posts from their team. Understand what problems they are solving (serverless GPU compute, cold start latency, developer experience for ML workloads). Write down two or three genuine connections between their technical challenges and your past work.

Week 2: Story bank. Prepare six to eight STAR stories covering: a major technical decision, a hiring success, a performance issue, a cross-functional conflict, a production incident, and a time you changed your mind based on data. Each story should have a concrete result you can describe clearly.

Week 3: Technical refresh. Review distributed systems concepts relevant to Modal's domain: container scheduling, cold start problems, fault tolerance, and observability. You will not be coding, but you may be asked to discuss architectural trade-offs or evaluate a design your team presents.

Week 4: Mock interviews and logistics. Do at least two mock behavioral interviews out loud, not just in your head. Practice giving tight answers (under three minutes for behavioral questions, under five minutes for complex technical discussions). Prepare three or four questions to ask interviewers that show you have done real research on Modal's roadmap and engineering challenges.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Going too broad on behavioral answers. Saying 'we improved the process' without naming what changed and by how much reads as thin. Interviewers at Modal are technical and will probe for specifics.

Underselling technical depth. Some EM candidates over-index on people management stories and come across as disconnected from engineering. At a startup like Modal, your team will respect you more if you can engage with hard infrastructure problems.

Treating remote-first as a footnote. Modal operates asynchronously across time zones. If you do not have a clear, practiced answer for how you build team culture and accountability without in-person time, that gap will stand out.

Asking generic closing questions. Asking 'what does a day in the life look like?' signals low preparation. Ask about the specific engineering challenges the team is currently tackling, what success looks like in the first six months, or where the biggest technical debt lives.

Over-hedging on failures. When asked about a mistake, candidates sometimes qualify the failure so much it stops sounding like a real mistake. Modal values honesty and learning. Name the mistake clearly, then show what you changed.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Modal Engineering Manager interview process typically have?

Candidates report the process typically includes a recruiter screen, one or two technical or system design rounds, behavioral interviews with senior engineers or leadership, and a final conversation with a founder or VP. The exact structure can vary, and Modal may adjust based on the role level. Budget for four to five total conversations across two to three weeks.

Does Modal ask coding questions for Engineering Manager roles?

Candidates typically report that EM interviews at Modal do not require live coding. However, you may be asked to walk through a system design, evaluate a technical proposal, or discuss engineering trade-offs in depth. Being able to engage fluently with infrastructure and distributed systems concepts is important even if you are not writing code.

What salary can an Engineering Manager expect at Modal?

Modal is a US-headquartered company and compensation for remote or India-based roles would differ from their US packages. For Engineering Manager roles in India more broadly, Glassdoor and industry surveys show a wide range depending on level and company stage. Knok's jobradar salary data shows Manager-level EM roles commonly cited in the 35-60 LPA range, Senior Manager roles in the 55-90 LPA range, and Director-level in the 90-150+ LPA range. Verify current Modal-specific figures directly with their recruiter.

Is prior experience with GPU infrastructure or ML platforms required?

Modal's core product is serverless GPU compute, so familiarity with ML infrastructure is a genuine advantage. That said, candidates report that deep platform engineering experience (container orchestration, developer tools, distributed systems) is equally valued. If you have not worked directly on ML infrastructure, focus your prep on connecting your past infrastructure work to Modal's core problems.

How important is the remote-first culture fit at Modal?

Very important, based on what candidates report and what Modal's public engineering culture signals. Modal operates asynchronously across time zones, so interviewers pay close attention to how you communicate in writing, how you run distributed teams, and how you maintain accountability without in-person oversight. Come prepared with specific examples of managing remote or hybrid teams effectively.

How should I research Modal before the interview?

Read their engineering blog and public GitHub repositories to understand the real technical problems they are solving. Follow the founders and senior engineers on social media for recent product updates. Look at their open job descriptions across all roles to understand where the company is currently scaling.
