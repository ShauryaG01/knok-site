03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR method (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for all behavioral questions. Here are three worked examples.

Q: Describe a time you improved the performance of a system or feature.

*Situation:* My team maintained a customer-facing dashboard that was loading slowly during peak hours, causing users to abandon sessions before the data appeared. The product team flagged it as a priority fix.

*Task:* I was asked to investigate and reduce the page load time within two sprint cycles.

*Action:* I profiled the frontend bundle and found several large unoptimized images and blocking JavaScript. I also audited the backend API calls and found three separate calls that could be merged into one. I implemented lazy loading for images, split the JS bundle, and refactored the API to return a combined payload.

*Result:* Page load time dropped noticeably according to our monitoring tools, and the product team reported a clear reduction in abandonment rates at the next sprint review. The changes have held stable in production since.

---

Q: How do you handle a situation where a senior team member disagrees with your technical approach?

*Situation:* During a backend redesign, I proposed using an event-driven architecture while a senior engineer preferred a synchronous REST approach.

*Task:* I needed to make my case clearly without creating friction, and reach a decision the whole team could commit to.

*Action:* I prepared a short comparison document outlining the trade-offs of each approach, covering scalability, operational complexity, and delivery timeline. I requested a focused one-hour discussion rather than debating it in standup. We walked through each concern together and I acknowledged the valid points in their approach.

*Result:* We agreed on a hybrid model: synchronous calls for the user-facing path and event-driven messaging for downstream processing. The senior engineer later cited the comparison document as a useful model for future design discussions.

---

Q: Tell me about a bug that was particularly difficult to find and fix.

*Situation:* Our payment service was intermittently returning error responses in production, but only under high concurrency. Every local test passed without issue.

*Task:* I owned the investigation and needed to identify and fix the root cause without disrupting live transactions.

*Action:* I added detailed structured logging around the payment flow and deployed to a staging environment under load testing. After reviewing the logs, I found a race condition in the session token validation logic where two concurrent requests were modifying shared state. I refactored that section to use a thread-safe approach and added a regression test specifically for concurrent requests.

*Result:* Error responses in production dropped to zero after the fix was deployed. The regression test caught a similar issue introduced by a different team member several months later.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For behavioral questions: Use STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result). Keep each part concise. Interviewers at HCL typically want to hear about real work from your own experience, not hypothetical scenarios. If you draw a blank, it is fine to pause and say 'let me think of a strong example for that.'

For concept and theory questions: A three-part structure works well. First, state your understanding of the concept clearly in one or two sentences. Second, give a concrete example from your own work. Third, mention trade-offs or edge cases. Interviewers notice immediately when someone has only memorised a definition without ever applying it.

For system design questions: Start by asking clarifying questions about expected scale, latency requirements, read-to-write ratio, and any hard constraints. Then sketch a high-level architecture verbally before going deep on any single component. Candidates report that jumping into solutions before understanding scope is one of the most common ways to lose marks in design rounds.

For coding questions: Think out loud. HCL interviewers typically want to see your reasoning process, not just a correct final answer. Walk through your approach before writing any code, state your expected time and space complexity, and name any edge cases you are considering. If you get stuck, narrate your thought process rather than going silent.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Candidates who have interviewed at HCL Technologies report several consistent qualities that interviewers look for.

Clear communication is valued as much as technical correctness. If you cannot explain what your code does or why you made a design choice, even a correct answer leaves doubt in the interviewer's mind.

Ownership mindset comes up often in post-interview feedback. Interviewers ask about past projects to see whether you think like someone who sees a problem through to completion, or someone who hands off when things get difficult. Use 'I' when describing your specific contributions, not just 'we.'

Depth over breadth matters at the technical level. HCL projects often involve deep integration work with enterprise clients. Knowing one or two technologies well and being able to explain their internals is more valued than listing many tools you have briefly touched.

Genuine curiosity about the business problem behind the technology is noticed. Interviewers at a services company appreciate candidates who ask about the client context or the business outcome, not just the tech stack.

Collaboration signals are read from how you talk about past teammates. Avoid framing previous colleagues or managers negatively. Focus on how you navigated challenges together and what you learned from those experiences.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused two-to-three week plan that candidates report works well for HCL Software Engineer interviews.

Week one: Technical foundations. Revisit core data structures (arrays, linked lists, trees, graphs, hash maps) and common algorithms (sorting, searching, recursion, dynamic programming basics). Practice writing clean, working code from scratch in your primary language. Avoid relying on autocomplete or IDE hints during practice sessions.

Week two: System design and company context. Study common design patterns: caching strategies, load balancing, database indexing and sharding, and message queues. Separately, read about HCL's key service areas (digital transformation, cloud migration, engineering services) so you can connect your past experience to the kind of work they do. This context matters a lot in the 'why HCL' question.

Week three: Behavioural prep and mock interviews. Prepare three to four strong STAR stories covering: a difficult technical problem you solved, a conflict or disagreement you navigated well, a time you took initiative beyond your defined role, and a project that did not go as planned with clear lessons learned. Do at least two timed mock interviews out loud, with a friend or on a practice platform.

On the day: Log in or arrive a few minutes early. Have your STAR stories ready but do not recite them mechanically. Ask at least one genuine question at the end about the team, the project, or the tech stack. Interviewers remember candidates who seemed genuinely interested, not just prepared.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping to code before clarifying the problem. Interviewers often leave problems intentionally ambiguous to see how you handle uncertainty. Ask one or two targeted questions before writing a single line.

Reciting definitions without examples. Saying 'polymorphism means many forms' is not enough. Every concept you mention should be followed immediately by a real example from your own work or projects.

Underselling your contribution. Many candidates default to 'we did X' when describing projects. Use 'I' when describing your specific decisions and actions. Interviewers cannot give you credit for what the team did collectively.

Ignoring edge cases in coding problems. Always mention what happens with empty input, null values, duplicate entries, or very large inputs. Even if you do not code every edge case, naming them shows engineering maturity.

Asking no questions at the end. Candidates who have nothing to ask signal low interest in the role. Prepare two genuine questions about the team's current challenges, the tech stack, or what success looks like in the first few months.

Using memorised answers that do not fit the question. If your prepared STAR story does not actually answer what was asked, interviewers notice immediately. Stay flexible and pick the most relevant example, or adapt one on the fly rather than forcing a poor fit.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the HCL Technologies Software Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report that the process typically involves three to four rounds. These commonly include an initial HR or recruiter screening, one or two technical rounds covering coding and core concepts, and a final managerial or senior HR round. The exact number can vary by role level and the specific team you are interviewing with.

Does HCL ask heavy Data Structures and Algorithms (DSA) questions?

For fresher and entry-level roles (0-2 years experience), candidates report a moderate DSA focus covering arrays, strings, and recursion. For mid and senior roles, system design depth and past project ownership tend to matter more than algorithmic puzzles. It is worth preparing DSA basics regardless of your experience level, as online assessments early in the process often include them.

How long does the HCL hiring process take from application to offer?

Candidates report that the timeline varies quite a bit by hiring channel. Campus and bulk hiring drives can move faster and sometimes conclude within a few weeks. Lateral hires for experienced roles typically take longer depending on internal approvals and team availability. Following up politely with the recruiter after each round is acceptable and often expected.

Does HCL conduct an online coding test before the interview rounds?

Many candidates report an online assessment early in the process, covering coding problems and sometimes aptitude or verbal sections. Fresher hiring drives in particular tend to include these tests as a filtering step. Preparing for the assessment the same way you would prepare for the interview itself is the safest approach.

What salary can I expect as a Software Engineer at HCL Technologies?

Based on publicly reported data and platforms like Glassdoor and levels.fyi, entry-level Software Engineers at HCL typically see offers in the 6-12 LPA range, while mid-level roles commonly land in the 15-25 LPA range. Actual offers depend on your specific experience, technical skills, and how the negotiation goes.

Should I negotiate the offer I receive from HCL?

Yes. Candidates who negotiate respectfully and with market data behind them commonly report getting some movement on the initial offer. Know your market rate using publicly reported sources like Glassdoor or levels.fyi, and frame the conversation around the value you bring to the role. A polite, evidence-based ask is rarely held against you.
