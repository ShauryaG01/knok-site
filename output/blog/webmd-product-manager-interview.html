01 Overview

Overview

WebMD is one of the most visited health information platforms in the world, helping users look up symptoms, medications, and wellness advice. As a Product Manager at WebMD, you work at the intersection of consumer health, clinical accuracy, and digital product strategy. The role demands comfort with regulated content, advertising-driven business models, and a highly anxious user base that often arrives on the platform worried about their health.

As of early July 2026, knok jobradar tracks 86 open roles at WebMD, which signals active hiring across the organisation. Across India more broadly, Product Manager demand is strong, with 2,009 PM listings tracked as of the same period. Bangalore leads with 271 openings, followed by Delhi (177), Mumbai (56), Pune (31), Hyderabad (24), and Chennai (18).

Salary bands for PM roles in India, based on knok jobradar data, range from 12-20 LPA at the Associate PM level up to 55-90+ LPA for Group or Principal PMs.

WebMD interviews typically span several rounds covering product sense, behavioral questions, data and metrics, and health-domain knowledge. Candidates report that interviewers pay close attention to how you balance user empathy with trustworthiness, and whether you understand the unique responsibility of building products around medical information.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up frequently in WebMD PM interviews, based on candidate reports and the nature of the health-tech domain.

  1. How would you improve WebMD's symptom checker to increase user trust and reduce anxiety-driven drop-offs?
  2. WebMD earns a large share of revenue from advertising. How do you balance ad revenue goals with a clean, trustworthy user experience?
  3. Describe a time you used data to reverse a product decision you were personally invested in.
  4. How would you prioritise features for a WebMD mobile app redesign given limited engineering bandwidth?
  5. What metrics would you use to measure the success of a new WebMD health tracker feature?
  6. A group of physicians writes in to say WebMD content is misleading patients. How do you respond as a PM?
  7. How would you design a personalised health dashboard for returning WebMD users?
  8. Tell me about a time you worked with medically or legally sensitive content. How did you manage the compliance requirements?
  9. WebMD is considering expanding into telehealth. Walk us through how you would define the MVP.
  10. How do you think about health literacy and accessibility when building features for a broad consumer audience?
  11. Describe a product launch that did not go as planned. What happened and what did you change?
  12. How would you use AI to improve the WebMD experience without compromising medical accuracy?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How would you improve WebMD's symptom checker?

*Situation:* At a previous company, I owned a health-adjacent feature where users searched for product side effects. Session data showed users dropping off after seeing a long, undifferentiated list of symptoms with no severity context.

*Task:* My goal was to improve completion rates and reduce the anxiety spiral where users convinced themselves of worst-case diagnoses.

*Action:* I partnered with our medical content team to add severity tiers and a filter showing the most common outcomes for the user's age and gender. I designed an A/B test comparing the original list view against a tiered view that surfaced common symptoms first. I also added a clear prompt to 'speak to a doctor' at the point where users were most likely to catastrophise.

*Result:* Completion rates improved and post-session satisfaction scores rose. Anxiety-driven exits fell, and more users reached the 'next steps' section of the flow, which was the core engagement goal.

---

Q: Describe a time you used data to reverse a product decision.

*Situation:* We had launched a daily health metric reminder feature that the team was excited about. I owned the post-launch success metrics.

*Task:* My target was to improve daily active usage through the new notification flow.

*Action:* Two weeks in, the engagement data looked mixed. I dug deeper and found that users receiving notifications at a high frequency were churning at a rate noticeably higher than users who received none at all. Despite pushback that it was 'too soon to conclude anything,' I took the finding to the team and proposed pausing the aggressive send cadence. I built a small prototype for user-controlled frequency settings and ran it past several users in informal sessions.

*Result:* After we shifted to opt-in frequency controls, churn among notification recipients dropped. Daily active usage grew more steadily because we were retaining users rather than overwhelming them.

---

Q: Tell me about a product launch that did not go as planned.

*Situation:* We were launching an appointment booking integration for a healthcare client. Everything passed in staging and the team was confident going into launch day.

*Task:* I was the PM coordinating the release across engineering, content, and the client's clinical operations team.

*Action:* Shortly after go-live, we discovered the integration was pulling stale availability data from the client's backend system. I set up an emergency sync with engineering and the client, communicated the issue transparently rather than waiting for them to notice it themselves, and worked with the team to deploy a targeted fix. Once stable, I added a real-time data freshness monitor to the internal dashboard so the team would catch similar issues proactively.

*Result:* The issue was resolved within a few hours. The client appreciated the honest communication and the monitoring solution we put in place. That incident became the standard template for how we handled third-party integrations going forward.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For product improvement questions (symptom checker, dashboard, telehealth MVP):

Start by clarifying the goal, whether it is engagement, trust, revenue, or retention. Then identify the user and their core job to be done. List two or three pain points with evidence or reasoning. Propose a solution, explain the trade-offs, and describe how you would measure success. WebMD interviewers want to see that you factor in clinical accuracy and the emotional state of health-anxious users, not just conversion metrics.

For prioritisation questions:

Use a simple impact-versus-effort framing. State your criteria explicitly (user value, business value, feasibility, regulatory risk). A quick table can help:

FeatureUser ImpactEffortRegulatory RiskPriority
Symptom severity tiersHighLowMediumP1
Telehealth bookingHighHighHighP2
Ad refresh optimisationMediumLowLowP2

Always tie your final ranking back to the stated business goal.

For metrics questions:

Cover three layers: engagement (sessions, return visits), quality (content accuracy flags, doctor escalation rates), and business (ad revenue per session, subscription conversion if applicable). Show that you understand WebMD's dual mandate of serving users and advertisers.

For behavioral questions (STAR format):

Situation (brief context), Task (your specific role and goal), Action (what you personally did, not what the team did), Result (measurable outcome or clear learning). Keep the situation and task short. Spend the most time on Action.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

WebMD PMs typically look for a few qualities that are specific to health-tech and the company's market position.

Clinical trustworthiness instinct. Interviewers want to see that you understand the weight of health information. Users come to WebMD when they are scared or confused. A candidate who treats the symptom checker like a standard search feature, without acknowledging the emotional and clinical stakes, will lose points quickly.

Comfort with competing stakeholders. WebMD serves users, advertisers, healthcare providers, and sometimes regulatory bodies. Candidates who can hold multiple stakeholder priorities at once, without collapsing them into a single narrative, tend to stand out.

Data literacy without data dependence. Interviewers want to see that you can reason with numbers, but also that you can make defensible calls when data is incomplete or ambiguous. This is common in health contexts where running certain experiments raises ethical questions.

Clear communication under ambiguity. WebMD content affects real health decisions. Interviewers probe whether you communicate product trade-offs clearly, both internally and to users, especially when a feature could be misunderstood or misused.

Health-domain curiosity. You do not need a clinical background, but you should demonstrate genuine curiosity about how people seek and act on health information. Mentioning specific WebMD products or real health-information UX challenges signals preparation and genuine interest.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know the product and the domain.

Use WebMD thoroughly as a real user would. Go through the symptom checker, browse a medication page, look at the health news feed, and note where the experience feels trustworthy or where it creates confusion. Read recent coverage of WebMD in health-tech news to understand the company's strategic direction. Note the role of advertising in the business model.

Week 2: Build your story bank.

Map your past experience to the question types listed above. Prepare at least two STAR stories for each category: data-driven decisions, cross-functional conflict, a launch gone wrong, and a prioritisation call under constraint. For each story, prepare a one-sentence summary you can deliver quickly if asked to 'give me a quick example.'

Week 3: Practise structured product thinking.

Pick a real WebMD feature and redesign it under a realistic constraint. Talk through your reasoning out loud. Ask a peer to challenge your assumptions. Practise the prioritisation table format so you can sketch one quickly in a live interview. Practise delivering a verbal product case at a pace that feels conversational, not rushed.

In the week before your interview:

Review WebMD's recent product announcements or any publicly available information about their roadmap. Prepare two or three thoughtful questions for your interviewers that show you have engaged with the product seriously. Confirm the interview format with your recruiter so you know whether to expect a take-home case, a live design exercise, or a purely behavioral round.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Ignoring the health context. The most common error is treating WebMD like a standard consumer app and proposing engagement-maximising features without acknowledging clinical accuracy or user anxiety. Always factor in the health stakes.

Vague metrics. Saying 'I would track engagement' is not enough. Name specific metrics, explain what they measure, and acknowledge the trade-offs. For example, higher session length on WebMD could mean better engagement, or it could mean users are more confused and cannot find what they need.

Over-indexing on the tech. Candidates sometimes spend too much time on the AI or ML layer and not enough on the user problem it solves. WebMD interviewers want to understand your user empathy first, your technical instincts second.

Not acknowledging the advertiser. WebMD's business model depends significantly on advertising. Candidates who build entire product visions with no mention of how ads fit into the experience signal a gap in business acumen.

Weak STAR stories. Many candidates describe what the 'team' did rather than what they personally did. The Action section of a STAR answer must be about your specific decisions and contributions, not a group narrative.

Failing to ask clarifying questions. In product design or case questions, jumping straight to a solution without asking about goals, constraints, or user segments is a common tell that a candidate is not yet thinking like a PM.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the WebMD PM interview typically have?

Candidates typically report a process spanning a recruiter screen, one or two hiring manager rounds, a product or case exercise, and a panel with cross-functional stakeholders. The exact structure varies by level and team. It is worth confirming the format with your recruiter before you prepare so you invest your time in the right areas.

Do I need a healthcare or medical background to interview at WebMD?

No clinical background is required, but you should demonstrate genuine curiosity about health information and the unique challenges of building in a regulated, high-stakes domain. Candidates who have worked in adjacent areas such as insurance tech, fitness apps, or patient-facing tools tend to have relevant examples ready. Show that you have used the product and thought critically about it.

What salary can I expect as a PM at WebMD?

Salary data specific to WebMD India roles is limited in public sources. Across the broader Indian PM market, knok jobradar data shows bands of 12-20 LPA at the Associate PM level, 24-40 LPA for mid-level PM roles with three to six years of experience, 40-60 LPA for Senior PMs, and 55-90+ LPA for Group or Principal PMs. For WebMD-specific figures, platforms like Glassdoor or levels.fyi may have community-reported data worth checking.

Is there a take-home assignment in the WebMD PM interview process?

Some candidates report receiving a take-home case or a product design prompt before one of the later rounds. Others go through a fully live process. Because this varies by team and role level, ask your recruiter directly whether a take-home component is part of your specific loop. If there is one, treat it as a chance to demonstrate your written communication and structured thinking, not just your ideas.

How should I handle the advertising versus user experience tension in my answers?

Acknowledge both sides explicitly rather than picking one. A strong answer frames it as a tension that good product work resolves over time: features that increase user trust and return visits ultimately make ad inventory more valuable. Show that you understand the business model and can hold both user and business goals in your thinking at the same time.
