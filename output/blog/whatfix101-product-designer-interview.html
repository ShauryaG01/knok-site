01 Overview

Overview

Whatfix is a Bangalore-based digital adoption platform (DAP) company. Their core product helps enterprise businesses add in-app guidance, interactive walkthroughs, and smart tooltips on top of any web software, so employees and customers get up to speed faster. A Product Designer at Whatfix works on two layers: the experience inside enterprise applications, and the authoring platform that Whatfix's own customers use to build those guided flows.

Candidates typically go through a portfolio screening, a take-home design assignment, and two to three interview rounds covering design thinking, product sense, and cross-functional collaboration. The panel commonly includes a senior designer, a product manager, and sometimes a design lead. Whatfix candidates report that the process emphasises your ability to explain decisions, not just show polished screens.

Whatfix currently has 36 open Product Designer roles tracked by knok jobradar, making it one of the more active hiring companies in India right now. Across India, there are 393 Product Designer openings as of July 2026, with Bangalore leading at 62. Salary ranges for Product Designers in India, as commonly cited in industry surveys, run from 6-12 LPA at entry level to 36-55+ LPA at lead or principal level.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Here are 12 questions candidates report being asked at Whatfix Product Designer interviews:

  1. Walk us through a project where you improved user onboarding. What decisions did you make, and what trade-offs did you weigh?
  1. Whatfix serves enterprise users who are not always tech-savvy. How do you design for a non-technical audience inside complex business software?
  1. How do you approach designing in-app guidance (tooltips, walkthroughs, banners) so it helps users without feeling intrusive or patronising?
  1. Describe a time you had to simplify a very complex workflow into a clean, usable interface. How did you approach it?
  1. How do you balance business goals against user needs when they point in different directions?
  1. Tell us about your experience with design systems. Have you helped build or maintain one, and what was your specific contribution?
  1. How do you decide whether a UX problem calls for in-app guidance versus a redesign of the underlying interface itself?
  1. Describe a data-informed design decision you made. Which metrics or research methods did you rely on?
  1. How do you design for accessibility in a B2B SaaS context, where the buyer and the daily end-user are different people?
  1. Tell us about a time when stakeholder or engineering feedback significantly changed your design. How did you respond?
  1. How do you work with engineers to make sure your designs survive implementation without losing quality?
  1. What does great UX look like inside an authoring tool, where your user is building experiences for someone else?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through a project where you improved user onboarding.

*Situation:* At my previous company, new users were landing on a feature-heavy dashboard with no clear starting point. Industry surveys commonly cite first-session drop-off as a top retention problem in B2B SaaS, and this product was no exception.

*Task:* I was asked to redesign the first-run experience so users could reach their first 'aha moment' faster, without disrupting the navigation for returning users.

*Action:* I ran five moderated usability sessions with new users and mapped exactly where they stalled. I found the core issue was that users did not know which module matched their job role. I proposed a short role-selection step at signup to personalise the default dashboard view. I worked with the PM to trim the flow to three screens, confirmed feasibility with engineering, and tested two prototypes with a fresh set of participants before handing off.

*Result:* The revised onboarding shipped after two sprint cycles. Industry surveys commonly cite that targeted personalisation reduces early drop-off, though exact impact depends on product type and segment. The key takeaway I carried forward: onboarding is a trust problem, not just a UI problem.

---

Q: How do you decide whether a UX problem calls for in-app guidance versus a redesign of the interface?

*Situation:* At a fintech startup, users were repeatedly calling support to ask how to initiate a bank transfer inside a newly launched module. The support volume was high enough that the PM wanted an immediate fix.

*Task:* My job was to diagnose whether this was a discoverability problem or a design problem, then recommend the right solution.

*Action:* I reviewed support transcripts and ran a short contextual inquiry with three users. I found two distinct issues: one button label was genuinely ambiguous ('Initiate' versus 'Send'), and a confirmation screen had no visible back action. I recommended fixing the label and adding a clear back button as permanent design changes. I used a one-time tooltip only for the confirmation screen during the very first session, not as a permanent overlay.

*Result:* The approach separated the symptom (users getting lost) from the root cause (unclear UI). This distinction is exactly what Whatfix interviewers typically probe for: knowing when a tooltip is the right tool, and when it is just covering a design problem.

---

Q: Tell us about a time stakeholder feedback significantly changed your design.

*Situation:* I was designing an analytics dashboard for a SaaS product. My initial design used a card-based layout validated across three usability sessions with daily end-users.

*Task:* Before development started, a senior stakeholder flagged that enterprise buyers also needed a summary view they could share in quarterly business reviews. My research had not covered this persona.

*Action:* I ran two interviews with customer success managers who attended those reviews. I found buyers needed a printable, single-screen summary. I adapted the design to include a collapsible 'executive summary' panel at the top, keeping the card layout intact below it so daily users were unaffected.

*Result:* Both personas were served without adding a separate page. I also updated my research checklist going forward to always ask: 'Who else uses or reviews the output of this screen?' That habit came directly from this correction.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For 'walk me through your work' questions: Start with the problem context (what the product does, who the user is), then move to your specific responsibility, the process you followed, the trade-offs you made, and what shipped or what you learned. Avoid listing tools. Keep the focus on decisions.

For 'how do you approach X' questions: State a brief principle, then back it with a concrete example from your work. Interviewers at Whatfix typically want to see a real point of view, not a recitation of a generic design process.

For conflict or collaboration questions: Use a structure of: the situation, your first instinct, what you did to understand the other perspective, and the outcome. Show that you can hold your design rationale while still being open to new information.

For 'what is good UX' questions at Whatfix specifically: Ground your answer in enterprise or B2B software. Concepts like progressive disclosure, role-based defaults, and reducing cognitive load during task-critical moments land well with their product team. Avoid defaulting to consumer-app patterns as your reference frame.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Whatfix interviewers commonly look for four qualities in Product Designer candidates.

Product thinking, not just visual craft. Can you explain why you made a design decision, not just describe how it looks? Candidates report that portfolio walkthroughs focused only on aesthetics tend to receive weak scores.

Understanding of enterprise UX constraints. Enterprise software involves legacy integrations, IT governance, and non-technical end-users who did not choose the product themselves. Showing you have designed under these constraints, even in one project, is a strong signal.

Strong collaboration habits. Whatfix is a product-led company. Designers work closely with product managers, engineers, and customer success teams. Interviewers often probe for specific examples of cross-functional influence, not just a general claim of 'I worked with the team.'

Clarity in communication. Because Whatfix builds tools that help other products communicate better with users, interviewers pay close attention to how clearly you explain your own thinking. Vague or jargon-heavy answers get penalised.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Understand the product deeply. Sign up for a Whatfix trial or watch their official product demo. Use the authoring tool yourself, note what works well, and note what feels unintuitive. Candidates report that interviewers ask 'have you used our product?' early in the process, and a genuine, specific answer stands out.

Week 2: Prepare your portfolio stories. Pick two to three projects that show onboarding design, workflow simplification, or data-heavy UI work. For each, prepare a five-minute verbal walkthrough covering problem, users, decisions, trade-offs, and outcome. Practise saying it out loud, not just in your head.

Week 3: Practise design exercises. Whatfix typically includes a take-home or live design challenge. Common prompts candidates report include redesigning a settings page, designing an onboarding flow for a new feature, or adding a capability to an existing enterprise tool. Practise working under a time limit and narrating your decisions as you sketch.

Ongoing: Read up on how digital adoption platforms differ from traditional product UX, and be ready to articulate why that distinction matters for design decisions. 07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating the portfolio review as a visuals presentation. Showing polished screens without explaining the decisions behind them is the most commonly reported weak point. Interviewers want to understand your reasoning, not admire your Figma components.

Ignoring the enterprise context. Designs built around consumer-app assumptions (heavy gamification, bright onboarding animations, aggressive personalisation) often get pushback at Whatfix. Enterprise users work under IT policies, role hierarchies, and task pressure that consumer apps rarely have. Show you know the difference.

Recommending in-app guidance as the answer to every UX problem. Candidates who reach for a tooltip or walkthrough for every issue signal they do not understand when guidance is the right tool. Interviewers want to see you diagnose the root cause first and earn the right to recommend guidance.

Thin collaboration stories. Saying 'I worked closely with the team' without a specific example does not land. Prepare at least two stories where you either changed your design based on engineering constraints or influenced a product decision based on your research.

Not asking questions at the end. Candidates who ask nothing are typically seen as low-interest. Prepare two to three genuine questions about the team's current design challenges or how they measure design quality, not about perks or leave policies.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Whatfix Product Designer interview typically have?

Candidates typically report three to four rounds: a portfolio screening call, a take-home design assignment, a design review with the hiring panel, and a final culture or leadership round. The exact number can vary by seniority and team. Ask your recruiter after the first call to confirm the current structure, as processes change over time.

Is there a design assignment, and how long does it usually take?

Most candidates report receiving a take-home assignment involving designing a feature or improving an existing flow. The brief is typically open-ended to see how you scope and frame problems. Candidates report spending four to six hours on it, though some invest more. Focus on clear problem framing and written decision rationale, not just polished visual output.

What tools does Whatfix expect Product Designers to know?

Figma is the most commonly cited tool in their job descriptions and interview conversations. Familiarity with prototyping, component libraries, and basic handoff workflows in Figma is expected. Deep expertise in motion design or front-end coding is not typically required at the interview stage, but understanding how your designs translate into engineering constraints is important to demonstrate.
