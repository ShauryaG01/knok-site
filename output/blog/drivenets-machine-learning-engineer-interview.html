02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates report questions clustering around three themes: ML fundamentals, systems and scale, and networking domain awareness. Here are the questions that come up most often:

  1. How would you design an anomaly detection system for large-scale network traffic data? Walk through feature engineering, model selection, and how you handle concept drift.
  1. Describe a time you deployed an ML model into a latency-constrained production environment. What trade-offs did you make?
  1. How do you handle highly imbalanced data when building a classifier to detect rare network faults?
  1. Explain how graph-based machine learning could model relationships between nodes in a distributed network topology.
  1. A model that performed well in staging starts degrading two weeks after going live. How do you debug it?
  1. How would you design a real-time feature pipeline for packet-level telemetry data at scale?
  1. What is your experience with time-series forecasting? Which approaches do you prefer for network traffic prediction and why?
  1. How do you reduce false positives in a fault detection model without significantly hurting recall?

  1. How would you explain a model's confidence score to a network operations engineer with no ML background?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use these as templates and adapt the specifics to your own experience.

---

Q: How do you handle highly imbalanced data when detecting rare network faults?

*Situation:* At a previous role, the team had a binary classifier to flag network link failures. The positive class (actual failures) made up a very small portion of the training set, and the model kept predicting 'no fault' nearly every time.

*Task:* My task was to rework the training approach so the model could reliably catch true faults without flooding the ops team with false alarms.

*Action:* I first audited why the class imbalance was so extreme, confirming it reflected real-world rarity rather than a data collection gap. I ran parallel experiments with SMOTE oversampling and class-weight adjustment. I switched the primary evaluation metric from accuracy to F1-score and precision-recall AUC. I also worked with domain engineers to define the relative cost of a missed fault versus a false alarm, which guided my final decision threshold.

*Result:* The tuned model caught significantly more true faults in held-out evaluation, and the ops team reported a clear drop in alert fatigue after a pilot deployment. The approach was documented and adopted as a standard pattern for future imbalanced-class work on the team.

---

Q: Walk us through a time you debugged a model that was degrading in production.

*Situation:* A traffic volume forecasting model I owned started producing noticeably worse predictions about three weeks after deployment. Our monitoring dashboard showed a steady rise in mean absolute error.

*Task:* I needed to identify the root cause quickly because downstream capacity planning decisions depended on those forecasts.

*Action:* I compared the distribution of incoming features against the training data distribution and found that a key upstream data source had changed its sampling interval, altering the shape of the input features. I reengineered the affected features to be robust to that change, retrained on a refreshed data window, and added a data-drift detector to the monitoring pipeline so the same issue would trigger an alert rather than a silent model decay.

*Result:* Forecast accuracy returned to baseline within the next release cycle. The drift detector caught two further upstream changes in the following months before they could affect predictions.

---

Q: How would you explain a model's confidence score to a non-technical stakeholder?

*Situation:* I had built a fault-prediction model for a network operations team, and the on-call engineers were ignoring alerts because they did not trust the raw confidence scores.

*Task:* I needed to rebuild that trust by making the model's outputs understandable and actionable for people with no ML background.

*Action:* I stopped showing raw probability scores and instead bucketed outputs into three plain labels: 'High risk, act now', 'Watch this link', and 'Looks normal'. I ran a calibration analysis and shared a simple table showing how often the model had been right historically in each bucket. I also added a one-line explanation per alert using SHAP-derived feature contributions, phrased in network terms the team already understood.

*Result:* The ops team began acting on high-risk alerts consistently. In a follow-up review three months later, they confirmed that the plain-language explanations made alerts feel credible rather than mysterious.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

The STAR structure (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the most reliable format for behavioral questions. Keep the Situation and Task brief, two to three sentences each. Spend most of your time on the Action, which is where interviewers assess your technical depth and decision-making.

For system design questions, use a four-step structure: clarify requirements and constraints first, sketch the high-level architecture, go deep on the ML-specific components (data pipeline, model choice, feature engineering), then address production concerns such as latency, monitoring, and retraining. DRIVENETS interviewers typically care more about your trade-off reasoning than about reaching one single 'correct' answer.

For ML fundamentals questions, lead with the concept in plain terms, give a concrete example from your own work, then discuss the limitations or failure modes. This shows both depth and intellectual honesty.

For domain-specific networking questions, framing your answer as 'I would first seek to understand X from the domain team, then apply Y approach' is well received. Candidates report that genuine curiosity about the domain is valued over claiming expertise you do not have.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Production-first thinking. DRIVENETS ships ML into high-reliability network infrastructure. Interviewers consistently look for candidates who think about monitoring, data drift, latency budgets, and failure modes from the start, not as afterthoughts.

Systems intuition. A pure research mindset does not fit this role. Candidates who can reason about scale, feature pipelines, serving infrastructure, and the full model lifecycle stand out strongly.

Domain curiosity. You do not need a networking background, but showing genuine interest in the problem space and asking good questions about the domain tends to earn strong feedback from the panel.

Communication clarity. Because ML models at DRIVENETS feed into decisions made by network operations engineers, interviewers probe whether you can translate model outputs into plain, actionable language.

Ownership. Behavioral questions are designed to assess whether you drove outcomes or were a passive participant. Use 'I' for your own contributions and 'we' for team efforts, and be specific about the decisions you personally made.

Preparation Plan

2Systems and MLOpsFeature pipelines, model serving, monitoring, drift detection, A/B testing

On networking basics: you do not need to become a network engineer. Spend a few hours understanding what network telemetry data looks like, why latency and packet loss matter, and what disaggregated networking means at a high level. This context will make your answers feel grounded.

On coding prep: candidates report LeetCode medium-level questions in the coding screen, focusing on arrays, hash maps, and graph traversal. Practise in Python and be ready to reason about time and space complexity out loud.

On behavioural prep: prepare four to five detailed STAR stories covering model deployment, debugging a production issue, cross-functional collaboration, and simplifying a complex result for a non-technical audience. Candidates report at least two behavioural questions per round.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping the 'why' behind design choices. Describing what you built without explaining why you chose one approach over another is a common gap. Always pair a decision with its rationale and the trade-off you accepted.

Ignoring production concerns in system design. Designing an ML system purely for offline metric performance misses what DRIVENETS cares about most. Bring up monitoring, retraining triggers, and latency budgets proactively.

Overclaiming networking expertise. If you do not have a networking background, do not invent one. Honesty paired with curiosity, such as 'I have not worked directly with BGP data, but here is how I would approach learning it', is a far better strategy.

Vague STAR answers. Answers like 'I improved model performance' with no detail on what you specifically did are too thin. Interviewers want concrete actions and observable outcomes, even if you cannot share exact figures.

Not asking clarifying questions. Jumping straight into a system design answer without aligning on scale, latency requirements, or data availability signals poor engineering habits. Taking two minutes to clarify is expected and respected.

Underestimating behavioural rounds. Some candidates prepare heavily for technical rounds and coast through behavioural questions. DRIVENETS values communication and ownership highly, so treat every round as equally important.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the DRIVENETS ML Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report a process that typically includes a recruiter screening call, one to two technical coding interviews, a system design round, and a final set of interviews with the hiring manager and cross-functional team members. The exact number of rounds can vary by team and location. Confirm the structure with your recruiter after the first call so you can plan your preparation accordingly.

Do I need a networking background to get this role at DRIVENETS?

A deep networking background is helpful but candidates report it is not a hard requirement for the ML Engineer role. Interviewers value ML engineering depth and production-mindedness more than domain expertise. Spending a few hours understanding network telemetry concepts and showing genuine curiosity about the problem space is usually enough to perform well on domain-related questions.

What coding languages does DRIVENETS expect for the ML Engineer role?

Candidates report that Python is the expected primary language for ML-related coding questions, with familiarity with standard ML libraries assumed. Some teams may also ask about C++ for performance-critical components. It is worth asking your recruiter whether the specific team you are interviewing for works in both languages, so you know what depth of C++ preparation is needed.

How should I prepare for the system design round?

Focus on ML system design rather than pure infrastructure design. Practise designing end-to-end pipelines covering data ingestion, feature engineering, model training, serving, and monitoring. For DRIVENETS specifically, be ready to discuss designs for anomaly detection or time-series forecasting on streaming data. Practising out loud with a timer helps with pacing, since candidates report these rounds typically run under an hour.

What salary can I expect as an ML Engineer at DRIVENETS in India?
