03 Interview Process

Interview Process

Candidates at similar people-focused companies typically move through three to five rounds. The exact number at Higher People may vary by function and seniority, but a standard process tends to look like this:

Round 1: Application and Resume Screening
Your resume goes through an initial review, either by an ATS system or an HR team member. Tailor your resume to the job description closely, using the same keywords and role language the posting uses.

Round 2: HR or Talent Acquisition Call
A recruiter or HR team member reaches out to check your interest, current role, notice period, and CTC expectations. Keep this conversation warm, clear, and direct. Have your expected CTC and earliest joining date ready before the call.

Round 3: Functional or Domain Round
This is where your actual skills are assessed. Depending on the role, you may face situational questions ('Tell me about a time when...'), a short take-home assignment, or a structured case discussion. Prepare two or three strong examples from your past that show real, specific outcomes.

Round 4: Managerial or Panel Round
You speak with a senior leader or a cross-functional panel. Questions here go deeper into how you think, lead, and handle pressure. Use the STAR method (Situation, Task, Action, Result) to structure your answers and keep them under two minutes each.

Round 5: HR Discussion and Offer
If the earlier rounds go well, HR revisits compensation, joining timelines, and role scope. Come prepared with a clear sense of your market rate and a firm answer on your notice period.

04 What They Look For

What They Look For

People skills come first. Given the company name and focus, Higher People likely places a premium on how well you communicate, collaborate, and build trust with others. In every round, interviewers are watching not just what you say but how you say it.

Relevant domain expertise. For the specific role you are applying to, you need to show you have done similar work before and can contribute quickly. Be specific about your experience rather than speaking in broad generalities.

A growth mindset. Companies that build their identity around people tend to value candidates who talk openly about learning, feedback, and improvement, not just past wins. Sharing what you have learned from a setback is often more impressive than listing achievements alone.

Problem-solving and ownership. Across functions, interviewers want to see that you take initiative. Use examples that show you spotted a problem and fixed it without being asked, not just that you completed assigned tasks on time.

Cultural alignment. A name like Higher People suggests a belief in developing individuals and teams. If you can speak to how you have helped others grow, or how you have contributed to a positive team environment, bring those stories into your conversations.

05 How To Prepare

How To Prepare

Research the company before every round. Read through the company website, LinkedIn page, and any recent news or announcements. Know what they do, who they serve, and what sets them apart. Interviewers consistently notice when a candidate has genuinely done their homework.

Customise your resume for each role. With 42 open roles across different functions, the same generic resume does not work for all of them. Adjust your summary and key achievements to reflect the language and priorities in each specific job description.

Prepare your STAR stories. Pick four or five strong examples from your career that cover communication, problem-solving, leadership, and handling a difficult situation. Practise telling each one in under two minutes, with a clear result at the end.

Know the impact of your work. Be ready to describe what changed because of your contribution. Concrete outcomes stay with interviewers far longer than vague statements like 'I improved the process' or 'I led the team.'

Prepare smart questions to ask. Have two or three genuine questions ready for each round. Ask about what success looks like in the role in the first few months, how the team handles feedback, or what the biggest challenge the team is working through right now. Avoid leading with salary questions in early rounds.

Practise speaking, not just reading. A mock interview with a friend or a recorded run-through reveals filler words, pacing issues, and clarity gaps that reading your answers to yourself never catches.
