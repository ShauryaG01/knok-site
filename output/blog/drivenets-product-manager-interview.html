01 Overview

Overview

DriveNets is a network software company that builds cloud-native, disaggregated networking solutions used by tier-1 telcos and large internet service providers worldwide. Their flagship product, DNOS (DriveNets Network OS), allows service providers to run carrier-grade networking on commodity, white-box hardware rather than proprietary equipment from traditional vendors. This puts their PMs at the intersection of deep networking technology, enterprise B2B sales, and cloud-native software development.

With 41 open roles currently on knok jobradar, DriveNets is in an active hiring phase. PM interviews here are typically more technical than at a consumer or SaaS company. Candidates report rounds covering product strategy, technical depth in networking, behavioral leadership, and cross-functional collaboration with engineering and sales. Expect your understanding of how telcos and ISPs make large purchasing decisions to be tested directly.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates who have interviewed at DriveNets for PM roles typically report questions across three areas: product thinking for complex infrastructure, technical depth in networking, and enterprise B2B strategy. Here are commonly reported question types:

  1. Disaggregated networking prioritization: DriveNets sells to tier-1 telcos. How would you prioritize a feature a large carrier urgently needs when it conflicts with your existing platform roadmap?
  1. Success metrics for DNOS: How would you define and measure success for a new release of DriveNets Network OS deployed across thousands of routers at a service provider?
  1. Customer escalation: A major ISP reports that a recent DNOS update caused routing instability in their production network. Walk us through how you would handle this as the PM.
  1. Competitive displacement: DriveNets competes with Cisco, Juniper, and Nokia. How would you approach a deal where the customer has a long-standing relationship with a legacy vendor?
  1. Simplifying complexity: How would you explain disaggregated networking to a CTO who has only ever bought proprietary networking hardware?
  1. Multi-segment roadmap: Your customers range from global hyperscalers to regional ISPs with very different needs. How do you build a roadmap that serves both without becoming a feature factory?
  1. Decision under uncertainty: Tell me about a time you made a major product call with incomplete technical information. How did you decide, and what was the outcome?
  1. Partner dependency: DriveNets relies on hardware partnerships. A key partner's hardware limitation is blocking a feature on your roadmap. How do you handle it?
  1. Pricing strategy: How would you think about pricing for a new DriveNets module targeting mid-market ISPs who are more price-sensitive than your usual tier-1 customers?
  1. Market outlook: Where do you see the network software and disaggregation market heading over the next few years, and how should DriveNets position itself?
  1. Cross-functional alignment: Describe a time you worked closely with sales and engineering to move a complex enterprise deal forward. What was specifically your role?
  1. Conflicting customer demands: Two of your largest customers want contradictory features in the same release cycle. How do you decide, and how do you communicate it?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a time you made a major product decision with incomplete technical information.

*Situation:* At my previous company, we built network monitoring software for enterprise customers. Midway through a release cycle, a key customer asked us to add support for a new routing protocol standard that our engineering team said was still evolving: a working draft rather than a ratified spec.

*Task:* I had to decide whether to build against a draft standard, risking rework later, or hold the feature until the spec was final, risking losing the customer to a competitor who had already announced partial support.

*Action:* I ran a rapid analysis: I interviewed three customer contacts to understand how critical the feature was versus 'nice to have,' consulted our lead network engineer on the likely stability of the draft, and checked what competitors had actually shipped versus merely announced. I also worked with our engineering lead to design a modular architecture so the protocol implementation could be swapped with lower rework cost if the spec changed.

*Result:* We shipped a clearly labeled 'preview' implementation within six weeks. The spec finalized a few months later with minor changes. Our modular approach meant the update took one sprint. The customer renewed and expanded their contract.

---

Q: Describe a time you worked with sales and engineering to move a complex B2B deal forward.

*Situation:* We were in a competitive evaluation with a large telecom operator who needed a custom integration between our platform and their existing OSS (Operations Support System). The sales cycle had stalled for two months.

*Task:* My role as PM was to bridge the gap: sales needed a commitment on delivery timeline, engineering needed a clear scope, and the customer needed confidence we understood their environment.

*Action:* I organised a joint working session with the customer's technical team, our sales engineer, and two of our backend engineers. I came prepared with a draft scope document and led the discussion to separate 'must-have' integration points from 'preferred' ones. After the session, I worked with engineering to estimate the must-haves only, then gave sales a confident timeline with clear assumptions documented in writing.

*Result:* The deal closed within a few weeks of that session. The integration shipped on schedule. The customer later became a reference account for us in the telco segment.

---

Q: How would you handle a situation where two large customers want contradictory features in the same release?

*Situation:* At a previous role, two enterprise ISP customers had opposing needs for a core routing feature: one needed strict QoS enforcement per-tenant, the other needed flexible, best-effort QoS they could configure themselves.

*Task:* Both accounts were renewal-critical. I needed to make a call that served our platform strategy, not just the loudest voice.

*Action:* I mapped both requests against our multi-year product vision. I ran an analysis of the broader customer base to see which model other customers leaned toward. I found that a configurable QoS model, defaulting to strict enforcement, would satisfy both customers if we exposed the right configuration options. I brought this proposal back to both accounts with a clear explanation of the tradeoffs.

*Result:* Both customers accepted the configurable approach. One gave minor pushback on the default setting, which we addressed with documentation and onboarding support. Neither churned. The feature shipped in the following release.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For product strategy questions (roadmap, prioritization, market positioning):

Use a simple three-part structure. First, state your criteria clearly: what matters most for this product and this customer segment? For DriveNets, that usually means reliability and carrier-grade standards before speed of delivery. Second, apply the criteria to the options on the table. Third, state your decision and the tradeoffs you are consciously accepting.

For 'explain X to Y' questions:

This tests your communication clarity for a technically complex product. Use the 'anchor and contrast' approach: anchor on something the listener already knows (proprietary routers from a familiar vendor), then contrast with the new concept (white-box hardware running software-only DNOS). Avoid jargon unless the listener clearly knows it. DriveNets interviewers look for this skill because PMs here often brief non-technical executives at large customer accounts.

For metrics questions:

Structure your answer in three layers. Adoption metrics: is the feature being used at all? Quality metrics: is it working reliably, with low packet loss and fast recovery from faults? Business metrics: is it driving renewal, expansion, or customer satisfaction? For infrastructure products like DNOS, quality metrics carry far more weight than at a consumer app.

For behavioral (STAR) questions:

Keep your Situation and Task brief, one to two sentences each. Spend the most time on Action: specifically what you personally did, not what the team did. Make your Result concrete with an outcome, even if you cannot share exact figures. Candidates report that DriveNets interviewers probe the Action section heavily, so be ready to walk through your decision-making process step by step.

For competitive questions:

Avoid generic 'we have better tech' answers. Frame your response around the customer's buying criteria: what does a telco CTO actually care about when switching from Cisco or Juniper? Usually it is total cost of ownership, reducing vendor lock-in, and proof of carrier-grade reliability. Show you understand the buying process, not just the product features.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Technical depth, not just product instinct. DriveNets builds infrastructure software for service providers. Interviewers want to see that you are comfortable discussing networking concepts at a level that lets you have credible conversations with network engineers. You do not need to write routing configurations, but you should understand why a telco cares deeply about routing protocol stability, latency, and carrier-grade uptime.

Enterprise B2B sensibility. Unlike consumer PMs who run experiments across large user bases, DriveNets PMs work with a small number of large, strategic accounts. Interviewers look for candidates who understand long sales cycles, the importance of reference customers, and how to balance one customer's urgent request against the platform's broader direction.

Cross-functional confidence. PMs at DriveNets work closely with senior network engineers and with enterprise sales teams. Interviewers want to see that you can earn the trust of both groups: engineers respect you because you engage seriously with technical constraints, and sales trusts you because you understand customer relationships and deal dynamics.

Structured thinking under ambiguity. Disaggregated networking is a relatively young product category, and the market is still defining itself. Interviewers want to see that you can make clear, well-reasoned decisions even when market signals, customer requirements, or technical options are incomplete.

Communication clarity. Part of the PM role here involves briefing non-technical decision-makers at large telcos and ISPs. Candidates who can explain complex networking concepts in plain, jargon-free language consistently stand out in the process.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Understand DriveNets and the market

Start by reading DriveNets' publicly available product pages and any press releases or case studies they have published. Focus on understanding what disaggregated networking means, who their main customers are (tier-1 telcos, large ISPs), and who their competitors are (Cisco, Juniper, Nokia, and pure-play software networking vendors). Read one or two industry articles about the shift from proprietary networking hardware to software-defined and white-box approaches.

Week 2: Build your networking vocabulary

You do not need to be a network engineer, but you should be comfortable with terms like BGP, MPLS, white-box hardware, carrier-grade, and network disaggregation. Look up plain-English explanations of each. Practice explaining disaggregated networking to someone with no networking background, using only everyday language.

Week 3: Prepare your stories

Map your experience to the themes DriveNets cares about: B2B enterprise product decisions, working with deeply technical teams, handling customer escalations, managing a roadmap under competing pressures, and competitive strategy. Prepare three to five STAR stories that cover these themes. Make sure each story ends with a concrete result you can describe.

Week 4: Practice and mock interviews

Practice answering the questions listed in this guide out loud, not just in your head. Ask a colleague to mock-interview you and give feedback. Pay particular attention to 'explain X to Y' questions, since those are a known part of the DriveNets process based on candidate reports. Prepare two or three thoughtful questions to ask your interviewers about product direction and team structure.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Going too surface-level on networking. Candidates who treat DriveNets as just another software company and skip the technical context often struggle in the product rounds. You do not need deep engineering knowledge, but showing zero familiarity with networking concepts signals you have not done your homework.

Pitching consumer-PM frameworks. Consumer-PM language and tools, like A/B tests, growth funnels, or user activation metrics, do not translate well to an infrastructure product with a handful of strategic enterprise accounts. If these are your default instincts, practice reframing them in an enterprise B2B context before your interview.

Focusing only on features, not on outcomes. DriveNets interviewers want to see business and customer outcomes, not a list of things you shipped. If your STAR answers end with 'we launched the feature,' push yourself to add what happened after: did the customer renew, did adoption increase, did it unblock a deal?

Underestimating the cross-functional piece. Some candidates talk only about their relationship with engineering and forget that the PM role here involves heavy collaboration with enterprise sales and customer success. Show examples from both sides.

Not asking good questions. Candidates report that DriveNets interviewers pay attention to the questions you ask at the end of each round. Generic questions miss an opportunity to show genuine interest. Ask something specific about product direction, competitive strategy, or how the team handles conflicting customer priorities.

Frequently asked

How technical do I need to be to pass a DriveNets PM interview?

You do not need to be a network engineer, but candidates report that a basic familiarity with networking concepts like BGP, MPLS, and white-box hardware helps significantly. You should be able to discuss why carrier-grade reliability matters and what disaggregation means for a telco's cost structure. Interviewers typically do not quiz you on protocol syntax, but they do expect you to engage seriously with technical tradeoffs and constraints rather than deflecting to 'that's an engineering question.'

How many rounds does the DriveNets PM interview process typically have?

Candidates report a process that typically includes a recruiter or HR screen, one or two product and technical rounds, a behavioral or leadership round, and a final cross-functional discussion. The exact number of rounds varies by role and level, and DriveNets may adjust the structure based on the seniority of the position. Expect the overall process to span a few weeks from first contact to offer, though timelines can shift.

DriveNets is an enterprise B2B company. How is that different from interviewing at a consumer company?

The core difference is your customer base and how you measure success. Consumer PMs talk about large user bases, A/B tests, and activation funnels. At DriveNets, your customers are a small number of large telcos and ISPs, each with significant contracts and long sales cycles. Interviewers want to see that you understand how enterprise deals work, how to manage a roadmap when a handful of accounts have outsized influence, and how to earn trust from both technical engineers and enterprise sales teams.

Is there a take-home assignment or case study in the DriveNets PM process?

Some candidates report a product case study or take-home exercise, but this is not universal across all levels and roles. Candidates typically describe being asked to analyze a market opportunity or prioritize a product scenario relevant to networking or enterprise software. Prepare by practising structured written answers to product strategy questions, keeping your reasoning clear and your recommendations specific rather than generic.
