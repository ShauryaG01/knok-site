03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How did you optimise a React app that was rendering large amounts of data slowly?

*Situation:* My team maintained an analytics dashboard showing campaign performance across hundreds of ad groups. As data volumes grew, the table took several seconds to render and the page became unresponsive on scroll.

*Task:* I was asked to reduce rendering time and make the UI feel snappy without changing the underlying data pipeline.

*Action:* I profiled the component tree using React DevTools and found that every row was re-rendering on any state change. I introduced list virtualisation so only visible rows mounted in the DOM. I also memoised row components and moved filter logic into a useMemo hook so expensive computations did not run on every keystroke.

*Result:* Scroll was smooth even with large datasets, initial render time dropped noticeably by the team's manual test, and the engineering lead adopted the same pattern for two other dashboards.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you built or maintained a reusable component library.

*Situation:* Across three product squads, the same button, input, and modal components were being re-implemented slightly differently, causing design inconsistency and duplicated bug fixes.

*Task:* I was asked to lead a small effort to create a shared component library that all three squads could adopt.

*Action:* I audited existing components, picked the most complete versions as a base, documented each component's props with TypeScript interfaces, and set up Storybook so designers could preview all states. I ran a short demo session per squad and wrote a contribution guide so anyone could add new components going forward.

*Result:* Two squads adopted the library within one sprint. Onboarding new developers became faster because they had one source for UI primitives rather than hunting across repos.

---

Q: Describe a disagreement you had with a designer over a UI decision.

*Situation:* A designer proposed a hover-based interaction for a data table that revealed action buttons only on hover. I felt this would break usability on touch devices and for keyboard-only users.

*Task:* I needed to raise this concern without blocking the design process or creating friction with the team.

*Action:* I prepared a short demo showing the broken experience on a touch device and a screen reader walkthrough. I came to the meeting with two alternative patterns: always-visible action icons and a context menu triggered by a 'more' button. I framed my concern around user impact, not personal preference.

*Result:* The designer appreciated the demo and chose the context-menu pattern. We shipped on time and avoided the accessibility issue entirely.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For technical questions, lead with your conclusion first, then explain your reasoning. Interviewers want to hear that you understand trade-offs, not just that you know syntax. If asked about state management, name the tool you would pick first, then explain why it fits better than the alternatives.

For performance questions, structure your answer around concrete steps: profile first, identify the bottleneck, pick a targeted fix, and measure the result. Avoid generic answers like 'I would use React.memo everywhere.' Show that you diagnose before you optimise.

For component or system design questions, ask a clarifying question before you start. Establish the data shape, expected user interactions, and any performance constraints. Then walk through your component breakdown out loud so the interviewer can follow your thinking in real time.

For behavioural questions, use a tight STAR structure. Keep Situation and Task brief (two to three sentences combined) so you can spend most of your answer on the Action. The Result should be specific: what changed, who noticed, or what was avoided.

For disagreement or conflict questions, interviewers at product-driven companies like Tradelab typically want to see that you can raise concerns with evidence and land on a decision that moves the team forward. Avoid framing yourself as always right.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Tradelab's frontend team builds interfaces where performance and data clarity matter directly to revenue. Based on what candidates typically report and what the product demands, interviewers are looking for:

Strong React and TypeScript fluency. You should be comfortable with hooks, component composition, and TypeScript generics without needing to look things up mid-interview.

Performance-first thinking. Ad tech UIs carry heavy data loads. Interviewers will probe whether you naturally think about rendering costs, memoisation, and virtualisation, or whether you only reach for them after things break.

Data visualisation awareness. Even if you have not used every charting library, you should be able to discuss trade-offs between SVG and canvas rendering, how to handle large datasets in charts, and how to make numbers readable for non-technical users.

Product ownership mindset. Tradelab's engineering culture (as candidates report) favours engineers who care about the end-user experience beyond their assigned ticket. Expect questions that probe whether you have pushed back on poor requirements or proactively improved UX without being asked.

Collaborative communication. Given the cross-functional nature of adtech products, interviewers look for engineers who can work with designers, data engineers, and product managers without creating friction.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Fundamentals and product context

Revisit React internals: reconciliation, the virtual DOM, rules of hooks, and common performance patterns (memo, useMemo, useCallback, lazy loading). Read through Tradelab's public product pages or any engineering blog posts they have published. Learn what programmatic advertising means at a basic level: how DSPs, SSPs, and real-time bidding work. You do not need to be an adtech expert, but knowing the domain helps you give contextual answers rather than generic ones.

Week 2: Coding practice and take-home readiness

Practise building data table and charting components from scratch. Focus on filtering and sorting large arrays efficiently, virtualising long lists, and writing clean TypeScript interfaces. If Tradelab sends a take-home, candidates commonly report that it involves a dashboard-style UI. Treat it like production code: lint it, test the core logic, and include a short README explaining your design decisions.

Week 3: Behavioural and design prep

Write out three to four STAR stories covering a performance win, a reusable component contribution, a cross-functional collaboration, and a time you improved something without being asked. Practise a component design walkthrough out loud: pick a complex UI (a date-range picker, a multi-series chart) and narrate your breakdown, data flow, and state decisions.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

  1. Skipping the profile step. When asked about performance, jumping straight to 'I would use React.memo' without mentioning that you would first measure the problem signals shallow understanding. Always mention profiling first.
  1. Shallow TypeScript knowledge. Many candidates say they know TypeScript but stumble on generics, conditional types, or utility types during a live question. Tradelab's codebase is likely typed end-to-end, so brush up beyond basic interface declarations.
  1. Treating the take-home as a quick exercise. Candidates who submit take-homes with no tests, no README, or components that only handle the happy path lose points they could have kept with an extra hour of polish.
  1. Vague STAR results. Saying 'the team was happy' or 'things improved' in the Result section wastes the strongest part of your answer. Tie results to something observable: a metric the team tracked, a follow-up decision made, or specific feedback received.
  1. Not asking clarifying questions in design rounds. Starting a component design answer without asking about data shape, user volume, or device targets looks like you jump to solutions. A short clarifying question shows engineering maturity.
  1. No curiosity about the product. For an adtech company, showing zero interest in how their product works reads poorly. Even one thoughtful question about the bidding UI or the data pipeline tells the interviewer you care about context, not just the coding task.
