03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through how you would respond to a data breach involving user health information.

*Situation:* At my previous company, we discovered that an internal misconfiguration had exposed a subset of user health records to an unintended internal service.

*Task:* I was the on-call security engineer and had to contain the issue, assess the impact, and coordinate communication with legal and product teams.

*Action:* I first isolated the misconfigured service to stop any further exposure. I then worked with the data team to scope exactly which records were affected and whether any external access had occurred. I drafted a brief for the engineering lead and legal team, and we coordinated user notifications in line with our privacy policy. I wrote a post-incident report with root cause analysis and recommended configuration controls to prevent recurrence.

*Result:* The exposure was contained quickly, affected users were notified in a timely manner, and we shipped a configuration audit process that caught similar issues in later releases.

---

Q: How would you threat-model a new product feature?

*Situation:* Our product team was building a feature that allowed users to share their workout data with a coach via a shareable link.

*Task:* I was asked to review the design before it went into development and flag any security concerns early.

*Action:* I ran a lightweight threat model using the STRIDE framework, covering Spoofing, Tampering, Repudiation, Information Disclosure, Denial of Service, and Elevation of Privilege. I found that the shareable link had no expiry controls, meaning a leaked link could expose data indefinitely. I also flagged that the coach's access permissions were not scoped tightly enough. I put together a short risk summary with recommended mitigations for the product and engineering teams.

*Result:* Both issues were fixed before launch. The feature shipped with time-limited links and role-based access controls, which reduced the attack surface meaningfully.

---

Q: How do you ensure data is encrypted at rest and in transit for a health app?

*Situation:* When I joined my previous team, encryption was inconsistent: some services used TLS and others did not, and database encryption was not enforced by policy.

*Task:* I was asked to audit the encryption posture across our stack and put together a remediation plan.

*Action:* I mapped all data flows and storage locations, then categorised them by sensitivity. For data in transit, I enforced TLS across all internal and external service calls and added checks to our CI pipeline to catch any new services that skipped it. For data at rest, I worked with the infrastructure team to enable encryption on all database volumes and object storage buckets. I documented the encryption standards in our internal security runbook so new engineers knew the baseline from day one.

*Result:* Within one quarter, we achieved consistent encryption coverage across the stack, which was later validated during an external security audit.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For system design security questions: Start with the data. Ask yourself what the system handles, who can access it, and what happens if it leaks. Then layer on your controls: authentication, authorisation, encryption, logging, and rate limiting. HealthifyMe handles personal health data, so showing you think about data minimisation and access scoping will stand out.

For incident response questions: Use a simple contain, assess, communicate, fix structure. Interviewers want to see that you stay calm, gather facts before acting, and loop in the right people early. Avoid framing yourself as the lone hero who solves it alone.

For compliance and standards questions: Reference the ISO 27001 standard (commonly cited as the benchmark for information security management systems) when talking about security policies and controls. For health data specifically, mention data localisation and consent requirements relevant to India's data protection landscape. Show you understand the spirit of compliance, not just the checkbox.

For behavioural questions: Use the STAR format: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep the Situation and Task brief. Spend most of your time on Action (what you specifically did, not what 'we' did) and make the Result concrete, even if you cannot share exact numbers.

For OWASP questions: Pick two or three entries from the OWASP Top Ten that are most relevant to a mobile and API-heavy product. Injection, broken authentication, and security misconfiguration are almost always relevant. Tie each one back to how a fitness app might be exposed.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Candidates who do well at HealthifyMe Security Engineer interviews typically demonstrate a few things beyond raw technical knowledge.

Product-minded security thinking. HealthifyMe is a product company, so interviewers want to see that you can balance security rigour with shipping velocity. Proposing a months-long implementation for a medium-risk API issue shows poor judgement. Show you can prioritise risks and recommend controls that fit the team's pace.

Genuine ownership of user data. The product sits in a sensitive category: health and fitness data is personal. Candidates who speak about user privacy with real conviction (not just as a compliance checkbox) tend to resonate with the team.

Clear communication under pressure. Security engineers at product companies spend a lot of time translating technical risk into language that product managers and executives can act on. Practise explaining a vulnerability in plain terms, not just in technical or CVE jargon.

Hands-on cloud experience. Candidates report that cloud security questions, particularly around misconfiguration and IAM policies, come up often. If you have AWS or GCP experience, prepare specific examples from your work.

A structured incident response mindset. You do not need to have managed a major breach. You do need to show you have a clear mental model for how to respond to one, and that you will not panic or go quiet on the team.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Foundations and research

Start by using HealthifyMe's app to understand what data it collects, how the coaching experience works, and where it connects with third parties. This context helps you tailor your answers. Revise the OWASP Top Ten with a focus on API and mobile threats. Review your cloud security fundamentals, especially IAM, storage security, and network controls on AWS or GCP.

Week 2: Practice and mock interviews

Practise framing your past work using the STAR format. Write out three or four experiences that cover: an incident you handled, a security design you contributed to, and a time you influenced a developer or team to adopt a better security practice. Run mock interviews with a peer if you can. Candidates report that HealthifyMe interviewers appreciate answers that are specific and grounded rather than generic.

Week 3: Polish and logistics

Prepare questions to ask the interviewer. Good ones include how the security team is structured, how security reviews fit into the development cycle, and what the team's biggest current challenge is. 07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Being too theoretical. Candidates who can recite definitions but cannot describe how they have applied them in practice tend to struggle. Prepare concrete examples for every major topic area.

Ignoring the health data context. HealthifyMe is not a generic SaaS product. Failing to acknowledge the sensitivity of health data in your answers signals a missed read on the role. Mention data minimisation, consent, and access controls in the context of personal health information.

Over-engineering your controls. Proposing a solution that would take months to implement for a simple API risk shows poor threat prioritisation. Match the control to the risk.

Saying 'we' instead of 'I' in behavioural questions. Interviewers want to know what you specifically did. Working on a team is fine, but make your individual contribution clear.

Not asking questions. Candidates who ask nothing at the end of a round are often seen as less engaged. Prepare two or three genuine questions about the team, the product, or the security roadmap.

Skipping the business impact. Security engineers who frame every finding purely in technical terms miss an opportunity. Practise translating a vulnerability into business risk: what data is at risk, who is affected, and what is the cost of not fixing it.
