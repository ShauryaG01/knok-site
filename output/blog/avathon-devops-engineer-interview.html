02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates who have interviewed at Avathon for DevOps roles report a mix of conceptual, scenario-based, and tool-specific questions. These are the ones that come up most often.

  1. Walk me through how you would design a CI/CD pipeline for a microservices application deployed on Kubernetes.
  2. How do you manage secrets and credentials securely across multiple environments?
  3. Explain how you have used Terraform to manage cloud infrastructure. What is your approach to remote state and module structure?
  4. How would you set up monitoring and alerting for a production API service? Which metrics would you prioritise?
  5. Describe a production incident you handled. How did you identify the root cause and what did you change afterwards?
  6. How do you handle rollbacks when a deployment fails mid-way in a Kubernetes cluster?
  7. What is your experience with GitOps tooling such as ArgoCD or Flux?
  8. How do you approach cloud cost optimisation without compromising reliability?
  9. Explain blue-green and canary deployment strategies. When would you choose one over the other?
  10. How do you manage configuration differences across dev, staging, and production without duplicating files?
  11. What steps would you take to harden a Docker image before pushing it to a production registry?
  12. How would you design a disaster recovery strategy for a stateful application?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use these as a starting point and personalise them with your own context and outcomes.

Q: Describe a time you improved a deployment process.

*Situation:* My team was releasing code manually using shell scripts stored in a shared folder. Different engineers ran slightly different versions and deployments were inconsistent, leading to frequent rollbacks.

*Task:* I was asked to design and implement an automated pipeline before the next major product launch.

*Action:* I set up a Jenkins pipeline with stages for build, automated tests, Docker image creation, and a Helm-based rollout to an AWS EKS cluster. I wrote a shared pipeline library so all services could reuse the same logic, and added Slack notifications for every deployment status change.

*Result:* Releases became consistent and reproducible. New engineers could trigger deployments without senior oversight, and the team stopped seeing 'it worked on my machine' failures at release time.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you improved security in your infrastructure.

*Situation:* The team was storing database credentials as plaintext inside config files committed directly to the Git repository. Anyone with repository access could read production passwords.

*Task:* I was responsible for migrating all secrets to a centralised solution before an upcoming security audit.

*Action:* I evaluated HashiCorp Vault and AWS Secrets Manager, and chose Vault for its dynamic secrets capability. I updated each application to fetch credentials at runtime using the Vault agent sidecar, removed all plaintext secrets from the codebase, rotated every exposed credential, and ran a knowledge-sharing session with the team on secrets hygiene practices.

*Result:* The repository was clean of hardcoded credentials by the audit date, and the team adopted Vault as the standard secrets store for all new projects going forward.

---

Q: Give an example of a production incident you resolved.

*Situation:* A critical API service started throwing intermittent errors in production late one evening. Users were experiencing failed requests, but basic uptime checks showed the service as healthy.

*Task:* As the on-call engineer, I needed to find the root cause and restore service quickly.

*Action:* I pulled logs from our ELK stack and spotted a pattern of timeout errors to a downstream database replica. I checked Prometheus metrics and found connection pool exhaustion. I temporarily increased the pool size limit to restore service, then traced the root cause to a slow query introduced in that day's release. I ran a Helm rollback to the previous version and filed a postmortem with a corrective action plan.

*Result:* Service was restored within minutes of the rollback. The incident was documented in a runbook, and we added a specific alert for connection pool saturation so future on-call engineers could catch it earlier.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For 'design a system' questions, start with requirements before mentioning any tool. State the problem you are solving and the key constraints (scale, cost, compliance, team size), then walk through your approach layer by layer. Interviewers want structured thinking, not a list of tool names.

For 'tell me about a time' questions, use STAR format: Situation (one to two sentences of context), Task (your specific responsibility), Action (what you personally did, not what the team did), Result (what changed and what you learned). Keep Situation and Task brief. Spend most of your time on Action and Result.

For tool-choice questions such as Terraform vs Pulumi or ArgoCD vs Flux, avoid saying one tool is always better. Show that you evaluate trade-offs: team familiarity, ecosystem maturity, operational overhead, and cost. Reference a real decision you made and explain the reasoning behind it.

For incident questions, interviewers at companies like Avathon typically care as much about your communication and escalation approach as about the technical fix itself. Mention how you kept stakeholders informed, how you structured the postmortem, and what you put in place to prevent recurrence.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Avathon candidates report that interviewers focus on depth and ownership more than breadth. Knowing the names of many tools matters less than being able to explain a real system you designed, built, and operated end to end.

Cloud-native fluency. Expect detailed Kubernetes questions on scheduling, CNI networking, network policies, and storage classes, not just kubectl basics. Candidates who have debugged real production failures on Kubernetes stand out clearly from those who have only used it in tutorials.

Infrastructure as code maturity. Interviewers typically probe how you structure Terraform modules, manage remote state, handle configuration drift, and version shared modules. A clear, practised answer here signals team-scale IaC experience, not just solo scripting.

Observability as a first-class concern. Avathon builds data-intensive AI platforms, so engineers are expected to treat metrics, logs, and traces as core design requirements. Be ready to discuss SLIs, SLOs, and how alerting improvements have changed your team's on-call experience.

Security built in, not bolted on. Expect at least one question per technical round on CI/CD pipeline security, container image scanning, or IAM least-privilege design. Frame security as something you plan at the start of a project, not something you add before launch.

Collaborative working style. Candidates report that interviewers often ask how you handle pushback from developers on DevOps processes or tooling requirements. Have a concrete example of making a process change developer-friendly and getting buy-in from the team.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Core technical revision
Review Kubernetes architecture in depth: control plane components, the scheduler, kubelet, CNI networking, and persistent volume claims. Revisit Terraform state management, module structure, and workspace usage. Skim the Helm documentation if you have not used it recently.

Week 2: Hands-on practice
Set up a local kind or Minikube cluster. Deploy a sample service with a Helm chart, build a basic CI pipeline on GitHub Actions or Jenkins, and expose Prometheus metrics from the service. Hands-on practice builds confidence much faster than reading documentation alone.

Week 3: Behavioral stories and system design
Write out three to five real incidents or projects from your career in STAR format. Practise saying each one aloud, aiming for two to three minutes per story. For system design, practise designing a GitOps-based deployment pipeline and a disaster recovery strategy for a stateful database.

Week 4: Company-specific research
Read Avathon's engineering blog and any publicly available case studies to understand their product and infrastructure philosophy. Map your experience to their focus areas: AI and ML workloads, data pipelines, and cloud-native infrastructure. Prepare two to three specific questions to ask your interviewer about their current technical challenges.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Naming tools without showing depth. Saying 'I have used Kubernetes and Terraform' without explaining a real design decision tells the interviewer very little. Always tie tool mentions to a specific problem you solved and a concrete outcome.

Jumping to solutions without stating requirements. Candidates who answer 'I would use EKS and ArgoCD' before discussing requirements come across as tool-first rather than problem-first. State constraints before proposing architecture.

Vague incident stories. Answers like 'we had an outage and I fixed it' do not land well. Use STAR format, be specific about your personal contribution, and explain what structural change you made to prevent the issue from recurring.

Underestimating security questions. Many candidates prepare heavily for CI/CD and IaC but get caught off guard by secrets management, image scanning, or IAM design questions. Avathon candidates typically report at least one security-focused question per technical round.

Not preparing questions to ask the interviewer. Asking nothing at the end of a round signals low interest. Prepare at least two specific questions about the team's infrastructure challenges, on-call culture, or technical roadmap.

Overselling team achievements as personal. Interviewers will probe. If you say 'we improved deployment frequency,' be ready to explain your exact individual contribution and the result.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does Avathon typically have for a DevOps Engineer role?

Candidates report typically three to four rounds: an initial HR or recruiter screening, one or two technical rounds covering hands-on scenarios and system design, and a final cultural fit conversation with a senior engineering manager. The structure varies by team, so ask your recruiter to confirm the exact format before your first round.

Is there a coding or scripting test in the Avathon DevOps interview?

Candidates typically report a practical scripting or automation task, often involving Bash, Python, or Kubernetes manifests. It is less about algorithmic coding and more about real-world tasks like writing a Dockerfile, a Helm template, or a short automation script. If scripting is not part of your daily work, practise these specifically in the weeks before your interview.

What salary can I expect for a DevOps Engineer role at Avathon?

Avathon does not publicly list salary ranges for most roles. Based on knok's job radar data for DevOps engineers across India, mid-level engineers (3-5 years) broadly see offers in the 15-28 LPA range, and senior engineers (6-9 years) in the 30-50 LPA range. For Avathon-specific reported figures, check Glassdoor and levels.fyi. Actual packages depend on your experience, the specific team, and how you negotiate.

Does Avathon hire DevOps engineers remotely or only from specific cities?

Avathon's open roles as tracked by knok are primarily listed for Bangalore, with select positions open to hybrid arrangements. Remote availability varies by team and seniority level. Confirm the work model for your specific role with the recruiter early in the process so there are no surprises after an offer is made.

How long does the Avathon hiring process take from application to offer?

Candidates typically report two to four weeks from first contact to receiving an offer, depending on team availability, scheduling, and notice period discussions. Following up with your recruiter after each round is a practical way to stay visible and get timely status updates without appearing pushy.

What tools and technologies should I focus on for the Avathon DevOps interview?

Based on candidate reports and publicly listed job descriptions, the core areas to prepare are Kubernetes, Terraform, Docker, CI/CD pipelines (Jenkins or GitHub Actions), AWS or GCP, and observability tooling such as Prometheus, Grafana, and the ELK stack. Since Avathon builds AI and data platforms, familiarity with GPU workload scheduling or data pipeline orchestration tools like Airflow is a useful differentiator.
