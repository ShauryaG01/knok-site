03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for behavioural and experience-based questions. Keep Situation and Task concise, and spend most of your time on Action and Result.

Q: Tell us about a time you fixed a security issue in a frontend application.

*Situation:* At my previous company, we built a loan application portal. During a code review, I noticed that user PAN numbers were being logged to the browser console inside a debug statement that had been left in production by mistake.

*Task:* I needed to remove the exposure immediately and verify that no similar issues existed elsewhere in the codebase.

*Action:* I raised it as a priority bug and deployed a patch the same day. I then ran a search across the entire codebase for console.log statements referencing user personal data. I also proposed an ESLint rule to automatically block console statements from reaching production builds, and wrote a short internal document on what categories of data must never be logged.

*Result:* The exposure was closed within a few hours. The ESLint rule was adopted team-wide and caught two similar issues automatically in the following months before they reached code review.

---

Q: How do you manage state in a multi-step financial onboarding form?

*Situation:* We built a multi-step KYC onboarding flow at a previous startup. Users frequently dropped off because navigating back to an earlier step wiped the data they had already entered.

*Task:* I was responsible for rebuilding the form state so users could move freely between steps without losing their inputs.

*Action:* I moved all form state into a central React context backed by useReducer, with each step's data stored under a separate key. Steps dispatched partial updates to the shared store rather than managing their own local state. I also added sessionStorage persistence so that a browser refresh mid-flow would restore the user's progress automatically.

*Result:* The product team reported a meaningful drop in onboarding funnel abandonment after the change. When we added an extra step to the flow later, the pattern made it straightforward to extend without refactoring anything else.

---

Q: Describe a performance improvement you shipped in a previous role.

*Situation:* Our transaction history page loaded a large list of rows and was causing visible lag on mid-range Android phones that our users commonly used.

*Task:* I was asked to investigate the rendering performance and ship a fix.

*Action:* I profiled the component using React DevTools Profiler and found that the entire list re-rendered on every filter state update, even when the list data itself had not changed. I applied React.memo to list item components, extracted the filter controls into a separate subtree so their state updates did not cascade to the list, and added virtual scrolling so only visible rows were mounted in the DOM at any time.

*Result:* Scroll performance improved noticeably on the test devices, and memory usage dropped as well, which mattered for users on entry-level phones. The fix also made the component easier to reason about because state was better isolated.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the standard for behavioural questions. Keep Situation and Task to one or two sentences each. Spend most of your answer on Action (specifically what you did, not what the team did) and end with a concrete, observable Result.

Problem-Solution-Impact works well for technical design questions. State the problem clearly, explain the solution you chose and why you preferred it over the obvious alternative, then describe the impact in terms the interviewer can relate to: user experience, reliability, load speed, or maintainability.

Think-aloud for system design. When asked 'how would you build X', start by clarifying requirements before jumping in. Is this for mobile or desktop? What is the expected data volume? Does it need real-time updates? Interviewers at product companies like carefi value structured thinking and product awareness, not just technical output.

Replace vague language with specifics. Instead of 'I improved performance', say 'I reduced visible lag on the transaction list by switching to virtual scrolling and memoising list items.' Even when you cannot recall exact metrics, describe what changed and how you verified the improvement.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on carefi's fintech context and candidate reports, interviewers typically look for the following qualities.

Strong React and TypeScript fundamentals. Expect questions on hooks, component lifecycle, memoisation, and type safety. Candidates who know only the surface of React without understanding when not to use certain patterns often do not progress past the technical round.

Security-first thinking. Fintech products handle money and personal data. Candidates who proactively mention XSS prevention, safe token storage, and input sanitisation stand out from those who address security only when directly asked.

Performance awareness. carefi likely serves users across a range of device types and connection speeds. Showing that you think about bundle size, lazy loading, virtualisation, and API waterfall patterns without being prompted signals seniority.

Fintech domain fluency. You do not need a finance background, but knowing terms like EMI, KYC, PAN, Aadhaar-based verification, and basic regulatory context in India signals that you can contribute to product discussions from day one.

A testing mindset. Engineers who have a clear opinion on what to test, what not to test, and why are valued over those who treat tests as an afterthought.

Clear communication. carefi is a startup, which means cross-functional work. Interviewers note how clearly you explain trade-offs, not just whether you arrive at the 'right' answer.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Core React and TypeScript depth

Revisit hooks (useState, useEffect, useContext, useReducer, useMemo, useCallback) with focus on when not to use each one. Build a small project using TypeScript generics with API responses. Practice explaining your code choices out loud as if you were in a live interview.

Week 2: Fintech-specific UI patterns

Build a mock EMI calculator, a multi-step form with persistent state across steps, and a transaction list with filters and virtual scrolling. These map directly to the kind of UI carefi builds. Study how to format Indian currency values using the JavaScript Intl.NumberFormat API for rupees, lakhs, and crores.

Week 3: Security, performance, and testing

Read the OWASP Top 10 for web applications, focusing on XSS, insecure storage, and CSRF. Profile a React application using Chrome DevTools and React DevTools Profiler to understand where rendering time is spent. Write tests for your mock projects using React Testing Library, covering both happy paths and error states.

Week 4: Interview practice and company research

Practice mock interviews using the questions in this guide, focusing on structured STAR answers. Read carefi's public content (blog posts, LinkedIn updates, product announcements) to understand their product direction. Prepare thoughtful questions to ask your interviewer about the engineering team's current challenges and tech decisions.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating React as the entire interview. carefi will test React depth, but candidates who cannot speak to security, testing, or performance often do not clear later rounds even with strong coding skills.

Vague STAR answers. Saying 'I improved the app's performance' without specifying what you changed, how you measured it, and what the outcome was is a red flag. Prepare specific stories from your past work before the interview, not during it.

Ignoring the fintech context. Candidates who arrive without basic familiarity with terms like KYC, EMI, or PAN look underprepared for a fintech role specifically. A quick read of carefi's product pages and public content will fix this before your first round.

Asking no questions at the end. Candidates who have nothing to ask are often seen as unengaged. Prepare genuine questions about the engineering team, the product roadmap, or the technical challenges the team is currently working on.

Overcomplicating system design answers. For a frontend role, interviewers want clean component thinking and clear trade-off reasoning, not a full distributed systems architecture. Match the complexity of your answer to the scope of the problem.

Skipping error and loading states. In financial UIs, a blank screen on API failure is not an acceptable user experience. Always mention how your design handles loading, errors, and empty data when walking through any UI design question.
