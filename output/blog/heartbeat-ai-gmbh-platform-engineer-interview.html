02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

The following questions come up often in Platform Engineer interviews at AI companies like Heartbeat AI. Candidates report these themes based on publicly shared experiences for similar roles.

  1. Walk us through how you have set up a Kubernetes cluster from scratch. What choices did you make on node configuration, networking, and storage?
  1. How do you design a CI/CD pipeline for a machine learning project where model artifacts, data versioning, and code all need to be tracked together?
  1. Describe your experience with Infrastructure as Code. Which tools have you used, and how did you handle state management and drift detection?
  1. How would you design a platform to serve low-latency AI model inference at scale? What components would you choose and why?

  1. How do you balance developer experience (fast deploys, easy debugging) with platform stability and security requirements?
  1. Describe how you would set up cost monitoring and optimisation for a cloud environment where GPU compute is a significant expense.
  1. What is your experience with container security scanning, policy enforcement, or admission controllers in Kubernetes?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through how you set up a Kubernetes cluster from scratch.

*Situation:* At my previous company, we were running containerised services on bare cloud instances managed manually. Deployments were inconsistent and scaling required human intervention each time.

*Task:* I was asked to migrate everything to a managed Kubernetes setup and own the full cluster design, from node groups to networking to observability.

*Action:* I chose a managed Kubernetes service and used Terraform to provision all resources. I set up separate node groups for general workloads and GPU workloads, configured a node autoscaler for bursty traffic, and wrote Helm charts for all internal services. I introduced a GitOps tool for continuous delivery and integrated a monitoring stack from day one. I also ran knowledge transfer sessions so the rest of the team could operate the cluster without depending on me.

*Result:* Deployment frequency increased significantly and on-call incidents related to infrastructure dropped over the three months following the migration. The team could self-serve standard deployments without escalating to the platform team.

---

Q: Tell us about a time your platform had an outage.

*Situation:* We had a production outage affecting our API serving layer. A config change rolled out via the CD pipeline caused pods to crash-loop, and the liveness probe was not catching the issue fast enough to trigger an automatic rollback.

*Task:* I was the on-call engineer that night and needed to restore service quickly while also identifying the root cause.

*Action:* I immediately rolled back the deployment using the GitOps tool's UI, which restored service within a few minutes. The next day I ran a blameless post-mortem with the team. We found that the config change had no staging promotion gate and that the liveness probe timeout was too aggressive for our service startup time. I added a mandatory staging gate to the pipeline and tuned the probe settings. I also wrote a runbook for this failure mode so future on-call engineers could resolve it faster.

*Result:* Service was restored within minutes of the first alert. The same failure mode has not recurred since we added the staging gate, and the runbook has since been used by other engineers for related issues.

---

Q: How do you balance developer experience with platform stability?

*Situation:* Our platform team was under pressure to speed up build times and simplify deployments, but a compliance review had introduced strict security and audit requirements that we could not work around.

*Task:* I needed to improve developer feedback loops without weakening the compliance posture.

*Action:* I introduced ephemeral preview environments that spun up per pull request using lightweight namespaces on our existing cluster, with all security policies enforced via admission controllers. I added layer-caching to our Docker builds to cut build times. For secrets management, I migrated to a vault-based setup with short-lived dynamic credentials so developers did not need to manage long-lived keys.

*Result:* Developer satisfaction improved in our quarterly internal survey. We passed our next compliance audit with no findings related to secrets management. Platform-related escalations to our team dropped noticeably over the following quarter.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For behavioral questions, use the STAR structure throughout. Keep the Situation and Task brief (two or three sentences each) and spend most of your time on Action and Result. The more specific your actions, the more credible your story. Interviewers at product companies are listening for judgment calls, not just task completion.

For system design questions, open by clarifying requirements: expected scale, latency constraints, consistency needs, and any compliance constraints like GDPR. Sketch the high-level architecture first, then go deeper on individual components. Close by discussing trade-offs and what you would revisit with more time or budget. This shows you think in layers, not just components.

For 'how would you' infrastructure questions, structure your answer across four layers: the infrastructure layer (cloud, networking, compute), the platform layer (orchestration, secrets, CI/CD), the developer experience layer (APIs, tooling, self-service interfaces), and the observability layer (metrics, logs, traces). Covering all four layers signals a holistic platform mindset rather than a narrow operational focus.

For tool-choice questions, never just list names. Explain which problem each tool solves and why you chose it over the obvious alternative. Saying 'I used Karpenter because we needed faster scale-up than the Cluster Autoscaler could provide for bursty GPU workloads' is far more convincing than 'I used Karpenter for autoscaling.'

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Heartbeat AI is building AI products, which means Platform Engineers are not just maintaining infrastructure. They are enabling model training, serving, and experimentation at scale. Here is what interviewers are typically looking for in this kind of role.

Ownership mindset. Can you take a platform problem end-to-end, from initial design to runbook, without someone holding your hand? Give examples where you drove a platform initiative, not just implemented a ticket handed to you.

Reliability thinking. Do you design for failure from the start? Show that you think in terms of failure modes, rollback strategies, and post-mortem culture rather than focusing only on keeping things running.

ML infrastructure awareness. You do not need to be a data scientist, but knowing how training jobs, model registries, feature stores, and inference servers work will set you apart from candidates who treat this as a generic cloud role. Show you understand what your ML engineering colleagues actually need from the platform.

GDPR-aware design. Since Heartbeat AI is a German company serving European users, data residency, deletion pipelines, and audit logging are legal requirements, not optional features. Mentioning these proactively in system design answers will leave a strong impression.

Clear communication. Platform teams are internal service providers. Interviewers want to see that you can document clearly, say no to unreasonable requests with a reasoned explanation, and give developers the context they need without over-engineering every interaction.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Foundations review. Revisit Kubernetes internals (scheduling, networking, RBAC, admission controllers), Terraform state management and module design, and the advanced features of your preferred CI/CD tool. Practice explaining each concept out loud as if teaching a junior colleague. This quickly surfaces gaps in your understanding.

Week 2: ML infrastructure focus. Read up on how tools like MLflow, Kubeflow, or Ray are typically deployed on Kubernetes. Understand what a feature store does and why ML teams need model versioning separate from code versioning. You do not need hands-on experience with all of them, but knowing the concepts helps you answer 'how would you build a platform for AI workloads' questions with real credibility.

Week 3: System design practice. Do two or three timed system design sessions on topics like 'design a multi-tenant ML inference platform' or 'design a CI/CD system for a team running GPU training jobs'. Focus on articulating trade-offs clearly rather than producing a perfect architecture diagram.

Week 4: Behavioral prep and company research. Map your past projects to common interview themes: outage response, platform migration, developer tooling, cost optimisation. Read Heartbeat AI's public content (blog posts, LinkedIn updates, job descriptions for adjacent roles) to understand their product and engineering values. Prepare four or five stories in STAR format covering different competencies.

Throughout: Brush up on GDPR basics as they relate to infrastructure, particularly data residency, encryption at rest, deletion pipelines, and audit logging. These are likely to come up given Heartbeat AI's European market focus.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Listing tools without context. Saying 'I know Terraform, Helm, ArgoCD, Prometheus' without explaining what problems you solved with each will not impress an experienced interviewer. Always anchor tool mentions to a specific decision, trade-off, or outcome.

Skipping the 'why'. Platform questions are largely about judgment. If you say 'I used Karpenter for autoscaling', follow up immediately with why you chose it over the alternative and what limitations you encountered. The reasoning matters more than the tool choice itself.

Ignoring the ML context. This is an AI company. Treating the role as generic DevOps or SRE work, without showing awareness of ML-specific requirements like GPU node management, experiment tracking, or model versioning, can signal a poor fit for the team.

Vague outage stories. Some candidates give outage answers that are all action and no reflection. Interviewers want to see that you ran a post-mortem, changed something specific, and can explain the second-order effects of that change.

Underestimating GDPR. Candidates who wave off compliance with 'we encrypt everything' without showing depth on data residency, deletion pipelines, or audit trails leave interviewers unconvinced. GDPR is a legal requirement in the European market, not a checkbox.

Talking only about infrastructure, not developer experience. Platform Engineers serve internal developers. If all your answers focus on cluster configuration and none address the interface you built for your users (self-service tooling, documentation, escalation paths), you may come across as siloed from the people your platform is meant to help.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Heartbeat AI GmbH Platform Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report a process that typically includes an initial recruiter or HR call, a technical screen with a platform or infrastructure engineer, a practical assessment (take-home or live coding), and a final round with hiring managers or cross-functional stakeholders. Some steps may be combined depending on the team's schedule. The full process typically spans a few weeks from first contact to offer.

Is the interview conducted in English or German?

Candidates report that Heartbeat AI interviews are conducted in English, which is standard practice for German tech companies hiring internationally. Day-to-day engineering work and technical documentation are also typically in English at companies of this profile. You should not need German language skills for the technical rounds.

Which cloud platforms should I focus on preparing for?

Most AI companies in Europe use AWS, GCP, or a combination of both, and this is what candidates typically prepare for. Kubernetes knowledge is platform-agnostic and highly valued regardless of the specific cloud in use. Preparing system design answers that work across both AWS and GCP, while noting where the two differ on managed services, will serve you well across most scenarios.

Does Heartbeat AI expect ML engineering experience for a Platform Engineer role?

You are not expected to build or train models, but familiarity with how models are trained, versioned, and served is a genuine advantage. Understanding tools like MLflow, Kubeflow, or Ray Serve at a conceptual level shows you can design a platform that meets ML engineering teams' actual needs. This matters more at an AI company than at a general SaaS company, so invest time here before the interview.

How should I handle a system design question about a technology I have not used directly?

Be upfront about your direct experience, then reason through the problem using first principles. Structured thinking and the ability to articulate trade-offs matter more to interviewers than familiarity with any single tool. Saying 'I have not used X, but here is how I would approach it' is far better than bluffing, and experienced interviewers will respect the honesty.

Are there location or visa requirements I should clarify before accepting an interview?

Location requirements vary by role and team. Some positions may be remote-friendly while others could require being based in Germany or another European location. Check the specific job description first, and if the requirement is unclear, ask the recruiter on the first call. Clarifying this early avoids surprises later in the process when an offer is already on the table.
