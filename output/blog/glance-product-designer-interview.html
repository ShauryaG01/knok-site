01 Overview

Overview

Glance is a lock-screen content platform built by InMobi Group, reaching hundreds of millions of Android users across India and Southeast Asia. The product delivers personalised content cards, live scores, short videos, and shopping moments directly on the phone lock screen, before the user even unlocks the device. Every design decision is seen in a passive, low-intent state, which makes visual clarity, motion design, and near-zero friction central to the craft here.

The design team works at consumer scale that is rare among Indian product companies. Candidates report that the interview process typically runs across a few rounds: a portfolio review, a design exercise or case study, a cross-functional discussion with PMs or engineers, and a culture or values conversation. Rounds are not always labelled formally, so treat every conversation as carrying equal weight.

As of July 2026, knok jobradar tracked 393 open Product Designer roles across India, with Glance alone accounting for 39 of them, making it one of the more active design hirers in the market right now.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up frequently in Glance Product Designer interviews, based on patterns candidates report publicly. Expect a mix of portfolio walkthroughs, behavioural scenarios, and live or take-home design problems.

  1. Walk me through a project where you designed for passive or low-intent browsing behaviour, not active search.
  2. How would you design a content card for the Glance lock screen that works equally well for a tier-1 city user and a first-time smartphone owner on a budget Android device?
  3. Glance surfaces content before the user has searched for anything. How do you design for discovery without causing distraction or annoyance?
  4. Tell me about a time you had to cut your design significantly because of a technical or business constraint. What did you keep and what did you drop?
  5. How do you approach motion and micro-interaction design, and what evidence tells you it is helping retention rather than hurting it?
  6. Describe a situation where your design hypothesis was proven wrong by user research or live data. What did you do next?
  7. How would you improve the Glance content feed to increase dwell time without raising cognitive load?
  8. Walk me through how you collaborate with a machine learning or data science team when personalisation is core to the product.
  9. Tell me about a design system component you built or significantly improved. What was your process and what changed after it shipped?
  10. How do you handle a disagreement with a product manager or engineer about a design direction, especially under deadline pressure?
  11. What metrics would you track to know whether a new content format on Glance is actually working?
  12. How do you design for users who may not read English fluently and rely more on visuals, icons, and colour to navigate?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for behavioural questions. The three examples below are illustrative structures you can adapt to your own experience.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you had to cut your design significantly because of technical constraints.

*Situation:* I was designing an onboarding flow for a content app targeting first-time smartphone users in tier-2 cities. My original design had an animated walkthrough with six screens and a short preference quiz.

*Task:* Engineering flagged that the animation assets would push the APK size past the carrier partner's limit, and the quiz added noticeable load time on low-end devices.

*Action:* I audited every screen with one question: 'What single decision does this screen help the user make?' I collapsed the six screens into two, replaced animations with static illustrations, and moved the preference quiz to post-onboarding so users could skip it. I also worked with the data team to replace quiz-based personalisation with implicit signals from the first few minutes of use.

*Result:* APK size stayed within the limit, load time dropped measurably, and day-three retention improved compared to the earlier version because users reached content faster.

---

Q: Describe a situation where your design hypothesis was proven wrong by user research.

*Situation:* I designed a 'save for later' feature for a news card, assuming users would want a reading list to return to.

*Task:* We ran a usability study with a small group of participants before launch.

*Action:* Most participants either could not find the save button or did not understand what 'save' meant in context. Watching the session recordings, I saw that users expected to share content rather than save it. I redesigned the primary action as a share button and moved save to a secondary long-press menu.

*Result:* In a follow-up test with the same group, task completion for the primary action improved substantially. The feature shipped with the share-first layout and no rollback was needed.

---

Q: Walk me through how you work with a machine learning team when personalisation is part of the product.

*Situation:* At a previous role, the ML team had built a recommendation model for a content feed and design was brought in only after the model logic was mostly finalised.

*Task:* My job was to design the UI, but some model outputs would have been confusing to users without explanation.

*Action:* I requested a working session with the ML lead to understand what signals drove each recommendation. I then designed lightweight reason labels on cards (for example, 'Because you read cricket news') so users understood why content appeared. I also flagged that the model had no fallback for new users with no history, and worked with the team to design a cold-start flow that collected just two preference signals up front.

*Result:* The reason labels were retained in the shipped product. Qualitative testing showed users felt more in control of their feed, and the cold-start flow reduced the time before the model had enough data to personalise.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For behavioural questions, use STAR: Situation (one or two sentences of context), Task (your specific responsibility), Action (what you personally did, not the team), Result (a concrete outcome, honestly hedged if the exact number is not yours to share).

For design problem questions, candidates find it useful to follow a four-step structure: (1) clarify who the user is and what the constraint is before touching any design tool; (2) state your assumptions out loud so the interviewer can correct them; (3) explore two or three directions briefly before committing to one; (4) explain your trade-offs, not just your final choice. Glance interviewers typically want to see your thinking process, not a polished mock-up produced in silence.

For metrics questions, anchor your answer to a specific user behaviour: time spent, return rate, tap-through rate, or share rate. Avoid saying 'improve engagement' without defining what changes and how you would measure it.

For conflict or collaboration questions, show that you can disagree constructively. Name the data or design principle you used to make your case, and acknowledge when the other person's constraint was legitimate.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Consumer empathy at scale. Glance's users span a wide range of devices, literacy levels, and data conditions. Interviewers want evidence that you have designed for real constraints, not just for high-end phones and fast Wi-Fi.

Visual and motion craft. The lock screen is a visual medium. Candidates who can speak fluently about typography, colour contrast, motion timing, and accessibility consistently stand out.

Data orientation. Glance is heavily instrumented. Interviewers look for designers who can define a metric for their own design decisions and read data without needing someone to translate it for them.

Systems thinking. With 39 open roles as of July 2026, Glance is building fast. They value designers who think in components and patterns, not one-off screens.

Collaboration with ML and engineering. Personalisation is core to the product. Candidates who have worked alongside model or data teams are seen as lower onboarding risk and tend to move faster through the process.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know the product. Use Glance on an Android device for at least a week before your interview. Note what works, what feels off, and one change you would make and why. This single exercise generates material for multiple interview questions.

Week 1 to 2: Audit your portfolio. Select two or three projects that show constraint-driven design: situations where you cut scope, responded to live data, or designed for a user with limited resources or literacy. Prepare a five-minute walkthrough for each, focused on decisions rather than visuals.

Week 2: Write out your STAR stories. Prepare answers for conflict, failure, and collaboration questions in writing before the interview. Rehearse them out loud so they sound natural, not scripted.

Week 2: Practice a design exercise. Candidates report that Glance sometimes gives a take-home or live whiteboarding task. Practice designing a content card or a notification surface from a brief, focusing on explaining trade-offs, not just the output.

Throughout: Research Glance's public direction. Look at their recent announcements, new content categories, and market expansions so you can frame your design decisions in terms of what the business is actually working on.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Talking about aesthetics without talking about behaviour. Saying 'I made it cleaner' is not enough. Explain what user behaviour changed or what constraint the design solved.

Ignoring the lock-screen context. Generic feed or app design thinking does not transfer directly. Failing to acknowledge that Glance operates in a passive, pre-unlock state is a signal that you have not actually used the product.

Over-claiming metrics. If you do not have the exact number, say 'our internal data showed an improvement' rather than inventing a figure. Glance teams are data-literate and will follow up with specific questions.

Portfolio with only final screens. Showing polished output without process, dead ends, or constraints makes it hard for interviewers to evaluate how you think. Include at least one slide of early explorations or a decision you reversed.

Not asking questions. Treating the interview as one-directional misses the chance to show strategic thinking. Asking about the team's biggest current design challenge signals genuine interest and helps you assess fit.

How many rounds does the Glance Product Designer interview typically have?

Candidates report that the process typically runs across a few rounds, commonly including a portfolio review, a design exercise (take-home or live), a cross-functional discussion with a PM or engineer, and a culture or values conversation. The exact number varies by team and seniority level. Glance does not always label rounds formally, so treat every conversation as carrying equal weight.

Is there a design exercise or assignment in the Glance interview process?

Candidates commonly report a design task as part of the process, either as a take-home brief or a live whiteboarding session. Typical prompts involve designing a content surface, a card format, or a new feature for the lock-screen context. Focus your response on explaining trade-offs and constraints rather than on producing a polished visual in the available time.

What kind of portfolio projects should I highlight for a Glance interview?

Prioritise projects that show constraint-driven design: times you simplified a flow for a low-literacy or low-bandwidth user, responded to live data, worked with ML or data teams, or contributed to a design system. Glance works at consumer scale with diverse users, so projects demonstrating empathy for non-ideal conditions tend to resonate more than polished enterprise or desktop web work.

How should I handle metrics questions if I cannot share exact numbers from a previous role?

Be honest and specific about what you can share. Saying 'our internal data showed an improvement in return rate' or 'a small usability study showed task completion improved' is credible and professional. Avoid inventing specific percentages or absolute figures. Interviewers at a data-driven company like Glance will ask follow-up questions, and an honest hedge holds up far better than a number that falls apart under scrutiny.

Does Glance hire Product Designers outside Bangalore?

Glance's engineering and product teams are primarily based in Bangalore, and knok jobradar data from July 2026 shows the majority of their 39 open design roles concentrated there. Some hybrid or remote arrangements exist, but candidates report that most collaborative senior roles expect regular Bangalore presence.
