02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates at Team8 report questions that test both core QA fundamentals and security awareness. Here are the most common ones:

  1. Walk me through how you would design a test plan for a new, security-focused feature.
  2. How do you decide which bugs to escalate immediately and which to log for the next sprint?
  3. Describe your experience with API testing. What tools have you used and what did you find most challenging?
  4. How do you handle a situation where a developer disagrees with a bug you have filed?
  5. Explain the difference between functional testing and security testing, and how they overlap in your day-to-day work.
  6. Have you worked with CI/CD pipelines? How did you integrate automated tests into the build process?
  7. How would you approach testing a product that has no existing documentation or specifications?
  8. Describe a time you caught a critical bug close to a release deadline. What did you do?
  9. What does your regression testing strategy look like for a product that ships frequently?
  10. How do you stay current with common vulnerabilities, such as those in the OWASP Top 10, that could affect the products you test?
  11. Walk me through how you write automation scripts. What language or framework do you prefer and why?
  12. How do you measure whether your QA process is actually improving product quality?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format for behavioral questions. Here are three examples tailored to the Team8 context.

---

Q: Describe a time you caught a critical bug close to a release deadline.

*Situation:* At my previous company, we were two days from releasing a major update to our fintech application.

*Task:* I was responsible for final regression testing across all payment flows.

*Action:* During exploratory testing of the transaction history screen, I noticed decimal values were being rounded incorrectly when currency conversion was applied. I reproduced it across multiple devices, documented it with screenshots and precise reproduction steps, and flagged it directly to the tech lead along with a suggested fix path to speed things up.

*Result:* The team patched it within a few hours, I ran a focused retest, and the release shipped on time. That bug, if it had reached production, would have shown incorrect amounts to users.

---

Q: How do you handle a situation where a developer pushes back on a bug you filed?

*Situation:* A developer at my previous company marked one of my bug reports as 'by design' without any prior discussion.

*Task:* I needed to either accept that call or make a stronger case based on evidence.

*Action:* I pulled up the original user story and the requirement it was tied to, highlighted the gap between expected and actual behavior, and proposed a short sync with the developer and the product owner together. I kept the conversation focused on user impact rather than who was right.

*Result:* The product owner confirmed it was a genuine defect. The fix went into the same sprint, and we added a checklist item to our definition of done to prevent similar gaps in future releases.

---

Q: How would you test a product that has no existing documentation?

*Situation:* I joined a startup where the core product had been built over two years with almost no written specifications.

*Task:* I was asked to build a test suite before the next client demo, starting from scratch.

*Action:* I spent a day with the product manager and two developers, asking 'what does this feature do and what would break it?' I mapped workflows, edge cases, and fragile areas into a shared document. Then I used session-based testing to cover the highest-risk paths first, logging findings as I went.

*Result:* Within two weeks I had documented test cases for all critical flows, found several bugs including three blockers, and the client demo ran without any live issues.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For 'design a test plan' questions: Cover scope (what is in and what is out), types of testing needed (functional, integration, performance, security), entry and exit criteria, the tools you would use, and how you manage risk. Team8's security focus means you should mention at least one security testing angle even when the question is primarily functional.

For 'how do you prioritize bugs' questions: Think in terms of severity (how badly does it break the product) combined with the likelihood of a real user hitting it. Critical-path bugs and security flaws go to the top regardless of sprint timing. Always explain your priority reasoning to the team rather than just assigning a label in the tracker.

For 'testing without documentation' questions: Describe a session-based approach: define a charter for each testing session, timebox your work, and log findings continuously. Stakeholder interviews are your substitute for written specs. Show that you can structure your own investigation from nothing.

For automation questions: Lead with your actual stack (Selenium, Cypress, Playwright, pytest, etc.), explain how you decide what to automate first (high-frequency, high-risk, stable flows), and finish with how you maintain tests when the product changes. Team8 values engineers who treat test code with the same rigour as production code.

For 'measuring QA effectiveness' questions: Mention defect leakage rate (bugs found in production vs. caught during testing), test coverage for critical flows, and customer-reported issues as the metrics that matter most. Avoid vanity metrics like total test case count.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Security-first mindset. Team8 builds products for high-stakes environments. Interviewers want to see that you naturally ask 'how could this be misused?' not just 'does this button work?' You do not need to be a security specialist, but connecting functional testing to security implications should feel natural to you.

Precision in communication. Good bug reports and clear test plans matter more at Team8 than at a typical product company. Interviewers probe how you write up findings and how you explain risk to non-technical stakeholders. Clarity counts as much as technical knowledge.

Ownership over process. Many Team8 portfolio companies are scaling fast, which means QA engineers are expected to build and improve processes, not just follow them. Candidates who say 'I set up the test framework from scratch' or 'I introduced the regression suite' stand out over those who only maintained existing work.

Collaboration under pressure. Fast release cycles and enterprise clients mean you will need to make quality calls quickly. Interviewers look for people who stay calm, prioritize clearly, and bring developers along rather than creating friction.

Technical depth in automation. Familiarity with tools like Postman, Selenium, Playwright, JMeter, or pytest is commonly expected. Be ready to discuss real tests you have written, not just tool names you have used.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Sharpen your fundamentals. Revisit SDLC and STLC end-to-end, write a sample test plan for a product you use every day, and read through the OWASP Top 10 at a surface level so you can speak to common vulnerability categories in the interview.

Week 2: Practise your behavioral answers. Write down three to five real situations from your career that show ownership, handling disagreement, and catching problems early. Map each one to the STAR format and practise saying them out loud. Natural delivery matters more than a polished script.

Week 3: Refresh your automation stack. Pick one or two tools you know best and be ready to walk through a real test you wrote, a problem you hit, and how you solved it. If you have a GitHub profile, review it before interviews so you can reference actual code confidently.

Week 4: Research Team8 specifically. Read about their portfolio companies and the kinds of products they build. Candidates report that tailoring your examples to cybersecurity or enterprise software scenarios lands noticeably well with interviewers.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating security testing as optional. At Team8, security is core to the product. Candidates who give purely functional answers without any security angle often do not advance past the technical round.

Filing vague bug reports. Interviewers sometimes ask you to walk through a real bug report you have written. Vague titles like 'button not working' signal weak communication. Strong reports include steps to reproduce, expected vs. actual behavior, environment details, and severity reasoning.

Listing tools without depth. Adding Selenium or Cypress to your resume and then struggling to discuss a real test you wrote with them is a common and costly trap. Know your tools deeply, not just by name.

Ignoring business impact. QA at Team8 is not just about catching bugs, it is about protecting enterprise clients. Candidates who frame their work only in technical terms and never mention user impact or business risk miss a key signal interviewers are looking for.

Over-rehearsing behavioral answers. Scripted answers that sound memorized come across poorly in conversational interviews. Use STAR as a structure, not a script. Keep it natural and conversational.

Not asking good questions. Candidates who ask nothing signal low curiosity. Ask about the team's current testing challenges, how QA fits into the release process, or what the biggest quality risks are in the current product stack.

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Team8 QA Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report that the process typically involves three to four rounds: a recruiter screening call, one or two technical rounds covering testing concepts and automation, and a behavioral discussion with a hiring manager. The exact format can vary across Team8 portfolio companies, so confirm the structure with your recruiter at the start of the process.

Is coding or automation knowledge required for this role?

Automation skills are commonly expected, particularly in Python or JavaScript. You do not need to write full application code, but you should be comfortable writing and reviewing test scripts. Candidates report that being able to walk through real automation code you have written is important in the technical rounds, so prepare specific examples in advance.

What salary can I expect as a QA Engineer at Team8?

Based on publicly reported ranges for QA Engineers in India, entry-level candidates (0-2 years) typically see offers in the 4-9 LPA range, mid-level (3-5 years) in the 9-17 LPA range, and senior engineers (6-9 years) in the 17-30 LPA range. Lead roles commonly reach 28-45+ LPA. Actual offers at Team8 will vary by portfolio company, your specific experience, and how well you negotiate.

Does Team8 ask security-specific questions in QA interviews?

Candidates report that security awareness comes up regularly, even in functional QA rounds. You are not expected to be a penetration tester, but familiarity with common vulnerability categories (such as OWASP Top 10) and the ability to describe how you would test authentication flows or data exposure scenarios is a clear advantage. Team8 builds products for security-conscious enterprise clients, so this mindset is taken seriously.

How should I prepare for the 'test a product with no documentation' question?

Interviewers want to see that you can structure your own work without hand-holding. Walk through how you would gather context from stakeholders, define test charters for each session, prioritize the highest-risk areas first, and document findings as you go. If you have a real example from your career, use it. Concrete experience always lands better than a theoretical answer.
