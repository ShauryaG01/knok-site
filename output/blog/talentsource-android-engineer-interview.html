02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly based on what candidates report from Talentsource and similar enterprise Android hiring processes. Prepare a concrete, example-based answer for each one.

  1. Walk me through an Android app you built end to end, from architecture decisions to Play Store release.
  2. How do you detect and fix memory leaks in Android? Share a specific example from your work.
  3. Explain the difference between ViewModel, LiveData, and StateFlow. When would you pick one over another?
  4. How do you manage background tasks today? Compare WorkManager, Foreground Services, and coroutines for different use cases.
  5. Describe a time you improved app performance. What metrics did you track and what tools did you use?
  6. How do you approach testing in Android? Explain your test pyramid and how you balance unit, integration, and UI tests.
  7. Explain Jetpack Compose's recomposition model. How do you avoid unnecessary recompositions in a production app?
  8. How do you handle navigation and deep linking in a large multi-module app?
  9. Compare Hilt with other dependency injection approaches you have used. Why would you choose Hilt?
  10. How do you ensure your app works well across different screen sizes, densities, and Android API versions?
  11. Tell me about a technical disagreement you had with a colleague. How did you handle it and what was the outcome?
  12. Enterprise clients expect high reliability. How do you build Android features that meet strict uptime and performance requirements?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time you improved app performance.

*Situation:* Our Android app had a known issue where the home screen took several seconds to load on mid-range devices, and user reviews were mentioning it directly.

*Task:* I was asked to investigate and bring the load time down before the next release cycle.

*Action:* I used Android Studio's Profiler to trace CPU, memory, and rendering. I found two problems: a synchronous network call on the main thread during startup, and an inefficient RecyclerView adapter that was rebinding every item on scroll. I moved the network call to a coroutine with a loading state, and rewrote the adapter to use DiffUtil for incremental updates.

*Result:* Load time dropped significantly, ANRs on that screen went to zero, and the team adopted DiffUtil as a standard pattern going forward.

---

Q: Tell me about a technical disagreement you had with a teammate.

*Situation:* A senior developer on my team wanted to bring in a third-party library for handling push notifications, while I felt the Firebase SDK covered our needs without adding another dependency.

*Task:* We needed to agree on a direction before sprint planning to avoid blockers.

*Action:* I wrote a short comparison doc listing bundle size impact, maintenance risk, and feature overlap. I shared it in Slack before our meeting so everyone could read it without feeling put on the spot. In the meeting, I acknowledged the strengths of his suggestion and focused the discussion on our specific requirements.

*Result:* We agreed to go with the Firebase SDK. The teammate appreciated the structured comparison, and we now use that format whenever the team evaluates new libraries.

---

Q: Walk me through an Android app you built end to end.

*Situation:* My previous company needed an internal field-service app for technicians who worked in areas with poor connectivity.

*Task:* I was the sole Android developer responsible for design, build, and deployment to an internal Play Store track.

*Action:* I chose MVVM with a Room database as the offline-first layer, syncing to a REST API when connectivity returned. I used WorkManager for background sync so it survived process death and battery optimization. I set up CI with GitHub Actions to run lint and unit tests on every pull request.

*Result:* The app launched on time, handled offline use reliably across the field team, and had a very low post-launch crash rate. It became the reference architecture for two subsequent internal apps at the company.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For technical questions, structure your answer in three parts: what the concept is, why it matters in production, and a real example from your own code. Interviewers at enterprise companies want to see applied knowledge, not textbook definitions.

For behavioral questions, use the STAR format: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task brief (two to three sentences), spend most time on the Action (what you specifically did, not what the team did), and close with a concrete Result. Avoid vague outcomes like 'it went well.'

For system design questions, start by clarifying requirements and constraints before proposing any architecture. Think out loud. Cover offline handling, failure recovery, and testability, since these matter heavily in enterprise Android work. Interviewers are evaluating your reasoning process, not just your final diagram.

For 'why Talentsource' questions, tie your answer to the company's enterprise focus and the kind of scale they work at. Generic answers about 'learning and growth' do not land well. Show that you understand their product and can connect your specific experience to their problems.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Depth over breadth. Talentsource interviewers typically probe until they find the edge of your knowledge. It is better to say 'I know this deeply' and demonstrate it than to claim familiarity with fifteen frameworks you have only skimmed.

A production mindset. Enterprise clients care about reliability. Expect questions about how you handle failures, monitor apps after launch, and communicate with non-technical stakeholders when something breaks. Showing that you think beyond 'it works on my machine' signals maturity.

Clean, testable code. Candidates report that reviewers look for separation of concerns, SOLID principles, and meaningful test coverage. Code that works but is untestable does not pass the bar at this level.

Clear communication. Android Engineers at Talentsource work closely with product managers and backend teams. Interviewers notice how clearly you explain trade-offs, not just whether you pick the right answer. Thinking out loud and summarising your reasoning helps.

Ownership. Stories where you drove a feature from idea to production, handled edge cases proactively, and followed up post-launch get strong reactions. Avoid framing yourself only as someone who executed tasks assigned by others.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Core Android fundamentals
Revisit Activity and Fragment lifecycle, ViewModel and LiveData, coroutines and Flow, and Jetpack Compose basics. Do not just re-read documentation. Write small code samples from memory, run them, and fix what breaks.

Week 2: Architecture and system design
Practice designing an offline-first app, a real-time messaging screen, and a modular feature library. Explain your choices out loud as if to an interviewer. Focus on how you would handle failure cases and recovery, since enterprise clients demand it.

Week 3: Behavioral preparation
Write down five to six STAR stories from your work history covering: a performance improvement, a disagreement resolved well, a project you owned end to end, a time you learned from a mistake, and a time you influenced a technical decision. Practice telling each story in under two minutes.

Week 4: Mock interviews and gap review
Do at least two mock technical interviews with a friend or on a practice platform. Review any weak areas. Read recent posts on the Android Developers blog so you can speak to current best practices in 2026, not patterns from a few years ago.

Before the interview
Confirm the interview format with your recruiter. Prepare two or three questions to ask the interviewer about the team, the tech stack, and how success is measured in the first six months. 07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping the 'why'. Saying 'I use Hilt for dependency injection' without explaining why you chose it over alternatives tells the interviewer nothing about your judgment. Always connect your choices to trade-offs.

Over-engineering design questions. Some candidates add complex caching layers and extra services to a simple app design to look impressive. Start minimal and add complexity only when the interviewer asks you to scale.

Vague STAR answers. 'We improved performance a lot' is not a result. Even without exact numbers, describe what changed: 'the screen loaded noticeably faster, ANRs on that screen disappeared, and users stopped mentioning it in reviews.'

Not asking clarifying questions. Jumping into a solution before clarifying requirements is a red flag in enterprise interviews. Interviewers want to see that you gather requirements before writing a single line of code.

Underestimating soft skills. Technical ability gets you to the final round. What closes the offer is how you communicate, how you handle pushback, and whether the team can picture working with you. Do not go silent or get defensive when challenged.

Poor time management in coding rounds. Candidates sometimes spend too long on one edge case and run out of time for the core solution. State your plan before you start coding, and check in with the interviewer if you are running low on time.
