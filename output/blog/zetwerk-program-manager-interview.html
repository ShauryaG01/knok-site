01 Overview

Overview

Zetwerk is one of India's largest B2B manufacturing marketplaces, connecting enterprise buyers with factories across categories like aerospace, heavy engineering, consumer goods, and infrastructure. As a Technical Program Manager at Zetwerk, you work at the intersection of product, engineering, and operations, driving programs that tie software platforms to physical supply chain realities.

The role calls for strong cross-functional coordination, stakeholder influence, and the ability to translate between technical teams and business or operations partners.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a time a key dependency slipped. How did you protect the overall program timeline?

*Situation:* At my previous company, we were building a supplier reconciliation module. A third-party API integration owned by a partner team was critical to our go-live, but that team hit resource constraints and signalled a three-week delay.

*Task:* I needed to protect the overall launch date and make sure my team did not sit idle while waiting.

*Action:* I mapped all workstreams to identify which were truly blocked versus which had only a soft dependency. I negotiated a partial stub contract with the partner team so our engineers could build and test against a mock. I rescheduled the QA cycle to run against the stub first, then do a focused regression once the real API landed. I updated the steering committee weekly with a clear blocked, at-risk, and on-track view.

*Result:* We absorbed the three-week delay into buffer and launched only four days late. The stub-first approach also surfaced three contract mismatches early, which would have caused a longer slip if caught in final testing.

---

Q: How have you bridged the gap between technical teams and operations or business stakeholders?

*Situation:* At a logistics startup, our warehouse management system team and the on-ground ops team spoke entirely different languages. Engineers tracked story points; ops managers tracked truck turnaround times.

*Task:* I needed both groups aligned on a shared roadmap for a new dispatch-automation feature.

*Action:* I introduced an impact-translation layer in our program reviews: every engineering milestone was paired with an ops metric it would move. I ran joint working sessions where ops managers narrated a day in their workflow and engineers mapped their features to specific pain points. I created a one-page status report that showed tech progress on one side and projected ops impact on the other.

*Result:* The next quarterly review was the first where ops leadership approved the roadmap without revision requests. Feature adoption post-launch was noticeably faster because ops teams had co-owned the definition from the start.

---

Q: Describe a time you pushed back on leadership over scope or timeline.

*Situation:* A VP wanted to add a new supplier-rating module to a platform migration program two weeks before go-live.

*Task:* I had to either absorb the scope or make a data-backed case to defer it, without damaging the relationship.

*Action:* I pulled the engineering leads for a quick estimate session and documented the risk: adding the module would require changes to three shared services, introduce new test cycles, and push us past a contractual deadline with a key enterprise client. I prepared a one-pager with two options: launch on schedule without the module, or delay the full launch by six weeks. I presented both to the VP with clear trade-offs and recommended the on-schedule path, proposing the rating module as the first post-launch feature.

*Result:* The VP accepted the recommendation. We launched on time, met the client deadline, and the rating module shipped five weeks later in a clean, standalone release.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use STAR for all behavioural questions. Keep Situation and Task brief: set the context without narrating the whole backstory. Action deserves the most time because that is what you controlled. Result closes with the impact, so always quantify or qualify it. 'Four days late instead of three weeks' beats 'we delivered on time.'

For 'how would you' design questions, open with the problem framing: who are the stakeholders, and what does success look like. Walk through your approach step by step. Close with how you would track and communicate progress. Interviewers are checking whether you think structurally.

For prioritisation questions, state your framework first (impact vs effort, or a grid of urgency and strategic value), apply it to the specific scenario, then acknowledge the trade-offs. Saying 'it depends' without a framework is a common weak answer.

For escalation questions, show you have a clear threshold. Try to resolve at the lowest level first, and escalate only when the risk is outside your control or when a decision requires authority you do not have. Interviewers at growth-stage companies want to see you are neither over-escalating nor under-escalating.

Quantify wherever possible, but be honest. If you do not recall the exact number, say 'roughly' or 'approximately.' Invented precision is easy for experienced interviewers to spot.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Zetwerk interviewers, based on candidate feedback, typically look for four things.

Comfort with ambiguity. Zetwerk moves fast and the requirements for a manufacturing-tech platform are rarely clean. They want to see that you can create structure in chaotic situations without waiting for perfect information.

Influence without authority. As a TPM you rarely control the engineers, vendors, or business teams you depend on. Interviewers probe for stories where you moved people without having formal power over them.

Operational empathy. Zetwerk's differentiation is that its tech actually works on the factory floor. Candidates who show they understand operational constraints, not just sprint velocity, stand out.

Bias for delivery. Growth-stage companies value people who ship. Interviewers will look for evidence that you close programs, not just manage them. Bring stories that end with 'we launched' or 'we unblocked the team.'

Candidates also report that culture conversations at Zetwerk often revolve around ownership and a builder mindset. Showing that you have contributed ideas beyond your job description is well-received.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

One week before the interview:

Read Zetwerk's publicly available content: their website, recent press coverage, and any engineering or product blog posts. Understand their core product (manufacturing marketplace), their buyer and supplier personas, and the tech challenges that come with coordinating physical production at scale.

Prepare six to eight STAR stories covering: a complex multi-team program, a dependency that slipped, a stakeholder conflict, a scope or timeline pushback, a process you improved, and a time you operated with incomplete information.

Three days before:

Practice answering out loud, not just in your head. Time yourself. Behavioural answers should land in two to three minutes. Run mock sessions with a peer if possible.

Review TPM fundamentals: risk registers, RACI matrices, OKR vs milestone-based tracking, and the difference between a program and a project. Zetwerk interviews typically include at least one 'how would you set this up' question.

Day before:

Prepare two or three questions to ask the interviewer. Good examples: 'What does the first ninety days look like for a new TPM here?', 'What is the biggest program coordination challenge the team is facing right now?', 'How does the TPM function interact with product and engineering leadership?'

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Being vague in STAR answers. 'I coordinated with multiple teams' tells the interviewer nothing. Name the teams, the conflict, and the specific action you took. Specificity signals real experience.

Treating TPM as a project management role. Zetwerk is hiring for program-level thinking: cross-team dependencies, strategic sequencing, and risk management across quarters. If your stories only cover managing a single team's sprint, reframe them to show the broader program context.

Not knowing Zetwerk's business model. Candidates who treat this as a generic tech interview miss the manufacturing-operations dimension. If you cannot explain what a manufacturer on Zetwerk's platform experiences day-to-day, your answers about operational empathy will feel hollow.

Over-escalating in case answers. Saying 'I would bring this to the VP' as a first move signals low confidence. Show that you try to resolve at the right level first.

Weak results in STAR stories. 'The project was successful' is not a result. Tie the outcome to a business or team impact, even if you have to say 'approximately' or 'about.'

Not asking questions. Candidates who ask nothing signal low interest. Zetwerk interviewers, like most at growth companies, treat curiosity as a signal of culture fit.
