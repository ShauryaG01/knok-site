01 Overview

Overview

Hyreo is an HR tech company that builds recruitment automation and candidate engagement platforms used by enterprise hiring teams across India. Designers here work on products that serve two distinct user groups: recruiters managing talent pipelines and candidates going through application and onboarding flows. As of July 2026, Hyreo had 96 open roles across the company, reflecting active growth and a product team that is scaling quickly.

For the UI/UX Designer role, candidates report a process that typically includes a portfolio screening, a take-home design assignment, and one or two interview rounds with product and design stakeholders. The process is generally conversational and focused on how you think rather than just what you have shipped. Expect questions about your design process, how you handle ambiguity, and how you work with engineers and product managers.

Because Hyreo's platform uses AI-driven features such as automated candidate matching and engagement tools, interviewers are particularly interested in how you design interfaces that make algorithmic outputs easy for users to understand and act on.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up frequently, based on what candidates report and the nature of Hyreo's product:

  1. Walk us through a project in your portfolio where you improved a key user flow. What was the problem and how did you know the redesign worked?
  2. How do you approach designing for two very different user types, such as recruiters and candidates, within the same product?
  3. Hyreo uses AI to surface recommendations to recruiters. How do you design interfaces that make AI-generated suggestions feel trustworthy and actionable?
  4. Describe your process when you receive vague or conflicting requirements from different stakeholders.
  5. How do you conduct user research when direct access to users is limited or the timeline is tight?
  6. Tell us about a time you simplified a complex multi-step workflow into a clean, usable interface.
  7. How do you handle a situation where a developer tells you your design is 'not feasible' within the sprint?
  8. What design and prototyping tools do you use, and how do you decide which tool is right for each stage of your work?
  9. How do you measure whether a design has succeeded after it ships?
  10. Describe a situation where usability testing revealed a serious problem with a design you were confident in. What did you do next?
  11. How would you approach designing a brand-new feature for a B2B SaaS product where you have no existing user data?
  12. What does accessibility mean in practice for you, and can you share a concrete example of how you have applied it in a project?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through a project where you improved a key user flow.

*Situation:* At my previous company, the product's onboarding had a significant drop-off at the document upload step. The team could see users were abandoning the flow but did not know why.

*Task:* My goal was to identify the friction points and redesign that step to increase completion.

*Action:* I ran a series of user interviews to understand where confusion was happening, mapped the pain points on a journey map, and found that users were overwhelmed by the number of fields shown at once. I redesigned the upload screen as a single-focus step with clear progress indicators and improved error messages. I tested the prototype with a small group of participants before handing off to engineering.

*Result:* After shipping, our analytics showed a meaningful improvement in step completion. The PM presented the data in the next sprint review and the pattern became a reference for how we designed other onboarding screens going forward.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you had to simplify a complex workflow.

*Situation:* The recruiter dashboard I was redesigning had a large number of filters displayed simultaneously on a single screen. The team knew this was overwhelming for new users but was afraid to remove functionality that power users depended on.

*Task:* I needed to reduce cognitive load without cutting any capability.

*Action:* I ran a card-sorting exercise with a group of internal users to find out which filters they used most often. I moved secondary filters into a collapsible panel and gave primary filters clear placement at the top. I built two prototype versions and ran a preference test before finalising the direction.

*Result:* After launch, the product team reported that support queries related to navigation confusion dropped noticeably. Follow-up usability sessions also showed new users completing their first search task faster than before.

---

Q: How do you handle developer feedback that your design is 'not feasible'?

*Situation:* During one sprint, a developer flagged that an animated transition I had designed would cause performance problems on older Android devices used by part of our user base.

*Task:* I needed to find an alternative that preserved the interaction intent without delaying the release or adding technical debt.

*Action:* I sat with the developer to understand the exact constraint, sketched a few lighter interaction alternatives, and quickly prototyped them. I confirmed feasibility with the developer and then brought the options to the PM so we could decide together. I documented the reasoning in our design notes for future reference.

*Result:* The feature shipped on time. The developer felt involved in the solution rather than just handed a problem, and the final interaction still felt intentional and polished.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For behavioural questions, use the STAR structure: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task brief so you spend most of your time on what you actually did (Action) and what changed because of it (Result). Interviewers at product companies care most about your thinking and your impact.

For design process questions (such as 'how do you approach a new brief'), use a Discover-Define-Design-Deliver structure. Briefly describe how you understand the problem, set success criteria, explore solutions, and validate before shipping. Adapt the depth based on what the interviewer is actually probing for.

For product thinking questions (such as 'how would you design X from scratch'), lead with the user problem before jumping to solutions. State who the user is, what they are trying to accomplish, what constraints exist, and then explain your design rationale. This signals that you think like a product designer, not just a visual designer.

For collaboration and trade-off questions, emphasise that you treat engineers and PMs as partners rather than blockers. Show that you can hold your design intent while being flexible on implementation details when the constraint is real.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Portfolio depth over breadth. Hyreo interviewers typically want to discuss a small number of case studies in detail rather than skim a gallery of finished screens. They want to understand your thinking: why you made specific decisions, what you tried that did not work, and how you validated your direction before shipping.

Product thinking. Because Hyreo serves recruiters and candidates in a B2B context, interviewers look for designers who understand business constraints alongside user needs. Be ready to talk about trade-offs between what users want and what the business can realistically support.

Collaboration and communication. Candidates report that interviewers probe how you work with engineers, PMs, and non-design stakeholders. Have specific examples ready of moments where you had to negotiate, explain your rationale, or adapt your design based on input from others.

Comfort with ambiguity. Features at a growing HR tech company often arrive with incomplete requirements. Interviewers look for designers who can make progress with what they have, ask the right clarifying questions, and validate assumptions quickly rather than waiting for a perfect brief.

Familiarity with B2B UX patterns. Dashboards, data tables, filtering systems, and multi-step forms are central to Hyreo's product space. If your portfolio includes enterprise or B2B work, lead with it.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Research Hyreo's product. Sign up for a trial or explore any public demos of their recruitment platform. Look at how they present the recruiter and candidate experience, note UI patterns, and think about what you would improve and why.

Step 2: Prepare a small set of strong case studies. Choose projects that show your end-to-end process: research, problem definition, design decisions, and outcomes. Prioritise work that involves complex workflows or data-heavy interfaces, since those are central to HR tech products.

Step 3: Practice presenting your work out loud. Walk through each case study within a set time limit. Focus on explaining why you made key decisions, not just what the final design looks like. Ask someone to interrupt you with 'why?' after each point you make.

Step 4: Practise a timed design exercise. Hyreo typically gives a take-home design task. Pick a B2B design problem, give yourself a few hours, and produce a structured response that shows your process and your thinking, not just polished screens.

Step 5: Prepare questions for the interviewer. Ask about the design team's structure, how design decisions are made, what a typical sprint looks like for a designer, and how design success is measured. These questions signal genuine interest in the product and the team.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Showing only final screens. Interviewers want to see your thinking, not polished mockups alone. A portfolio that shows finished UI without explaining the problem, the research, and the decisions behind it will not stand out in a product company interview.

Describing what you did without saying why. 'I redesigned the dashboard' is not a case study. 'I redesigned the dashboard because recruiters were spending too long finding their active candidates, and I solved it by reorganising the information hierarchy' is. Always anchor your choices to a user or business reason.

Ignoring the B2B context. HR tech products are used by professionals every day as a core part of their job. Consumer UX and enterprise UX are different in important ways: density, efficiency, and learnability matter more in B2B. If you only have consumer product experience, acknowledge it and show that you understand the difference.

Being vague about tools and process. Saying you use Figma is expected. Explaining how you structure components for a design system, how you manage handoff with engineers, or how you document design decisions shows professional maturity.

Not clarifying assumptions before designing. Whether you are given a take-home or a live design exercise, candidates who define the problem and state their assumptions before drawing screens consistently perform better. Write down what you are solving for before you open any design tool.

Framing all your work as solo achievements. Design at a product company is a team effort. If you describe every project as something you did alone, interviewers will wonder how you will fit into a cross-functional team.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Hyreo UI/UX Designer interview typically involve?

Candidates report a process that typically includes a portfolio screening or initial recruiter call, a take-home design assignment, and one or two rounds of interviews with design or product stakeholders. The exact number of rounds can vary based on the seniority of the role and the team's schedule at the time. It is worth asking the recruiter for a full outline of the process at the start so you can plan your preparation accordingly.

What kind of design assignment does Hyreo give?

Candidates typically report receiving a B2B product design prompt, often related to a recruiter-facing or candidate-facing interface. You are usually expected to submit a structured response showing your process rather than just final screens. The time allowed varies but is commonly a few days for a take-home task. Focus on defining the problem clearly, stating your assumptions, and showing at least one round of iteration or validation thinking rather than aiming for visual polish alone.

Does Hyreo care more about visual polish or product thinking?

Based on candidate reports, product thinking is weighted heavily at Hyreo, especially for mid-to-senior roles. Interviewers want to understand how you frame problems, make decisions under constraints, and collaborate with engineering and product. Visual polish matters for the presentation of your portfolio, but a designer who explains their reasoning clearly will typically outperform one who shows beautiful screens without context or rationale.

What salary can I expect for the UI/UX Designer role at Hyreo?

Hyreo has not publicly disclosed salary bands for this role. For market context, publicly reported data on platforms like Glassdoor or AmbitionBox can give you a sense of what UI/UX Designers earn at similar-stage HR tech companies in India. Come into salary discussions with a range based on your experience level and the cost of living in the city where the role is based, and be ready to justify your number with market data if asked.

Is the Hyreo UI/UX Designer role open to remote or hybrid work?

Work-from-home and hybrid policies at Hyreo can change and are best confirmed directly with the recruiter or on the specific job listing. At the time of the knok data snapshot in July 2026, UI/UX Designer openings across India were concentrated in Delhi (8 openings) and Bangalore (6 openings) in the broader market, which suggests most companies in this space still prefer candidates in major metros. Clarify location expectations early in the process to avoid surprises.

How should I structure my portfolio before a Hyreo interview?

Lead with a small number of detailed case studies rather than a large gallery of finished work. Each case study should clearly state the problem, describe your research or discovery process, walk through key design decisions, and share any measurable outcome or learning. If you have B2B or enterprise experience, put that first since it is directly relevant to Hyreo's product context. Make it easy for an interviewer to skim to the reasoning behind each project, not just the visual output.
