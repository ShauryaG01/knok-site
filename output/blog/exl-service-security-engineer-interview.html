01 Overview

Overview

Across all companies tracked, there are 628 Security Engineer jobs live, with Bangalore the dominant hub at 69 postings.

Security Engineers at EXL typically work on protecting data pipelines, cloud platforms, and client-facing analytics products that serve large enterprises in healthcare, insurance, and financial services. The work spans cloud security, identity management, compliance, and incident response.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

EXL Security Engineer interviews lean heavily on practical scenarios and on how you handle the intersection of security and business operations. Here are twelve questions candidates commonly encounter:

  1. Walk me through how you would respond to a suspected data breach in a client analytics environment.
  2. How do you approach securing a cloud-based data pipeline that processes sensitive client records?
  3. EXL works with healthcare and insurance clients. What data privacy regulations are relevant to these sectors, and how have you applied them?
  4. Describe your experience with vulnerability assessment and penetration testing. Which tools have you used and what was your process?
  5. How would you design identity and access management controls for a large enterprise deployment?
  6. What is your process for threat modeling when a new service or application is being built?
  7. How do you integrate security into a CI/CD pipeline without creating friction for development teams?
  8. EXL serves clients across multiple geographies. How do you manage compliance obligations across different regional data protection laws?
  9. Describe a time you had to communicate a security risk to a non-technical stakeholder. What was your approach?
  10. What experience do you have with SIEM platforms? How have you set up or tuned alerts to reduce false positives?
  11. You have a vulnerability backlog with more findings than your team can fix this sprint. How do you prioritize?
  12. How have you applied security frameworks and standards in a previous role, and how did you measure their effectiveness?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format for behavioural questions: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep answers to two to three minutes when speaking. Here are three examples tailored to EXL's focus areas.

Q: Walk me through how you would respond to a suspected data breach in a client analytics environment.

*Situation:* At my previous company, our SIEM flagged unusual outbound traffic from a server in our analytics cluster late on a Friday evening.

*Task:* I was the on-call security engineer responsible for containing the incident, assessing the scope, and communicating to both internal leadership and the affected client.

*Action:* I immediately isolated the affected server from the network to stop any further data movement. I pulled logs from the SIEM and identified a compromised service account with overly broad read permissions. I revoked the account, blocked the external IP address, and worked with the infrastructure team to patch the vulnerable application that had been exploited. I then coordinated with legal and account management to draft a client notification.

*Result:* We contained the breach within three hours and completed root cause analysis within twenty-four hours. The client received a full incident report within forty-eight hours. The incident led us to enforce least-privilege policies on all service accounts across our environments.

---

Q: Describe a time you explained a security risk to a non-technical stakeholder.

*Situation:* Our team found that a third-party vendor integration was transmitting customer data over an unencrypted internal API endpoint. Fixing it would require delaying a product launch by about a week.

*Task:* I needed to convince the business head to approve the delay without triggering panic or resistance.

*Action:* I skipped the technical details and framed the risk in business terms: the regulatory exposure from potential fines and audit findings, and the reputational risk if customer data was intercepted. I drew a simple diagram showing exactly where data was exposed and presented two options, one with a short delay to fix the issue and one that shipped on time but accepted the risk, with the likely consequences of each clearly laid out.

*Result:* The business head approved the delay without pushback. The integration launched securely the following week. That stakeholder later asked me to run a similar risk briefing for the broader leadership team.

---

Q: How do you prioritize vulnerabilities when your backlog is longer than your team can clear?

*Situation:* After a quarterly scan at my previous employer, we had a large number of findings across development and production environments, far more than one sprint could handle.

*Task:* I needed to produce a prioritized remediation list that the engineering team could act on without needing to understand the full security context of each item.

*Action:* I scored each finding on three factors: exploitability in our specific environment, criticality of the affected asset, and potential business or compliance impact if exploited. I separated production from development environments, pushed all critical production findings to the top, and grouped similar findings so that one fix could close multiple items. I built a tracking dashboard in our ticketing system to give the team clear visibility.

*Result:* The team cleared all top-priority items within two weeks. The grouping approach reduced total remediation effort significantly. This prioritization model became our standard process for future scans.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the most reliable structure for behavioural questions. Lead with brief context (one to two sentences), then explain your specific role, walk through what you actually did step by step, and close with a concrete outcome.

For technical scenario questions, use a think-aloud approach. State your assumptions first ('I would assume this is a cloud-hosted environment handling PII'), then walk through your reasoning in layers: identify the threat, assess the impact, describe the controls you would put in place, and explain how you would monitor and verify. This shows structured thinking even when you do not know the exact answer.

For compliance and framework questions, do not just recite standard names. Describe how you applied relevant controls in practice. Interviewers want to hear about a gap assessment you ran, a control you implemented, or a remediation plan you built, not a list of standards you have studied.

The CAR shortcut (Challenge, Action, Result) works well for shorter answers where context is already established. Use it when the interviewer has provided the scenario and just wants to know what you would do.

For all answers at EXL, connect your response to their business context: they serve large regulated clients, so frame your answers around data protection, audit readiness, and minimising client risk rather than purely technical outcomes.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates report, EXL Security Engineer interviewers evaluate five things:

Technical depth without jargon. They want to see that you understand how attacks work and how controls fail, not that you can recite standard names. Expect to walk through scenarios step by step rather than just name the right tool.

Client and data awareness. EXL's clients are in regulated industries. Interviewers typically probe whether you understand the security implications of handling sensitive data at scale, including what happens from a client relationship perspective when things go wrong.

Communication across audiences. Security Engineers at EXL often work with non-technical business teams and with client stakeholders. Candidates who can explain risk clearly and without condescension tend to stand out.

Incident response readiness. Candidates report a consistent emphasis on how you handle incidents: detection, containment, communication, and post-incident review. Having a clear and practiced process matters more than having a long list of tools.

Practical experience with security tooling. Familiarity with SIEM platforms, vulnerability scanners, cloud security controls, and identity management systems is valued. You do not need experience with every tool EXL uses, but you should be able to describe real scenarios where you used the tools you claim to know.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week one: Understand EXL's business and security context.
Read about EXL's service lines, especially their analytics, healthcare, and insurance work. Understand what categories of data they handle and why security matters to their clients. Review the specific job description carefully and map each requirement to a real experience from your background.

Week two: Brush up on technical fundamentals.
Review cloud security concepts for the platforms mentioned in the job description (commonly AWS, Azure, or GCP). Refresh your knowledge of network security, identity and access management, and encryption. Practice walking through threat models for simple architectures out loud, timing yourself.

Week three: Practice behavioural answers.
Write out STAR answers for the twelve questions listed above. Practice saying them out loud, not just writing them. Two to three minutes per answer is the right range. Get comfortable explaining technical concepts in plain terms, as if speaking to a business stakeholder who has no security background.

Week four: Mock interviews and logistics.
Do at least one mock technical interview with a peer. Research EXL's current news, recent client wins, and any publicly reported compliance initiatives. Prepare two or three thoughtful questions to ask your interviewers about the team's current security priorities and how they measure success.

For compliance and framework questions, review the publicly available documentation for major information security management standards and cloud security benchmarks. Focus on understanding the intent behind key control areas rather than memorising control identifiers.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Naming frameworks without explaining application. Saying 'I have experience with NIST and other security standards' and stopping there signals surface-level knowledge. Always follow with a specific example of how you applied a control, ran a gap assessment, or drove a remediation.

Generic incident response answers. Many candidates describe the textbook steps (detect, contain, eradicate, recover) without connecting them to a real scenario. EXL interviewers reportedly look for the specific decisions you made and why, not a recitation of the playbook.

Underestimating the compliance angle. Because EXL works with healthcare, insurance, and financial services clients, compliance is not a checkbox. Candidates who treat it as secondary to technical controls often do not advance past the first technical round.

Ignoring communication skills. Some candidates prepare heavily for technical questions and are caught off guard by stakeholder communication scenarios. Prepare at least two examples of explaining security risk to a non-technical audience before your interview.

Not asking questions. Candidates who ask no questions at the end of an interview are typically seen as less engaged. Prepare questions about the team's current threat priorities, how security fits into the product development process, or how the team measures its own effectiveness.

Overstating tool experience. If you mention a specific platform or tool, be ready to describe a real scenario where you used it and what outcome it produced. Interviewers often probe tool claims with targeted follow-up questions.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the EXL Security Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates typically report three to four rounds: an initial HR or recruiter screening, one or two technical rounds, and a final discussion with a hiring manager or senior team lead. The exact structure varies by team and location. It is worth confirming the format with the recruiter before each round so you can prepare the right material.

Does EXL ask for live coding or hands-on security exercises?

Candidates report that EXL's Security Engineer interviews are more scenario-based and conceptual than live-coding heavy. You are more likely to be asked to walk through a threat model, describe how you would investigate an alert, or explain how you would architect a control. Some technical roles may include a practical component, so confirm with the recruiter if you are unsure what to expect.

What security tools should I be familiar with before an EXL interview?

Candidates report that familiarity with SIEM platforms (such as Splunk or Microsoft Sentinel), vulnerability scanners, and cloud security tooling for major providers is valued. Identity and access management tools and endpoint detection platforms also come up in interviews. You do not need expertise in every tool, but you should be able to describe specific scenarios where you used the ones you mention.

How important is compliance knowledge for this role at EXL?

Very important. EXL's clients are predominantly in regulated industries like healthcare, insurance, and financial services. Candidates who understand data privacy regulations relevant to these sectors and can connect compliance requirements to actual technical controls stand out clearly. Pure technical skills without compliance awareness are rarely enough to advance through all rounds.

What is the best way to answer framework questions if I do not have direct audit experience?

Be honest and then pivot to what you do know. You could say something like: 'I have not led a formal audit, but I have implemented specific controls aligned with the framework's objectives and tracked remediation against those requirements.' Interviewers value honest, applied understanding over claimed credentials you cannot back up with real examples.

Is Bangalore the best city to apply for Security Engineer roles at EXL?

Based on knok's job radar data from July 2026, Bangalore leads Security Engineer postings across all companies with 69 openings, followed by Delhi and Pune at 12 each. EXL itself has 398 open Security Engineer roles across India. If you are open to relocation, Bangalore offers the widest choice, but EXL's scale means roles are available in multiple cities.
