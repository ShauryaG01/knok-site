02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions are drawn from candidate reports and common patterns in Android engineering interviews at product-focused FMCG tech companies. Expect a mix of core Android, system design, and behavioural questions.

  1. Walk me through the Activity and Fragment lifecycle. How do you handle configuration changes without losing state?
  2. What architecture pattern do you prefer (MVVM, MVI, or Clean Architecture) and why? How did you apply it on a real production project?
  3. Explain Kotlin Coroutines and Flow. When would you choose StateFlow over SharedFlow?
  4. AB InBev's BEES platform serves retailers in areas with unreliable connectivity. How would you design an offline-first ordering feature?
  5. How do you manage dependency injection in a multi-module Android project using Hilt or Dagger?
  6. Explain recomposition in Jetpack Compose. How do you prevent unnecessary recompositions that hurt performance?
  7. How do you diagnose and fix an ANR (Application Not Responding) error or a jank-heavy scroll list?
  8. Walk me through paginating a large product catalogue using Paging 3 with a REST API backend.
  9. How do you handle network failures gracefully in a Retrofit and OkHttp setup? What retry or caching strategy would you use?
  10. What is your approach to unit testing and UI testing in Android? Which tools do you rely on and why?
  11. Tell me about a time you shipped a critical feature under a tight deadline. What trade-offs did you make?
  12. How would you design a real-time order-tracking screen that performs well on low-bandwidth connections common in smaller Indian cities?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format for all behavioural questions. Aim for about two minutes per answer when spoken aloud. Below are three examples tailored to AB InBev's product context.

Q: Tell me about a time you built an offline-first feature.

*Situation:* I worked on a B2B commerce app used by distributors in Tier 2 cities where internet connectivity dropped frequently during the working day.

*Task:* We needed the product-ordering flow to work without a live connection, syncing reliably once connectivity returned.

*Action:* I used Room as the local source of truth, synced data to the server using WorkManager whenever connectivity was restored, and wrote conflict-resolution logic so that orders placed offline merged correctly with server state without duplicates.

*Result:* Crash rates on the ordering flow dropped noticeably, which our Crashlytics dashboard confirmed, and field sales teams reported far fewer stuck or lost orders in weekly standups.

---

Q: Describe a time you improved app performance.

*Situation:* Our app's home feed took several seconds to show content after launch, and users were dropping off before the screen loaded.

*Task:* I was asked to cut cold-start time before a major product release going out that quarter.

*Action:* I profiled the app with Android Studio Profiler, found heavy network calls and database reads happening on the main thread at startup, moved them to Coroutines on the IO dispatcher, added in-memory caching for the first screen, and deferred non-critical SDK initialisation using App Startup.

*Result:* Cold-start time fell by a meaningful amount tracked in Firebase Performance, and the product team observed improved day-one retention in their analytics.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you worked under a tight deadline.

*Situation:* A key retail partner required a digital invoice feature before their quarterly vendor review, with no room to shift the date.

*Task:* The feature had to be built, tested, and released in under two weeks with no existing foundation to build on.

*Action:* I split scope into a must-have core (PDF generation and a share sheet) and a nice-to-have (email directly from the app). I aligned on the API contract with the backend engineer on day one and wrote unit tests alongside the feature code to avoid a last-minute bug rush.

*Result:* The core feature shipped on time, the partner signed the renewal, and the email-from-app feature went out in the following sprint with no regressions reported.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For system design questions: Start with the user problem (who uses this feature, on what network, at what scale), then name your constraints (offline support, large datasets, sync conflicts), then walk through your component choices (local DB, sync strategy, UI layer), and finish with the trade-offs you considered. AB InBev's apps often serve retailers and delivery agents in varied network conditions, so always address offline and low-bandwidth behaviour explicitly.

For architecture questions: Name the pattern, explain the specific pain it solves (testability, lifecycle safety, separation of concerns), then give a concrete example from your own work. Interviewers want to know you have felt the problem the pattern exists to fix, not just read about it in a blog post.

For debugging and performance questions: Lead with your diagnostic process before jumping to the fix. Saying 'I opened Profiler, saw main-thread disk reads on startup, then moved them to a background Coroutine' is far stronger than 'I optimised the startup time.'

For behavioural questions: Keep Situation and Task short (one or two sentences each) and put most of your time into Action. Make Results specific where you have data, and honest where you do not. Saying 'I do not have the exact figure handy, but our internal Crashlytics dashboard showed a clear drop' is more credible than an invented number.

For 'Why AB InBev' questions: Connect your interest to something real: the scale of serving millions of retailers across India, the technical complexity of the BEES platform, or the challenge of building apps that work reliably in Tier 2 and Tier 3 markets. Generic answers about 'great culture' land poorly in technical rounds.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

AB InBev's engineering teams build at scale across India and globally. Based on candidate reports, interviewers typically look for the following qualities.

Solid Android fundamentals. Lifecycle management, threading, Jetpack components, and Kotlin are non-negotiable. You should explain not just what you used but why you chose it over the alternatives.

Product awareness. AB InBev is not a pure-play tech company. Engineers collaborate closely with product and business teams. Interviewers want to see that your technical decisions connect back to a concrete user outcome, not just a clean architecture diagram.

Ownership and delivery. FMCG moves fast. Stories about shipping under pressure, handling ambiguous requirements, or managing competing priorities resonate well in this environment.

Collaborative working style. Candidates report being asked about cross-team work: how you aligned with a backend engineer on an API contract, how you flagged a scope risk to a PM early, or how you helped a junior engineer get unstuck on a tricky lifecycle bug.

Pragmatic trade-offs. A candidate who says 'I used MVVM here because Clean Architecture added unnecessary layers for a single-screen flow' signals more engineering maturity than one who always reaches for the most complex solution.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Android fundamentals review
Revisit Activity and Fragment lifecycle edge cases, Kotlin Coroutines and Flow, Jetpack Compose recomposition, and dependency injection with Hilt. Build or revisit a small project that uses Room for local storage and communicates with a REST API. Hands-on practice stays with you far longer than reading documentation.

Week 2: System design and AB InBev context
Practise designing mobile features from scratch: offline-first ordering, real-time delivery tracking, large-catalogue pagination. Read publicly available information about the BEES platform and how AB InBev uses technology for retail operations. This context sharpens your system design answers and signals genuine interest in the company's specific challenges.

Week 3: Behavioural and coding practice
Prepare four or five STAR stories covering a tight deadline, a performance problem you fixed, a disagreement with a teammate you resolved well, and a feature you are genuinely proud of. Do a few coding exercises in Kotlin covering common patterns (hashmaps, trees, two pointers, BFS/DFS), as candidates report at least one DSA problem appears somewhere in the process.

Before each round: Review the specific job description for the team you are interviewing with. If it mentions a particular product (consumer app, B2B tool, supply chain), tailor your examples to that context. Prepare two or three specific questions for the interviewer about tech stack decisions, current engineering challenges, and what success looks like in the first few months.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Focusing only on code, not on context. Android interviews at product companies like AB InBev test whether you think about the user, not just the class hierarchy. Connect your technical answer to a real user outcome.

Skipping the 'why' in architecture answers. Saying 'I used MVVM' without explaining what problem it solved tells the interviewer nothing about your judgement. Always follow the pattern name with the reason you chose it for that specific situation.

Generic behavioural answers. 'I am a team player' with no specific story behind it loses points quickly in a structured interview. Every quality you claim must be backed by a concrete example.

Ignoring offline and low-bandwidth scenarios. AB InBev's B2B apps are used in markets where connectivity is unreliable. In any system design question with a field or retail angle, always address what happens when the network drops.

Not asking questions at the end of a round. Candidates who ask nothing signal low interest. Prepare specific questions about the team's current engineering challenges, release cadence, or the biggest technical decision they made in the past year.

Overcomplicating solutions. Proposing a complex distributed architecture for a feature that needs a simple API call and a local cache suggests poor scope judgement. Interviewers at product companies consistently value pragmatic, delivery-focused engineers.

How many rounds does the AB InBev Android Engineer interview typically have?

Does AB InBev ask data structures and algorithms (DSA) questions?

Candidates report at least one coding problem in the interview, typically involving arrays, strings, trees, or hashmaps solved in Kotlin or Java. The difficulty is generally not at competitive-programming level, but you should write clean, correct code with reasonable time complexity. Reviewing common patterns like two pointers, sliding window, and BFS/DFS is sufficient preparation for most candidates.

Is Jetpack Compose knowledge required for the Android Engineer role?

Based on candidate reports, Compose knowledge is increasingly expected rather than optional at AB InBev. You should be comfortable explaining recomposition, state hoisting, and the difference between remember and rememberSaveable. If your current project still uses XML layouts primarily, building a small Compose side project before the interview will close that gap quickly.

How important is knowledge of the BEES platform going into the interview?

You do not need deep internal knowledge of BEES, but showing awareness that AB InBev builds B2B apps for retailers and distributors helps you frame your answers well. Interviewers respond positively when candidates connect their design choices to real constraints like low connectivity, large product catalogues, and non-technical end users. That framing signals genuine interest in the company's actual work rather than a generic Android role.
