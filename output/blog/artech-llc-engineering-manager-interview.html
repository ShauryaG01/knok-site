02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates who have interviewed at Artech LLC for Engineering Manager positions typically report that questions cluster around people leadership, delivery in complex client environments, and technical credibility. Here are the questions you should be ready for:

  1. Tell me about a time you built an engineering team from scratch or significantly scaled one. How did you hire, onboard, and ramp people up?
  2. Describe a project where the deadline was at risk and the team was struggling. What did you specifically do to get it back on track?
  3. How do you handle a situation where a senior engineer on your team consistently underperforms or resists feedback?
  4. Walk me through how you set an engineering roadmap and decide what technical debt to address versus what new features to prioritise.
  5. Tell me about a time you had to push back on a product manager or a business stakeholder. How did you handle the disagreement?
  6. How do you keep engineers motivated and retain top talent in a competitive hiring market?
  7. Describe your experience working with offshore or distributed teams. What changes in how you communicate and manage?
  8. Tell me about a significant technical decision that went wrong on your watch. What happened and what did you change afterwards?
  9. How do you measure the health and productivity of your engineering team beyond just delivery metrics?
  10. Artech often works with enterprise clients on large, complex programmes. Tell me about a time you managed delivery across multiple stakeholders with competing priorities.
  11. What does your approach to 1:1s, performance reviews, and growth conversations actually look like in practice?
  12. How do you ensure quality and reduce technical risk when requirements are unclear or frequently changing?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a time you had to get a struggling project back on track.

*Situation:* Our team was several weeks into a product migration for a large enterprise client and had fallen significantly behind schedule. Two senior engineers had also raised concerns about the data-layer design we had committed to early in the project.

*Task:* I needed to quickly assess the actual risk, reset expectations with the client honestly, and find a credible path to delivery without losing the team's confidence in the process.

*Action:* I called a focused technical review with the engineers and our architect. We mapped the risks clearly and identified which parts of the design could stay and what needed to change. I then had a direct conversation with the client's project lead, sharing a revised timeline with clear milestones and the reasoning behind each change. I also temporarily pulled a senior engineer from another workstream to support the migration team.

*Result:* We shipped the migration later than the original target but within a range the client accepted. They appreciated the transparency, and we kept the account and the relationship intact. The two engineers who raised the issue told me it was the first time a manager had genuinely acted on their technical concerns rather than just reassuring them.

---

Q: Describe how you handled a senior engineer who was underperforming.

*Situation:* A senior backend engineer on my team had deep domain knowledge but had stopped delivering reliably. Junior engineers were quietly picking up the slack, and trust within the team was beginning to erode.

*Task:* I needed to address the performance issue fairly and quickly, without losing someone with valuable institutional knowledge or further damaging team morale.

*Action:* I started with a direct 1:1, asking open questions about what was really going on. It turned out he was burned out and felt his contributions were going unrecognised. I set a structured improvement plan with specific delivery goals, regular check-ins, and a clear statement that things needed to change. I also worked with him to identify a visible piece of work that played to his core strengths.

*Result:* His delivery improved noticeably within the plan period. He told me later that the structure and the honest conversation gave him a way to reset. The junior engineers noticed the change too, and overall team trust recovered over the following weeks.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you pushed back on a business stakeholder.

*Situation:* A product director wanted to ship a new feature on a timeline that our team's estimate said was not feasible without serious risk. The feature touched a payment flow with strict compliance requirements.

*Task:* I needed to present a clear, fact-based case for why the timeline created unacceptable risk, not just for the engineering team but for the business as a whole.

*Action:* I put together a concise one-page breakdown showing the engineering effort, the compliance checkpoints we could not skip, and three specific risks from rushing: data integrity issues, a potential audit finding, and technical debt that would slow our next quarters of delivery. I presented this jointly to the product director and the engineering head, and proposed a phased release that gave the business meaningful value sooner while protecting the compliance requirement.

*Result:* The phased approach was approved. The first phase launched on time. The product director later told me the risk breakdown was what shifted her thinking, because she had not understood the compliance dependencies before that conversation.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR is your default format

Most Artech EM interviews are behavioural, so STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) should be your default structure. Keep each story to three to four minutes. Interviewers typically cut you off if you run longer, and the Result is the part candidates most often rush or skip entirely.

For technical questions, walk through your decision process

When asked about architecture or technical decisions, follow this structure: state the problem and constraints first, describe the options you considered, explain why you chose what you did, and share what you learned or would do differently. This shows engineering depth without losing the interviewer in implementation detail.

For stakeholder and conflict questions

Use a simple three-part structure: describe the disagreement clearly, explain how you brought evidence or data into the conversation, and share the outcome for both the project and the relationship. Artech works in client-facing contexts, so showing you can manage up and sideways matters as much as managing your own team.

Anchor your answers with specifics

Include concrete details wherever they are honest: team size, delivery outcomes, quality or velocity changes with context. Interviewers remember specifics long after the interview ends. If you do not have exact numbers, say 'roughly' or 'approximately' rather than guessing or leaving the story vague.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

People leadership is the first filter

Artech EM interviewers consistently look for evidence that you can hire well, develop engineers, and handle difficult conversations with clarity and fairness. Vague claims like 'I am a people person' land flat. Specific stories about 1:1s, growth plans, and how you have handled underperformance carry real weight.

Delivery in ambiguous, client-facing environments

Because Artech frequently delivers complex programmes for enterprise clients, interviewers want confidence that you can drive delivery even when requirements shift, stakeholders are unclear, or your team is spread across time zones. Examples that show cross-functional coordination under real pressure stand out.

Technical credibility without going too deep

You do not need to write code in the interview, but you should be able to discuss architecture trade-offs, review the decisions your team has made, and explain why one approach was chosen over another. Interviewers want to trust that you can have a genuine technical conversation with your engineers, not replace one.

Communication that is direct and honest

Candidates report that Artech interviewers pay close attention to how clearly you communicate under pressure. Owning mistakes, showing what you learned, and demonstrating that you communicate proactively (not only when things go wrong) all come across well in the room.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Build your story bank

Write out eight to ten stories from your career covering: a project you recovered, a team you built or scaled, a conflict you navigated with a peer or stakeholder, a technical decision that went wrong, and a time you influenced without authority. Map each story to the STAR format now so you are not constructing them under pressure in the interview itself.

Week 2: Research Artech and the specific role

Read through Artech's publicly available materials: LinkedIn posts, job descriptions for the team you are joining, and any client case studies they publish. Candidates report that interviewers respond well when you connect your answers to the kind of work Artech actually does. If the role mentions a specific industry (banking, healthcare, retail), prepare at least one example from that domain if you have relevant experience.

Week 3: Practice out loud

Record yourself answering five questions and play them back. Most candidates are surprised by how much they hedge, repeat context, or bury the result. Ask a peer or mentor to run a mock interview with you. Focus especially on the 'difficult conversation' questions, which are the ones candidates most often answer vaguely or with softened details.

In the days before the interview

Prepare three to four sharp questions to ask your interviewers: about the team's current priorities, how success is measured in your first six months, and what challenges the team is actively working through. Asking good questions is itself part of the evaluation at the EM level.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Saying 'we' when the question asks about 'you'

Engineering Managers naturally use 'we' to describe team work, but interviewers are trying to understand your specific contribution. Use 'I' when describing decisions and actions you personally took. Save 'we' for team outcomes and shared achievements.

Rushing through the result

Most candidates spend too long on context and action, then land the result in a single sentence. The result is what proves your actions actually worked. Quantify it where you honestly can. If the outcome was mixed or took longer than expected, say so and explain what you learned. Interviewers respect that honesty.

Softening stories about difficult conversations

When asked about underperformance or conflict, many candidates smooth the edges to avoid sounding harsh. Interviewers see through this. A specific, honest account of a hard conversation handled fairly, with a real outcome, is far more credible than 'I had a chat and things improved.'

Skipping your questions at the end

Some candidates treat the 'any questions for us?' segment as optional. For an EM role, the questions you ask signal how you think about team health, delivery, and your own onboarding. Not asking, or asking only about compensation at this early stage, leaves a weaker impression than it should.

Going too deep on technical detail

Engineering Managers sometimes try to prove technical credibility by walking deep into architecture specifics. Artech interviewers are checking that you can hold a real conversation with senior engineers, not that you can replace one. Keep technical answers at the decision and trade-off level.
