01 Overview

Most Asked Questions

These questions reflect Thoughtly's product domain and what candidates at similar AI-focused companies typically report.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a project where latency was a hard constraint.

*Situation:* I was on the backend team at a fintech startup processing payment confirmations. The UX team had data showing that if the user's app screen did not update within two seconds of a bank response, users assumed the transaction had failed and retried it.

*Task:* My job was to reduce the time from bank webhook receipt to mobile push notification. We were sitting at a commonly reported four-to-five-second average, which was clearly too slow.

*Action:* I profiled the entire pipeline and found the bottleneck was a synchronous call to our notification service that waited for a database write to complete before dispatching. I separated the write from the notification using a lightweight job queue so the push fired as soon as the webhook was validated. I also added a cache layer for user device tokens to stop hitting the database on every notification.

*Result:* End-to-end latency dropped to under 1.5 seconds in staging. After shipping, the drop-off rate on the payment confirmation screen fell noticeably in the following week.

---

Q: Tell me about a time a production system you owned started failing.

*Situation:* About three months into a new job, a cron job I had inherited started failing silently every night. Users were not receiving their scheduled email digests, and no one had caught it for nearly a week.

*Task:* I needed to find out why it was failing, fix it fast, and make sure we would catch it next time before users noticed.

*Action:* I checked the logs and found the job was timing out on a table that had grown much larger than when the query was first written. The date filter column had no index. I added the index, rewrote the query to process users in batches, and set up an alert on job completion status so any future failure would page the on-call engineer within minutes.

*Result:* The job ran cleanly that night and has not timed out since. The alert has since caught two other unrelated cron failures before any user was affected.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you pushed back on a technical decision.

*Situation:* My team was planning to store all AI-generated call transcripts in a single shared database table with no per-customer partitioning. I raised a concern that this would become a compliance and performance problem as we scaled.

*Task:* I needed to make the case for a different approach without stalling the sprint we were already in the middle of.

*Action:* I put together a short one-page document showing the projected table growth based on current call volume, the latency risk for large customers querying across millions of shared rows, and two lightweight alternatives: row-level tenant filtering with an indexed tenant column, or schema-per-tenant for our largest accounts. I presented it at the next planning meeting and asked the team to weigh in rather than pushing my preferred option.

*Result:* The team agreed on the indexed tenant column approach, which we implemented in the same sprint with minimal extra effort. It also became the standard pattern for new tables going forward.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the most useful framework for behavioural questions at Thoughtly and most engineering interviews. Keep each part tight: one to two sentences for Situation and Task, three to four sentences for Action (the substance of your answer), and one clear Result with a concrete outcome wherever possible.

For system design questions, candidates report that interviewers at AI product companies respond well to answers that start with requirements clarification ('Is this read-heavy or write-heavy? How many concurrent users?'), then move to a high-level component diagram, then drill into one or two genuinely hard tradeoffs. Do not try to cover every edge case. Pick the hardest part and go deep on that.

For product or 'why Thoughtly' questions, use a three-part structure: (1) what you find genuinely interesting about the problem space, (2) a specific observation you made about their product or approach, and (3) what you want to build or learn there. Avoid generic answers about 'AI being the future.'

For tradeoff and build-vs-buy questions, a clean structure is: state your default principle, give one example where you followed it, and give one example where you made an exception and why. This shows you have a view without sounding dogmatic.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Comfort with real-time and distributed systems. Thoughtly's core product depends on low-latency pipelines connecting telephony, LLMs, and backend services. Interviewers are listening for whether you understand where latency comes from and how to reason about it in a principled way.

Genuine curiosity about AI and LLMs. You do not need an ML or research background. Candidates who have actually built something with an LLM API, experimented with prompting strategies, or thought about how to make AI outputs reliable will stand out from those who mention AI only in passing.

Ownership and speed. Early-stage companies typically want engineers who notice problems and fix them without being asked, ship fast, and write code others can maintain. Use your answers to show you have shipped things end to end, not just contributed to one piece of a large system owned by someone else.

Clear technical communication. In a small team, the ability to explain a tradeoff to a non-specialist colleague matters as much as making the right call. Practice saying: 'here is what I chose, here is what I gave up, here is why that was the right call for this context.'

Product sense. Because the team is small, software engineers at Thoughtly likely influence product decisions directly. Show that you use and think about the product, not just the code underneath it.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Know the product and the problem space

Use Thoughtly's product or watch available demos to understand how their AI voice agents work end to end. Read up on how telephony APIs connect to LLM pipelines, and how streaming responses work in practice. Be ready to discuss this architecture in your own words without reading from notes.

Step 2: Coding practice

Focus on data structures and algorithms problems at the medium difficulty level, since that is where most engineering coding rounds concentrate. Also practice problems involving queues, async processing, and string or stream manipulation, which are directly relevant to voice and text pipeline work.

Step 3: System design

Practice designing two or three systems relevant to Thoughtly: a real-time message pipeline, a webhook delivery service, and a multi-tenant data store. Write out each design and time yourself explaining it in under twenty minutes. Practice starting every design by asking clarifying questions, not by jumping to components.

Step 4: Behavioural prep

Prepare five to six STAR stories covering: a high-pressure production incident, a technical disagreement you navigated, a time you learned something fast, and a project you owned end to end. Say each one aloud and keep it under three minutes.

Before your interview

Check Thoughtly's recent blog posts, job descriptions, and any public engineering content. Note specific technical choices they mention. Bring two or three genuine questions about their stack, reliability approach, or roadmap.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping to a solution before clarifying requirements. In system design rounds, interviewers often leave the problem vague intentionally to see if you ask good questions. Pausing to clarify scale, constraints, and success criteria is not a sign of weakness. It is a signal of engineering maturity.

Treating the AI layer as a magic box. Saying 'and then the LLM handles it' without discussing latency, failure modes, or output validation will raise concerns at an AI product company. Show that you have thought about what happens when the model is slow, wrong, or unavailable.

Vague STAR answers. 'I improved performance' is less convincing than 'latency dropped to under 1.5 seconds.' Use concrete outcomes even when you cannot share exact internal figures. Phrases like 'noticeably by our internal metrics' or 'reduced enough that on-call pages stopped' are honest and specific.

Not knowing the product. Thoughtly currently has 4 open Software Engineer roles, which means competition is real and your interviewer is also evaluating product fit. If you cannot explain what Thoughtly does or why you find it interesting, that is a clear missed opportunity.

Over-engineering the design. Candidates report that interviewers at smaller AI companies prefer a simple, working design with clearly stated tradeoffs over a complex architecture that tries to solve every hypothetical future problem at once.

Asking nothing at the end. Not asking any questions signals low interest. Prepare two or three genuine questions about the team's current technical challenges, how they handle reliability, or what the first few weeks in the role look like.
