01 Overview

Overview

Zuddl is a B2B SaaS platform that helps companies run virtual, hybrid, and in-person events at scale. From webinars and town halls to large enterprise conferences, the product centres on two distinct audiences: event organizers who build and manage complex schedules, and attendees who navigate live sessions in real time. Designers at Zuddl work on intricate workflow problems where a single confusing UI step can affect an entire audience mid-event.

With 10 open roles listed as of mid-2026, Zuddl is actively hiring across experience levels. Candidates report the interview process typically runs across two to three rounds, starting with a portfolio walk and moving into a design exercise or take-home case study, followed by a conversation with a senior stakeholder. Zuddl does not publish official round names, so treat any process description as approximate.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates who have interviewed at Zuddl typically report questions across three themes: B2B product thinking, handling real-time and high-stakes scenarios, and cross-functional collaboration.

  1. Walk us through a B2B product you designed end-to-end. What made it difficult?
  2. Zuddl's organizers often manage live events with large audiences in real time. How do you design for high-pressure scenarios where mistakes are visible immediately?
  3. How would you redesign the onboarding flow for a first-time event host on Zuddl?
  4. Describe a workflow you simplified after it had grown too complex. What was your process and what did you cut?
  5. Tell us about a time you used research or data to push back on a product or business decision.
  6. How do you design a feature that needs to work consistently across web, mobile, and large-screen displays?
  7. What does a healthy design system look like in a startup that is still iterating quickly?
  8. How do you prioritize when three different teams each need design support at the same time?
  9. Describe a design decision that did not work. What did you learn and what did you change?
  10. How do you work with engineers to make sure a design is built the way you intended, especially under time pressure?
  11. How do you balance the needs of a power user (someone who runs events regularly) against a new user logging in for the first time?
  12. Zuddl competes with established event platforms. How do you think about differentiation through UX?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through a B2B product you designed end-to-end.

*Situation:* My team was building an admin dashboard for a logistics company. Operations managers were spending a large part of each day switching between separate tools just to get a basic status update on their fleet.

*Task:* I owned the UX for a unified command view that would consolidate these tools without overwhelming users who had different roles and different information needs within the same organisation.

*Action:* I started by shadowing three different user types for a week. I mapped their jobs-to-be-done separately, then looked for where their workflows overlapped. I ran a card sort to understand how they grouped information, and used that to define a modular dashboard where each user type could pin the panels most relevant to them. I worked closely with engineering to prototype a live data feed so the dashboard updated without requiring a full page reload.

*Result:* After three months in production, the ops team reported in a post-launch survey that their status-check time dropped significantly. The product lead used this as a case study in the next funding conversation.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you used data to push back on a product decision.

*Situation:* A PM wanted to add a mandatory multi-step setup wizard to a feature that already had healthy completion rates. The business reason was to collect more structured data from users upfront.

*Task:* I needed to make a clear case that the wizard would hurt completion without actually solving the underlying data problem.

*Action:* I pulled session recordings and funnel data. I found that most users completed the feature quickly, and that even a single extra step in similar flows had historically correlated with a drop in completion, based on our own A/B test records from an earlier release. I proposed an alternative: a progressive disclosure approach where the extra fields appeared contextually, only when a user triggered an action that actually needed them.

*Result:* The PM agreed to a short test of my approach. The contextual version collected comparable data with no measurable drop in completion and became the default implementation.

---

Q: Describe a design decision that did not work. What did you do next?

*Situation:* I redesigned a schedule builder for a virtual events tool. I was confident the new drag-and-drop interface was more intuitive than the old table-based one.

*Task:* My job was to validate the design before it shipped to all users.

*Action:* We ran a usability test with a small group of event organizers. Most of them struggled to understand how to lock a session time so it could not be moved accidentally. I had assumed the visual affordance was obvious, but it was not. I went back, added a visible lock icon with a clear tooltip, and moved the control to where users were already looking based on eye-tracking patterns from the test.

*Result:* In a follow-up test with a different group, every participant completed the lock action without assistance. The lesson I carried forward: never skip testing with the actual user type, even when an interaction feels self-evident to you.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for experience questions. Structure every story as Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task brief (two or three sentences each). Put the bulk of your time on Action, since that is what interviewers are actually evaluating: your process, not just the outcome.

Jobs-to-be-done for 'how would you design X' questions. Before sketching solutions, state the job the user is trying to accomplish and the context around it. For Zuddl, an organizer's core job is often: 'run a live event without anything going wrong in front of my audience.' That framing changes what good design looks like far more than any visual choice.

Constraint-first for prioritization questions. Name your constraints before naming your solution: timeline, engineering capacity, known user pain, business impact. Show that you think in trade-offs, not only in ideal-world outcomes. Interviewers at growth-stage startups particularly value this kind of thinking.

Metrics anchoring for outcome claims. Whenever you describe a design outcome, pair it with a metric or a proxy signal. 'The drop-off rate fell after launch' is stronger than 'users liked it.' If you do not have a number, say what you would have measured and why you would have chosen that metric.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Systems thinking over pixel craft. Zuddl's product has many interdependent states (pre-event, live, and post-event) with multiple user types acting simultaneously. Interviewers want designers who can hold that complexity in mind and make decisions that do not break one flow while fixing another.

B2B empathy. The end users are professionals with real stakes. Show that you understand the difference between designing for a consumer who is casually browsing and an event organizer who has a full audience waiting for a session to begin.

Comfort with ambiguity. Zuddl is a growth-stage startup. Candidates who ask good clarifying questions, state their assumptions explicitly, and adjust based on feedback tend to stand out over those who jump straight to a polished answer.

Strong portfolio communication. Your portfolio is only as good as your ability to talk through it. Interviewers are not just looking at the screens; they are listening for how you framed the problem, what you ruled out, and why you made the trade-offs you did.

Cross-functional fluency. Product designers at Zuddl work closely with PMs and engineers. Mentioning moments where you navigated disagreement or helped an engineer understand a design intent tends to land well with interviewers.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Use the product before your first round. Sign up for a Zuddl trial or attend a public webinar hosted on the platform. Run through the organizer setup flow from start to finish. Take notes on what feels smooth and what feels confusing. Interviewers notice when a candidate has clearly used the product versus when they have only skimmed the website.

Pick two or three relevant portfolio pieces. Prioritise B2B or enterprise projects, or any work involving dashboards, multi-step workflows, or real-time interfaces. If your background is mostly consumer, find the closest analogy in your work and be ready to explain how your thinking transfers to a higher-stakes context.

Prepare for a design exercise. Candidates report that Zuddl typically includes a take-home or live whiteboard challenge. Practice redesigning one flow of a familiar product out loud, in a single sitting. Narrate your trade-offs as you go; the reasoning matters as much as the output.

Research the competitive context. Know the names of two or three other platforms in the virtual events and webinar space. Be ready to articulate where Zuddl sits in that market and where you see UX opportunities that have not been addressed yet.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Presenting a consumer-only portfolio without translation. If all your work is consumer apps, you need to actively bridge the gap. Explain how your thinking applies to a high-stakes B2B context; do not expect interviewers to make that connection on your behalf.

Jumping to UI before defining the problem. In design rounds, many candidates start sketching screens immediately. Interviewers at Zuddl (candidates report) want to see you pause, ask clarifying questions, and frame the user's job before you begin designing.

Vague outcome claims. Saying 'users loved it' or 'the product improved significantly' without any supporting evidence weakens your story. Even small qualitative signals, such as fewer support tickets or faster task completion in a usability test, are better than no signal at all.

Not knowing what Zuddl actually does. Treating it as a generic events app is a common signal that a candidate has not prepared. Know the difference between their webinar product, their conference product, and how they position for enterprise clients.

Underestimating the stakeholder management question. Several candidates report being asked how they handle disagreement with PMs or engineers. Have a specific, balanced story ready, one where you listened, adapted, and still maintained design quality in the end.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Zuddl Product Designer interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically involves two to three rounds. This usually begins with a portfolio review or introductory call, moves into a design exercise (take-home or live), and ends with a conversation involving a senior team member or founder. Zuddl does not publish an official process, so treat any description as approximate and confirm the details with your recruiter.

Does Zuddl give a design assignment or take-home test?

Candidates commonly report a design exercise as part of the process, either a take-home task or a live whiteboard session. The prompts typically relate to event management workflows or user onboarding scenarios. Prepare by practising a timed redesign exercise on a product you know well, narrating your trade-offs out loud as you work.

Do I need B2B experience to get a Product Designer role at Zuddl?

B2B experience is a strong advantage because Zuddl's users are professionals with high stakes. That said, candidates with consumer backgrounds have cleared interviews by clearly explaining how their skills transfer. Focus your portfolio walk on workflow complexity, multi-user thinking, and cross-functional collaboration rather than visual polish alone.

What salary can I expect as a Product Designer at Zuddl?

Zuddl does not publicly disclose its pay bands. Across the broader Indian market, mid-level Product Designers (3-5 years of experience) are commonly cited in the 14-24 LPA range, and senior designers (6-9 years) in the 26-40 LPA range, based on knok jobradar data across 393 openings. For Zuddl-specific figures, check Glassdoor or levels.fyi and cross-reference with your recruiter during the offer stage.

Is Zuddl a good company for product designers to grow their career?

Zuddl is a growth-stage B2B startup, which typically means small design teams, direct exposure to complex product problems, and faster progression than a large enterprise. The trade-off is less process, fewer dedicated design ops resources, and more ambiguity in scope. It tends to suit designers who want real ownership and are comfortable making calls without a large support structure around them.

What is the best way to prepare my portfolio for a Zuddl interview?

Lead with one or two case studies that show you can handle complexity and multiple user types, ideally from a B2B or workflow-heavy context. For each piece, be ready to explain the problem you were solving, what approaches you ruled out, and how you measured or would have measured success. If your portfolio is consumer-focused, prepare a short bridge for each piece that connects it to Zuddl's product context before the interview.
