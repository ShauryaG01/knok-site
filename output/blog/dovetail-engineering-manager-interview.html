01 Overview

Overview

Dovetail is a customer research platform that helps product, design, and research teams capture, organise, and share user insights. As a remote-first company with roots in Australia, Dovetail places strong emphasis on async communication, deep craft, and building products that researchers genuinely love to use.

The Engineering Manager role here sits at the intersection of people leadership and product thinking. You are expected to grow engineers, partner closely with product managers and designers, maintain strong engineering standards, and help build a culture where customer empathy shapes every technical decision.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format for every behavioural question: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep the Situation brief, spend the most time on Action, and always land a concrete Result.

Q: How do you balance shipping velocity with long-term technical quality?

*Situation:* Our team was under pressure to ship a major integration feature before a competitor launched something similar.

*Task:* I needed to keep us moving fast without accumulating technical debt that would slow us down in future quarters.

*Action:* I introduced a lightweight 'tech debt budget' practice where the team reserved a portion of each sprint for refactoring and test coverage. I held a short weekly check-in with the tech lead to review any shortcuts we were taking and log them in the backlog. I also worked with the product manager to frame quality investments as risk reduction, which helped get buy-in from stakeholders.

*Result:* We shipped the feature on time. In the sprints after launch, we cleared the logged debt items and the team reported fewer production incidents. The practice became a standard part of how we plan.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you had to align engineering with a product team that had competing priorities.

*Situation:* Product wanted to accelerate a new analytics dashboard while engineering had committed to a platform migration critical for reliability.

*Task:* I needed to protect the migration timeline while keeping the product team informed and on-side.

*Action:* I set up a joint planning session where both teams mapped their dependencies and risks visibly. I proposed a phased approach: we would complete the migration in parallel with a lightweight version of the dashboard. I maintained a shared tracker updated weekly so both sides could see progress without extra status meetings.

*Result:* The migration completed on schedule and a simplified version of the dashboard shipped the following sprint. The product lead later said the shared tracker changed how we collaborated going forward.

---

Q: How have you helped an engineer grow toward a senior or staff-level role?

*Situation:* One of my engineers had strong coding skills but avoided leading cross-team technical discussions, which was holding back their growth.

*Task:* My goal was to give them structured opportunities to build that confidence without overwhelming them right away.

*Action:* I started by pairing them with a senior engineer on a small cross-team design review, then gradually gave them ownership of a larger initiative. I gave specific, written feedback after each interaction and ensured they had preparation time before any high-visibility meeting.

*Result:* Within about a year they were leading their own design review sessions independently and were promoted to senior engineer. They said the structured exposure made the difference.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is your core tool for all behavioural questions. Keep the Situation brief, spend the most time on Action, and always close with a concrete Result, even if it was partial or mixed.

People, Process, Product works well for questions about team health or delivery. Structure your answer around: how you set up your people (hiring, growth, feedback), what processes you put in place (planning, reviews, async norms), and how that connected to product outcomes.

The Trade-off structure is useful for technical or architecture questions. State the options you considered, the constraints you were working within, the decision you made, and what you would do differently now. Dovetail interviewers care about your reasoning process, not just the outcome.

The Empathy loop structure is worth preparing for user-centric questions. Describe how you created feedback loops between engineers and real users: things like shadowing research calls, reading support tickets, or inviting customers to sprint demos. This resonates strongly with Dovetail's product culture.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Dovetail interviewers typically look for a specific combination of qualities based on the company's culture and product area.

Deep product empathy. Dovetail builds tools for researchers. Interviewers want to see that you understand, and have helped your teams understand, what it means to build for a thoughtful, detail-oriented audience.

Async-first communication skills. As a remote-first company, Dovetail values written clarity. Expect questions that probe how you document decisions, run retrospectives, and keep distributed teams informed without over-meeting.

A coaching mindset over a doing mindset. Interviewers want managers who multiply the team's output rather than solve problems themselves. Answers that show you delegate, develop, and give structured feedback tend to land better than answers focused on your own technical contributions.

Comfort with ambiguity. Dovetail is a growth-stage company. Interviewers expect you to show that you can make decisions when information is incomplete and course-correct without drama.

Honesty about trade-offs. Polished answers that hide failures or skip difficult decisions flag poorly. Dovetail interviewers, candidates report, respond well to candid reflection on what went wrong and what you changed as a result.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

One week before your first round: Read everything publicly available about Dovetail's product, their engineering blog, and any talks or interviews by their engineering leaders. Understand what makes their research platform different from competitors.

Build your story bank. Write out a set of five or six experiences you can adapt for different questions: one on hiring, one on a technical trade-off, one on a difficult conversation, one on cross-functional alignment, one on a team culture initiative, and one on a failure with a clear learning.

Practise STAR out loud. Many candidates write good answers but stumble when speaking them. Time yourself and aim for answers that run two to three minutes without padding.

Research the specific role. Dovetail currently has 7 open Engineering Manager roles tracked on knok jobradar. If the job description names a team area such as infrastructure, product, or platform, tailor your examples to match that context.

Prepare sharp questions for the interviewer. Ask about how engineering and research teams collaborate directly, how engineering priorities get set, and what success looks like in the first few months. These questions signal genuine engagement and product-mindedness.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Saying 'we' instead of 'I'. Interviewers are assessing you as a manager. Make your specific role in decisions and outcomes explicit throughout your answers.

Skipping the Result. Candidates often run out of time or confidence before stating what actually happened. Always land the outcome, even if it was partial or included a lesson learned.

Being vague about process. 'I helped the team improve communication' means very little. Name the specific practice you introduced and describe how you knew it was working.

Ignoring the remote-first context. Answers built entirely around in-person team norms can feel out of place at a company that operates across time zones by design. Reference async practices and distributed team experience wherever relevant.

Over-indexing on technical depth at the expense of people stories. Engineering Manager interviews at product companies like Dovetail lean heavily on leadership and culture. Candidates who spend most of their time on architecture and code reviews, rather than team growth and cross-functional work, typically report lower success rates.

Not asking questions at the end. Dovetail interviewers, candidates report, tend to view a lack of questions as low engagement with the role. Prepare at least three thoughtful ones before your interview.
