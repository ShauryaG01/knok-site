02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions are commonly reported by candidates who have interviewed at Dream for QA Engineer roles.

Technical and Tools

  1. Walk me through how you would design a test strategy for a high-traffic feature like a live match scorecard.
  2. What is your experience with API testing? Which tools do you prefer and why?
  3. How do you approach performance and load testing? Describe a scenario where you validated a feature under peak load.
  4. What automation frameworks have you built or contributed to? What language did you use and how did you structure it?
  5. How does QA fit into your team's CI/CD pipeline? What do you automate at each stage?

Behavioral and Process

  1. Tell me about a time you disagreed with a developer about whether a bug was critical. How did you handle it?
  2. How do you manage QA when release deadlines are tight and not all tests are complete?
  3. Describe a situation where your testing caught a critical bug before it reached production.
  4. How do you work in a team where developers regularly skip writing unit tests?

Domain and System Design

  1. How would you test a real-time leaderboard feature that updates live for millions of users?
  2. How would you test a payment gateway integration end to end, including failure scenarios?
  3. If Dream were launching a new game mode during a high-stakes cricket event, what would your test plan look like?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for all behavioral questions. Here are three model answers.

---

Q: Describe a situation where your testing caught a critical production bug before it went live.

*Situation:* My team was preparing a major release for a payment flow update, just two days before a high-traffic tournament weekend.

*Task:* I was responsible for end-to-end validation of the checkout and wallet deduction flow.

*Action:* During exploratory testing, I noticed the wallet balance was not rolling back correctly when a payment failed mid-transaction. I reproduced it across three device types and raised it as a severity-1 issue, attaching logs and a screen recording for the developer.

*Result:* The bug was fixed and re-tested within the same day. The release went out on schedule. I documented the test case and added it to the regression suite so it would be caught automatically in future.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you had to deliver QA in a very tight sprint with incomplete requirements.

*Situation:* A feature for a new contest format was handed to QA late in the sprint, with the requirements document still being updated.

*Task:* I needed to ensure core flows worked without waiting for a finalised spec.

*Action:* I identified the must-test paths by speaking directly with the product manager and developer. I wrote a lightweight test checklist focused on entry, scoring, and result display. I flagged open questions in writing so there was a clear paper trail, and I ran parallel manual and automated checks on the known flows.

*Result:* We caught two regression issues in the scoring logic and demoed a stable build. The approach was later adopted by the team as a template for fast-track QA during short sprints.

---

Q: How have you built or improved a test automation framework?

*Situation:* At my previous company, the automation suite had grown organically and was failing intermittently, making CI results unreliable.

*Task:* I was asked to audit the suite and reduce flakiness.

*Action:* I identified the most frequently failing tests and categorised failures into wait-time issues, hardcoded test data, and environment dependencies. I introduced explicit waits, centralised test data management, and split the suite into smoke and full regression tiers so CI could run the fast tier on every commit.

*Result:* Flakiness dropped significantly, build confidence improved across the team, and developers started trusting CI results again. Merge cycles became noticeably faster.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For behavioral questions, use STAR:

  • Situation: Set the context briefly. One or two sentences is enough.
  • Task: State your specific responsibility, not the whole team's job.
  • Action: This is the most important part. Describe what YOU did, step by step. Be specific about tools, decisions, and reasoning.
  • Result: Give a concrete outcome. If you do not have a hard number, describe the impact clearly (team adopted the approach, release was not delayed, user trust was protected).

For technical questions, think aloud in a structured way:

  1. Clarify scope and constraints before jumping into an answer.
  2. State your approach at a high level first.
  3. Walk through specifics: tools, test types, edge cases, data setup.
  4. Mention what you would NOT test and why. Scope exclusions show maturity.
  5. Close with how you would measure quality or success.

For test strategy and system design questions:

Start with risk. Ask yourself: what is the worst thing that can go wrong here? Design your strategy around those risks first, then fill in regression and edge cases. Dream's platform handles real-money transactions and real-time data under extreme load, so interviewers expect you to prioritise financial accuracy, data consistency, and performance above all else.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Candidates who have cleared Dream QA interviews typically report that interviewers look for these qualities.

Ownership, not just execution. Dream's QA team is not a sign-off gate. Interviewers want to see that you treat product quality as a personal responsibility, not something that ends when a ticket is closed.

Depth on automation. Being able to 'write Selenium scripts' is table stakes. Interviewers probe framework design, maintainability, and integration with CI pipelines. They want to know how you handle flakiness, data management, and parallel execution.

Performance and scale awareness. Dream's platform faces extreme concurrency during live match events. Candidates who can speak to load testing, performance metrics, and failure-mode testing stand out from those who only know functional testing.

Clear thinking under pressure. Several candidates report that interviewers present a scenario with a tight deadline and watch how you prioritise, communicate trade-offs, and make decisions with incomplete information.

Business context. Understanding that Dream is a real-money gaming platform, where a payment bug or incorrect score directly harms users and company trust, signals that you think beyond test cases to actual user impact.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Technical foundation

  • Revisit your strongest automation framework. Be ready to explain design decisions, not just how to use the tool.
  • Practice API testing walkthroughs using tools like Postman or RestAssured. Cover happy path, error codes, and edge cases in your walkthrough.
  • Pick one load testing tool you have used (JMeter, k6, or Locust) and be ready to walk through a real scenario in detail.

Week 2: Domain and system thinking

  • Study how real-time systems work at a conceptual level: WebSockets, caching, eventual consistency. You do not need to build them, but you need to know how to test them.
  • Practice designing test plans for two scenarios: a payment flow and a live data feed feature. Time yourself to build the habit of structured answers.
  • Prepare at least five STAR stories covering: a bug caught before release, a conflict with a developer, a process improvement you drove, a tight deadline you navigated, and an automation win.

Week 3: Mock interviews and polish

  • Do mock technical interviews with a peer or through a practice platform. Ask for feedback on how clearly you think aloud.
  • Review your resume and align your stories to the keywords in Dream's job description.
  • Prepare three strong questions to ask your interviewer, focused on team structure, on-call practices, and how QA is involved in sprint planning.

Candidates report that Dream's process typically moves quickly once you clear the initial technical screen, so have references and offer documents ready in advance.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

1. Treating QA as a passive role in your stories. If your STAR answers always start with 'I was assigned to test...', you sound like an executor rather than a driver. Lead with decisions, initiatives, and ownership.

2. Vague automation answers. Saying 'I have experience with Selenium and TestNG' without explaining what you built, how you structured it, or what problems you solved does not differentiate you from any other candidate.

3. Skipping failure scenarios in test plans. Interviewers at product companies specifically probe whether you think about what happens when things go wrong: network timeouts, payment failures, data mismatches. Do not just test the happy path.

4. Not connecting QA to business impact. Saying 'I found several bugs' is weaker than saying 'I flagged a payment rollback issue that, if it had reached production on a match day, could have caused serious user trust and financial harm.'

5. Over-preparing for tools, under-preparing for thinking. Dream interviewers typically care more about how you reason through a testing problem than which exact tool you use. Practice thinking aloud, not just reciting tool names.

6. Asking no questions at the end. Candidates who ask nothing signal low interest. Prepare specific, curious questions about the team's quality culture, incident response, and how QA collaborates with product from the start of a sprint.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Dream QA Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically involves 3 to 5 rounds. This usually includes an initial screening call, one or two technical rounds covering automation and test design, and a final discussion with a senior engineer or engineering manager. Some candidates report an additional test strategy or system design round for mid and senior levels. Round structure can vary by team, so confirm details with your recruiter after the first call.

What salary can I expect as a QA Engineer at Dream?

Based on knok jobradar market data, QA Engineer salary bands are: Entry level (0-2 years) 4-9 LPA, mid level (3-5 years) 9-17 LPA, senior level (6-9 years) 17-30 LPA, and lead roles 28-45+ LPA. Actual offers at Dream depend on your specific experience, skills, and how you negotiate. Glassdoor and levels.fyi carry publicly reported figures from Dream employees that you can cross-check before your offer discussion.

Does Dream focus more on manual or automation testing in interviews?

Candidates consistently report that automation is the primary focus, especially for mid and senior roles. Interviewers typically probe framework design, CI integration, and how you handle test flakiness over time. Manual and exploratory testing skills are also valued, particularly in test strategy questions. Having both, with clear depth in automation, gives you the strongest profile for Dream's QA interviews.

Is performance testing knowledge required for a QA Engineer role at Dream?

For senior and lead roles, candidates report that performance and load testing knowledge is very commonly asked about. Given Dream's scale during live events, the ability to design and interpret load tests is a real differentiator. For entry and mid level roles, a solid conceptual understanding of performance testing (what to measure, which tools exist, what a baseline looks like) is typically sufficient, though hands-on experience always helps your case.

How should I prepare for a test strategy question at Dream?

Start your answer by identifying the biggest risks for the feature, not by listing test types. For Dream, those risks typically involve real-money accuracy, real-time data correctness, and system behavior under peak concurrent load. Walk through your test layers: unit, integration, API, UI, performance, and exploratory. Mention what you would explicitly leave out of scope and why. Interviewers want to see structured risk-driven thinking, not an exhaustive checklist.
