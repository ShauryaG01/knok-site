01 Overview

Overview

Bigbasket.com is India's largest online grocery and daily-needs platform, part of Tata Digital. PMs here work on problems like delivery slot optimisation, homepage personalisation, private label growth, the BB Star subscription program, and staying competitive against quick-commerce rivals. As of July 2026, there are 4 open PM roles at Bigbasket.

Candidates report a process that typically spans several rounds: a recruiter screening, at least one product sense or design round, a metrics and analytical thinking round, and a behavioural or leadership discussion with a senior leader. Some candidates also report a take-home case study, particularly for senior roles. Round order and labels vary by team, so confirm the structure with your recruiter.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How would you improve Bigbasket's slot-booking experience to reduce cancellations?

*Situation:* In a previous role at a logistics-heavy e-commerce platform, cancellation rates spiked whenever customers could not get their preferred delivery window.

*Task:* I was asked to reduce order-level cancellations without adding delivery capacity.

*Action:* I ran a funnel analysis and watched session recordings to find that most cancellations happened at the slot-selection screen when only distant windows appeared first. I worked with engineering to re-rank available slots by match to each customer's past delivery preferences, and added a 'notify me when my preferred slot opens' nudge. I also coordinated with the ops team to surface under-utilised early-morning slots that were being wasted.

*Result:* Cancellations at the slot-selection step dropped noticeably within the first month. I was transparent that this was a pre-and-post comparison, not a controlled experiment. I would apply the same diagnosis-first approach at Bigbasket, starting with drop-off analysis and customer interviews before proposing any solution.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you used data to reverse a product decision.

*Situation:* Our team had launched a simplified product detail page on mobile, removing what we considered clutter.

*Task:* I was tracking post-launch metrics when I noticed add-to-cart rates had improved but repeat purchases of the same item within a month had dropped.

*Action:* Digging into the data, I found that removing nutritional and ingredient details was causing customers to hesitate on repurchase because they could not recall which variant they had liked. I presented cohort data to the team and proposed restoring those details behind a collapsible section, targeting the food and health categories first.

*Result:* The team reversed the change for that category. Repeat-purchase rates recovered within about six weeks. I was clear in my presentation that this was cohort-level observation, not a controlled test, and flagged the causal limitation explicitly.

---

Q: How would you prioritise features for the consumer app versus BB Daily (B2B)?

*Situation:* At a previous company, I managed a platform with both consumer and business-facing segments competing for the same engineering bandwidth.

*Task:* I needed a prioritisation framework the whole team could use consistently, rather than making ad-hoc calls each sprint.

*Action:* I adapted RICE scoring so that 'reach' was weighted by revenue contribution per segment, and 'impact' was assessed against each segment's north-star metric separately. I ran monthly calibration sessions with business and ops stakeholders to revisit the weights as priorities shifted.

*Result:* Reprioritisation debates dropped significantly. Both segments saw more predictable feature delivery because trade-offs were transparent and documented rather than implicit or political.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For product design and improvement questions:
Start with the user, not the solution. Ask: who uses this feature, what job are they trying to do, and where do they fail today? Define success metrics before listing ideas. This prevents the common pattern of jumping straight to feature concepts without diagnosing the real problem first.

For metrics questions:
Use a funnel structure. Name your north-star metric and explain why you chose it over alternatives. Then break it into leading indicators across acquisition, activation, retention, and revenue. For Bigbasket specifically, retention and basket size tend to matter more than pure acquisition because grocery is a high-frequency, habitual category.

For prioritisation questions:
Use RICE (Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort) or a simple impact-versus-effort matrix. Tie your choices back to Bigbasket's current focus: in 2026, that likely means retention, quick-delivery capability, and private label growth.

For estimation questions:
State your assumptions out loud. Break the problem into components (households in the city, smartphone penetration, online grocery adoption), multiply through, then sanity-check your answer against anything you know about the market. Interviewers value clear, structured reasoning far more than a precise final number.

For behavioural questions:
Use STAR: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task brief (two or three sentences), spend most of your time on Action (the specific decisions you made and why), and be honest about Results, including any caveats about data quality or causal claims.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Bigbasket interviewers typically look for a few qualities specific to the company's context.

Comfort with operational complexity. Grocery delivery involves dark stores, perishable inventory, and last-mile logistics. Candidates who treat the app as a pure software product, ignoring supply chain constraints, tend to struggle in case rounds.

User empathy for real Indian shoppers. Bigbasket's core users include households managing monthly budgets, working professionals scheduling weekend deliveries, and tier-2 city customers who are relatively new to online grocery. Interviewers want to see you think about these users concretely, not as generic 'personas'.

A clear view on competition. Bigbasket is up against well-funded quick-commerce players. Expect questions where you need a defensible point of view on what Bigbasket can win at and what it should not try to match directly.

Metric ownership. Strong candidates name a north-star metric for any feature, explain why they chose it, and describe guardrail metrics they would watch to catch unintended side effects.

Structured communication. Bigbasket PM rounds move quickly. Interviewers appreciate candidates who lead with a structure before diving into details, and who check in with the interviewer ('Does this direction make sense?') rather than talking at length without pausing.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Understand the product and business deeply.
Spend time as a Bigbasket customer if you are not already. Order groceries, explore BB Star, try the B2B offering if relevant. Read recent coverage of Bigbasket's strategy, Tata Digital's plans, and the quick-commerce competition. Note where the app experience feels rough or requires more effort than it should.

Week 2: Build frameworks and practice out loud.
Practice product-design and metrics questions out loud each day, or with a peer. Record yourself once to catch unstructured answers and filler words. Prepare a short 'why Bigbasket' story that references specific product decisions you find interesting, not just the brand or its scale.

Week 3: Mock interviews and story preparation.
Do full mock interviews covering product design, metrics, estimation, and behavioural rounds. Prepare three or four STAR stories from your past work that can flex across strategy, execution, data, and cross-functional collaboration questions. For each story, know your outcome clearly and be ready to acknowledge what you could not prove causally.

Ongoing: Keep a note of anything surprising you observe in the Bigbasket app or in grocery-delivery news. Bringing a specific, recent observation into the interview signals genuine curiosity, not just rehearsed answers. 07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating Bigbasket like a pure tech startup. The company runs dark stores, manages perishable inventory, and operates a last-mile delivery network. Answers that ignore these constraints come across as surface-level.

Generic answers that could apply to any e-commerce company. If your answer to 'How would you improve Bigbasket?' could be copy-pasted for Flipkart or Amazon, it is not specific enough. Reference BB Star, the slot-booking model, or the quick-commerce challenge by name.

Jumping to solutions before diagnosing the problem. Many candidates skip user research and funnel analysis and go straight to 'I would build a feature that...'. Bigbasket interviewers typically want to see the diagnosis step first.

Ignoring margin and operational cost. Bigbasket operates on thin margins. Feature ideas that carry significant operational cost without a clear retention or revenue upside are unlikely to impress.

Weak or vague STAR stories. Outcomes like 'the product improved' or stories where your personal contribution is unclear hurt your candidacy. Be specific about what you owned, what decision you made, and what the outcome was, with honest caveats about data limitations.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Bigbasket PM interview typically have?

Candidates report several rounds on average, though the exact number varies by team and level. Typically this includes a recruiter screening, at least one product sense round, a metrics or analytical thinking round, and a final leadership or cultural-fit discussion. Confirm the structure with your recruiter before the loop begins, as it differs across teams and seniority levels.

Is there a take-home assignment in the Bigbasket PM interview?

Some candidates report a take-home product case or written exercise, particularly for senior-level roles. Others go through only live interview rounds. Because the process is not standardised across all teams, ask your recruiter upfront whether a take-home is part of the loop for your specific opening, so you can plan your time accordingly.

What salary can I expect for a PM role at Bigbasket?

Based on knok jobradar data from July 2026, ranges run from 12-20 LPA for Associate PMs up to 55-90+ LPA for Group or Principal PM levels. PM roles requiring 3-6 years of experience typically fall in the 24-40 LPA band. Actual offers depend on your level, current compensation, and any competing offers, so always come prepared to negotiate.

How important is grocery or e-commerce domain knowledge for this role?

Bigbasket does not require prior grocery experience, but candidates who understand the operational side, including dark store logistics, perishable SKU management, and delivery slot economics, tend to stand out. If you are coming from a different domain, map your past product work to analogous problems in grocery, and show you have genuinely used the product before your interview.

How should I prepare for questions about competing with quick-commerce apps?

Form a clear point of view before the interview. Think about what Bigbasket does better than quick-commerce players, such as a deeper SKU catalogue, planned weekly shopping behaviour, and cost efficiency for large basket sizes, and what it should not try to match directly. Interviewers want to see you reason about competitive positioning, not just describe the landscape from the outside.

Where are most Bigbasket PM openings located?

Bigbasket is headquartered in Bangalore, so most PM roles are typically Bangalore-based. For broader context, knok's jobradar tracked 271 PM openings in Bangalore and 177 in Delhi across all companies as of July 2026, reflecting how heavily the PM job market skews toward Bangalore. Some Bigbasket roles do come up as remote or hybrid, so check individual job descriptions for location requirements.
