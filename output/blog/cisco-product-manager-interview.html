01 Overview

Overview

Cisco is one of the world's leading networking, security, and collaboration technology companies. With 208 open Product Manager roles tracked by knok as of July 2026, Cisco is one of the most active PM hirers in the Indian tech market right now.

Cisco PM interviews typically span multiple rounds covering product sense, analytical thinking, behavioural fit, and some technical depth. Candidates report that the process is structured and business-outcome-focused. Interviewers want to see how you frame customer problems, how you define success metrics, and how you connect product decisions to Cisco's enterprise strategy.

Cisco's PM roles span several business units: networking infrastructure, cybersecurity, cloud collaboration (Webex), and AI-powered products. Your prep should reflect which unit you are interviewing for.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Based on candidate reports and the nature of Cisco's enterprise-focused business, here are the questions you are most likely to face.

  1. Cisco sells to large enterprise IT teams as well as smaller businesses with limited in-house IT staff. How would you prioritise features for a product that must serve both audiences?
  2. How would you define and measure success for a major new feature on Cisco Webex?
  3. Walk me through how you would investigate and respond to an enterprise customer who is churning after years on a Cisco platform.
  4. Describe a time you had to make a product decision with incomplete or conflicting data. What did you do?
  5. Cisco is shifting from hardware-centric revenue to software subscriptions. How does that change a PM's priorities and success metrics?
  6. How would you improve Cisco's SASE offering for mid-market customers who lack a large in-house security team?
  7. Tell me about a time you had to align stakeholders with conflicting priorities. How did you get to a decision?
  8. How do you think about building products for IT administrators versus the end users those admins serve?
  9. Design a new feature for Cisco Webex that improves meeting engagement for remote teams.
  10. How would you prioritise a roadmap when engineering capacity is limited and sales is pushing for several different features at once?
  11. What is a product you use and admire? What would you change about it and why?
  12. Cisco competes with players like Juniper and Palo Alto in parts of its portfolio. How do you think about competitive product positioning in an enterprise context?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a time you had to align stakeholders with conflicting priorities.

*Situation:* At my previous company, we were building an enterprise dashboard feature. The sales team wanted custom reporting fields to close a large deal faster. Engineering wanted to hold off because the customisation would create technical debt in the core data model. My product design counterpart wanted to focus on a navigation redesign that had been deprioritised for months.

*Task:* I needed to get all three teams to a shared decision without losing the deal or creating a maintenance burden.

*Action:* I set up a joint session with representatives from all three groups. Before the meeting, I mapped each team's concern to the business outcome they actually cared about. I proposed a time-boxed solution: a lightweight custom field that lived outside the core data model, shipped in the current sprint to protect the deal, with a written commitment in our tracker to revisit the navigation work in the next planning cycle.

*Result:* The deal moved forward. Engineering felt heard because we avoided the debt they flagged. Design had a documented commitment rather than an open-ended deferral. The custom field was later sunset cleanly when we did a proper rebuild.

---

Q: Walk me through how you would investigate and respond to an enterprise customer who is churning.

*Situation:* At a B2B SaaS company I worked at, a key account that had been on the platform for several years began the offboarding process unexpectedly.

*Task:* I was asked to lead a retrospective to understand the root cause and prevent similar churn.

*Action:* I pulled usage data for the account over the previous two quarters and identified a steep drop in active users roughly four months before the churn notice. I then interviewed the account's primary admin and two end users separately to triangulate what changed, and also spoke with the customer success manager covering the account. The picture that emerged was that a competitor had approached them with a capability our platform lacked, and our support response time during a critical incident had eroded trust. I documented the findings and brought a proposal to leadership: a fast-track fix for the support escalation workflow and a prioritised roadmap item for the missing capability.

*Result:* We did not save that particular account in time, but the escalation workflow change was cited positively in the next renewal cycle by accounts with similar profiles. The missing capability entered the roadmap and shipped in the following half.

---

Q: Describe a time you had to make a product decision with incomplete data.

*Situation:* We were deciding whether to build native offline mode for our mobile app. Usage analytics showed drop-offs but could not tell us whether users were leaving because of connectivity issues or unrelated UX friction.

*Task:* I had to make a build-or-defer recommendation to the engineering lead without clean data.

*Action:* Rather than delay indefinitely, I designed a quick qualitative study. I reached out to a sample of users from regions where connectivity is less reliable, ran brief interviews, and cross-referenced support tickets tagged with 'connection' or 'sync' keywords. I also reviewed publicly reported app store reviews to see how often connectivity came up as a complaint. Based on this mixed-signal approach, I built a lightweight business case: a simple offline queue with low engineering effort versus a full offline mode, with a clear decision point tied to a pilot.

*Result:* We shipped the lightweight offline queue first. User complaints related to connectivity dropped noticeably in support tickets over the following quarter, validating the direction and giving us the data to justify the fuller investment.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Cisco interviewers typically expect structured thinking. These frameworks come up repeatedly in PM interviews at enterprise tech companies.

For product design and improvement questions: Use a user-first structure. Start by identifying who the user is (in Cisco's case, often an IT admin, a network engineer, or an executive buyer). Name their core job-to-be-done before you propose any solutions. Candidates who jump to feature lists without defining the problem rarely score well.

For prioritisation questions: A simple Impact vs. Effort framing works well and is fast to explain in an interview. You can also reference a RICE-style scoring (Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort) to show rigour. For Cisco specifically, factor in enterprise sales cycles: a feature that unblocks a large renewal often gets weighted higher than a pure engagement play.

For metrics and success questions: Anchor on a North Star metric that reflects the core value your product delivers. Then layer in input metrics (actions users take that drive the North Star) and guardrail metrics (things you must not break). For a Webex feature, your North Star might be 'meaningful meeting minutes per active team', not just total logins.

For root-cause and investigation questions: A structured funnel approach helps: move through acquisition, activation, retention, revenue, and referral. Start at the top and work down. Cisco interviewers appreciate when you acknowledge what data you would need but do not have, rather than fabricating certainty.

For behavioural questions: STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the clearest structure. Keep Situation and Task brief. Spend most of your time on the specific Actions you personally took. Results should be concrete: even if you cannot share exact numbers in an interview, describe the direction of change and why it mattered to the business.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Cisco PM interviewers are typically looking for a specific combination of signals that reflects the enterprise software context.

Enterprise customer empathy. Cisco's buyers are IT leaders, network administrators, and procurement teams. Interviewers want to see that you understand how enterprise product decisions differ from consumer ones. Enterprise users often cannot simply switch tools: they have procurement processes, security approvals, and training cycles to manage.

Technical credibility, not engineering depth. You do not need to write code, but you need to speak credibly with engineers about trade-offs. Cisco's products sit on complex infrastructure. Candidates who can engage with a platform architect without hand-waving score higher than those who defer entirely.

Data discipline. Candidates who can define a clear metric, explain what it measures and what it does not measure, and describe how they would run an experiment or analyse a trend stand out. Vague answers like 'I would track engagement' are a red flag at any PM level.

Cross-functional ownership. Cisco's PM role involves working closely with engineering, design, sales, and customer success. Interviewers probe whether you can influence without authority and keep a diverse stakeholder group moving toward a shared goal.

Business model awareness. Cisco's ongoing shift toward software subscriptions and recurring revenue is a live topic internally. Showing that you understand how subscription metrics (retention, expansion, net revenue retention) differ from one-time licence metrics signals you are thinking at the right level for the role.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused preparation approach over two to three weeks covers what Cisco interviewers typically test.

Company and domain research. Read Cisco's most recent investor relations materials and earnings call summaries. Understand which product lines are growing and which are being consolidated. Pick the one or two business units most relevant to your target role and go deep: read product documentation, watch Cisco Live session recordings, and note the problems customers report in forums and app store reviews.

Framework practice and mock interviews. Choose two product design questions and two metrics questions from the list in this guide. Write out full STAR answers for at least three behavioural questions using real experiences from your own work history. Practice saying these answers out loud. A good PM answer is thorough but does not meander.

Gap-filling and interviewer research. Look up your interviewers on LinkedIn before each round. Note their backgrounds: engineers-turned-PM, business-side, or design. This often signals what they weight most. Prepare two or three thoughtful questions to ask at the end of each round. Questions about product strategy, team structure, or current challenges leave a better impression than silence.

Keep a running document of Cisco product decisions you find interesting, competitor moves, and customer pain points you read about. Referring to specific, observed examples in your answers is far more compelling than reciting frameworks. 07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

These are the patterns that most often cost candidates a Cisco PM offer.

Bringing a consumer product lens to enterprise questions. Talking about 'delightful UX' or 'virality' without connecting to enterprise adoption, IT admin workflows, or procurement realities signals a mismatch. Cisco's buyers are professionals operating inside organisations with strict change-management processes.

Jumping to solutions before defining the problem. Many candidates hear a product design question and immediately list features. Interviewers notice when you skip user identification and problem framing. Spend a meaningful portion of your answer on the problem before proposing anything.

Vague metrics. Saying 'I would track user engagement' without specifying what engagement means in this context, how you would measure it, and what a good versus bad result looks like is a red flag at any PM level.

Using 'we' when the interviewer wants to hear what 'you' did. In STAR answers, vague collective ownership hides your individual contribution. Interviewers are evaluating you, not your team.

Not asking clarifying questions. Launching into a long answer without clarifying scope, user, or constraints suggests you would do the same in a real product meeting. Asking one or two short clarifying questions before you begin shows good instincts.

Underestimating the technical depth expected. Cisco builds infrastructure products. Candidates who brush past technical trade-offs with 'I would work with my engineers on that' lose credibility. You do not need to be an engineer, but you should be able to discuss architectural trade-offs at a conceptual level.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Cisco PM interview process typically have?

Candidates typically report a process that includes an initial recruiter screen, one or two hiring manager conversations, and a panel or loop of several rounds covering product sense, behavioural questions, and sometimes a case study or take-home exercise. The exact structure varies by level and business unit, so ask your recruiter what to expect before each stage.

Does Cisco give a PM case study or take-home assignment?

Some candidates report receiving a short take-home case or an in-interview case exercise, particularly at the Senior PM level and above. These cases typically involve Cisco's own product areas rather than generic consumer scenarios. Prepare to structure your answer around enterprise customer needs and clear success metrics, not just user experience improvements.

How important is networking domain knowledge for a Cisco PM role?

It depends on the business unit. Roles focused on Cisco's core networking and security infrastructure typically expect stronger domain knowledge, while roles on Webex or newer AI-driven products may weight product and analytical skills more heavily. Review the job description carefully and research the specific team to calibrate how deep you need to go on networking or security concepts before your interview.

What salary can I expect as a PM at Cisco India?

Based on knok's job radar data, PM salaries at the 3-6 year experience level typically fall in the 24-40 LPA range, while Senior PM roles tend to be in the 40-60 LPA range. Glassdoor and levels.fyi community reports suggest actual offers also include RSUs and other benefits that can meaningfully add to total compensation. Always negotiate after receiving an offer.

Which cities have the most Cisco PM openings in India?

Bangalore leads with 271 PM openings tracked by knok, followed by Delhi with 177 and Mumbai with 56. Pune has 31 openings, Hyderabad has 24, and Chennai has 18. Cisco's Bangalore office is the largest engineering and product hub in India, so roles there tend to have more scope for growth into senior positions.

How do I stand out if I do not have enterprise software experience?

Cisco interviewers look for transferable skills: structured thinking, customer empathy, cross-functional collaboration, and data discipline. If your background is in consumer products, prepare specific examples of how you have worked with complex stakeholder groups, managed technical trade-offs, or driven business outcomes beyond user growth metrics. Bridge the gap explicitly rather than hoping the interviewer makes the connection for you.
