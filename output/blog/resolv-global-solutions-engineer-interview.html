01 Overview

Overview

Resolv.Global is actively hiring Solutions Engineers, with 151 open roles listed as of July 2026. The Solutions Engineer sits at the intersection of technical know-how and client engagement: your job is to help enterprise customers adopt and get real value from the product, from first demo through to a successful go-live.

Candidates typically report a multi-stage process covering an initial HR or recruiter screen, one or two rounds focused on technical and product knowledge, and a final conversation with a senior stakeholder or team lead. The sequence and number of rounds can vary, so confirm the format with your recruiter once you are scheduled.

The role rewards people who combine genuine technical depth with strong communication skills. Interviewers look for evidence that you can hold your own with a client's engineering team and, in the same breath, explain the same concept to a CFO in plain language.

Most Asked Questions

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time you managed a difficult implementation that was at risk of going off-track.

*Situation:* I was the lead Solutions Engineer for a mid-market client migrating from a legacy CRM to our platform. Two weeks into onboarding, we discovered their data model had custom fields our standard import tool did not support.

*Task:* I needed to protect the agreed go-live date while resolving a technical blocker that required cross-team coordination.

*Action:* I immediately looped in our implementation team and opened a ticket with engineering for a custom mapping script. At the same time, I reframed the migration timeline with the client, splitting it into two phases so they could go live on core workflows first. I set up daily check-ins to keep all parties aligned and updated the client's project lead after every session.

*Result:* The client went live on core modules on schedule. The custom fields were migrated in the second phase, two weeks later, with no data loss. The client expanded their contract in the following quarter.

---

Q: How do you explain a technically complex feature to a non-technical decision-maker?

*Situation:* A VP of Operations at a manufacturing client could not understand why an API-based integration was better than their existing file-based process.

*Task:* I needed to get their sign-off on the technical design without losing them in jargon.

*Action:* I used a simple analogy: the file-based process is like sending letters through post, while the API is like a phone call that gives you a response in real time. I then tied the technical advantage directly to a business outcome they cared about, faster stock reconciliation. I prepared a one-page visual summary in place of a technical diagram, and sent it ahead of the meeting.

*Result:* The VP approved the integration design in that meeting. Their team confirmed in a post-launch review that reconciliation time had dropped noticeably, which they cited as the key business win.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you collaborated with sales to close a deal that was at risk.

*Situation:* A large prospect had gone quiet after an initial demo. The sales rep flagged that the prospect's IT team had unresolved concerns about data security.

*Task:* My role was to address the technical objections clearly and honestly, without over-promising on the product roadmap.

*Action:* I joined a follow-up call specifically to walk through the security architecture, including encryption standards and access-control design. I then prepared a written FAQ document the prospect's IT team could share internally. Where questions touched on future features, I was transparent about what was live versus what was on the roadmap, so there were no surprises post-sale.

*Result:* The prospect's IT team gave their sign-off within a week, and the deal closed in the same quarter. The sales rep later said the written FAQ was the turning point because it gave the internal champion something concrete to share upward.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use STAR for every behavioural question. Structure your answer as Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task brief, spend most of your time on Action, and always close with a concrete outcome. If you do not have a perfect example, adapt a close one and be transparent about the context.

Use Problem-Solution-Impact for hypothetical scenarios. When asked how you would handle a technical situation, frame it as: here is the problem, here is what I would do, and here is the outcome I would aim for. This shows structured thinking without memorised scripts.

Apply the 'so what' test. After every answer, check whether you stated what actually happened as a result of your actions. Solutions Engineer interviews reward evidence of outcomes, not just activity.

Anchor technical explanations to business value. When you describe a technical approach, pair it with the customer benefit. Do not just say 'I used webhooks instead of polling.' Say 'I switched to webhooks, which cut latency and reduced API call volume for the customer.' The second version shows you understand why the decision mattered.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Solutions Engineer interviews at companies like Resolv.Global typically assess four things.

Technical credibility. You do not need to be a software engineer, but you must be comfortable reading API documentation, understanding integration patterns, and holding a technical conversation with a client's engineering team without losing your footing.

Customer empathy. Interviewers want to see that you lead with the customer's problem, not the product's features. Candidates who talk only about what the product does, without connecting it to what the customer needs, typically score lower.

Cross-functional collaboration. Solutions Engineers sit between sales, product, and engineering. Expect questions about how you communicate upward, escalate blockers constructively, and turn customer feedback into useful product input.

Communication clarity. Your ability to simplify complex topics, both verbally and in writing, is directly evaluated. Practise explaining your past technical work in plain language before the interview.

Ownership mindset. Interviewers often look for signs that you track customer outcomes beyond your own immediate deliverables. Show that you follow up, measure results, and take accountability for the full implementation, not just your slice of it.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Understand the product. Spend time on Resolv.Global's public website, product pages, and any available case studies or whitepapers. Map out what problems the platform solves, who the typical buyer is, and what integrations it supports. This is the foundation for almost every interview question you will face.

Step 2: Build your story bank. Write out five to six past experiences covering: a difficult implementation, a customer conflict you resolved, a cross-team collaboration, and a technical concept you explained to a non-technical audience. Use STAR format for each so you are not constructing them live in the room.

Step 3: Research the company context. Look at recent news, LinkedIn posts from the Resolv.Global team, and any publicly available customer reviews on sites like G2 or Capterra. Knowing the company's current direction lets you tailor answers and ask sharper questions.

Step 4: Practise a live demo or walkthrough. Solutions Engineer interviews sometimes include a demo exercise. If you have access to any comparable SaaS platform, practise walking through a feature as if presenting to a client, focusing on business outcomes rather than feature descriptions.

Step 5: Prepare three to five smart questions. Ask about the onboarding process for new Solutions Engineers, how success is measured in the first few months, and how the team handles product gaps flagged by clients. These show genuine interest and a structured, professional mindset.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping the business outcome. Candidates describe what they did in detail but forget to say what actually happened. Always close STAR answers with a concrete result, even a qualitative one.

Over-indexing on technical depth. Solutions Engineers are not software engineers. Spending the entire interview on architecture, without showing customer empathy, signals a poor fit for the customer-facing parts of the role.

Giving hypothetical answers to behavioural questions. Saying 'I usually do X' instead of describing a specific incident is a red flag. Interviewers want real examples, not described processes.

Failing to research the product. Arriving at a Solutions Engineer interview without understanding what Resolv.Global's product does is one of the fastest ways to lose the interviewer's confidence. Even a brief review of the product page makes a visible difference.

Not preparing questions to ask. Candidates who have no questions at the end signal low engagement. Prepare at least two thoughtful questions.

Overpromising on product gaps. When asked how you handle a feature the product does not have, saying 'I tell the customer we will build it' is a red flag. Interviewers want to see honesty about limitations combined with skill at managing the customer relationship through them.
