01 Overview

Overview

Lithic is a US-based fintech company that builds card issuing and payment infrastructure APIs. Businesses use Lithic's platform to launch virtual and physical card programs without building compliance and banking plumbing from scratch. With 10 open roles currently listed, including Engineering Manager positions, Lithic is actively growing its engineering leadership.

The EM interview at Lithic is fintech-specific. Candidates typically face questions about leading high-reliability API teams, working under compliance constraints, and making product decisions in a regulated environment. Candidates report the process typically includes a recruiter screen, a hiring manager conversation, and a panel covering leadership, cross-functional collaboration, and technical judgment. No specific round names are standardised, so confirm the format with your recruiter after the first call.

For market context, knok's job radar data shows Engineering Manager roles in comparable companies range from 35-60 LPA at Manager level, 55-90 LPA at Senior Manager level, and 90-150+ LPA at Director level. Lithic's specific compensation is not widely published, so check Glassdoor and levels.fyi for current candidate reports.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Here are 12 questions that come up frequently in Lithic EM interviews, drawn from the company's product domain and engineering culture:

  1. Tell us about yourself and why you want to work at Lithic. Interviewers want to hear that you understand the card-issuing API business, not just that it is a 'fintech startup.'
  1. How have you managed engineers building APIs used by external developers? Developer experience and API reliability are central to Lithic's product. Your answer should cover how you set quality standards and handle breaking changes.
  1. Describe a time you balanced feature velocity with compliance or regulatory constraints. Fintech teams live in this tension constantly. Be specific about the actual constraint, not just the general idea.
  1. How do you build and run an on-call rotation for a high-availability payments system? Lithic's infrastructure must always be on. Interviewers want to know you take reliability seriously at the process level, not just the technical level.
  1. Tell us about a time you drove a major technical initiative from idea to production. This tests your ability to lead through ambiguity, align stakeholders, and ship.
  1. How have you handled a team member who was underperforming? A classic people question, but Lithic probes for specifics, not platitudes.
  1. How do you think about technical debt in a fast-moving fintech product? They want to see you make pragmatic trade-offs, not just cite best practices.
  1. Describe how you collaborate with product managers and compliance or legal teams. Cross-functional work is central to fintech engineering. Show you can operate in that environment without becoming a bottleneck.
  1. How do you hire and evaluate senior engineers? Lithic cares about technical bar. Walk through your process with concrete examples.
  1. Tell us about a time your team missed a deadline or a launch did not go as planned. They are looking for ownership, learning, and process improvement, not excuses.
  1. How do you build psychological safety on a team shipping critical financial infrastructure? Lithic's culture values candid feedback and learning from failure, especially in high-stakes environments.
  1. What does good engineering look like to you in a payments context? This is a values question. Have a grounded answer that connects reliability, developer experience, and security.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time you balanced feature velocity with compliance constraints.

*Situation:* I was managing a payments team at a fintech company when our compliance team flagged that PCI-DSS requirements would force us to rearchitect how we stored card metadata, right in the middle of an active sprint.

*Task:* I needed to keep the feature on track for a committed customer launch while meeting the compliance requirement. Slipping the date was not an option because a major enterprise customer was waiting.

*Action:* I sat with our compliance officer and tech lead to scope the minimum required change. We split the sprint: part of the team handled the compliance rework on a separate branch, the rest continued the feature build. I updated the product manager daily and flagged blockers the moment they came up.

*Result:* We shipped only a couple of days late instead of the multi-week slip the compliance team initially feared. The rearchitecture also improved our audit trail, which became a point in later customer security reviews.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you drove a major technical initiative from idea to production.

*Situation:* Our card issuing service was hitting latency spikes during peak transaction periods. Customer complaints were growing and we risked breaching our SLAs.

*Task:* As EM, I needed to lead an initiative to identify the root cause, build a fix, and ship it without disrupting ongoing product work.

*Action:* I ran a structured problem-framing session with the team. We identified a database hot-spot in our authorisation flow and built a two-phase plan: a short-term caching fix and a longer-term schema change. I negotiated with product to ring-fence a small group of engineers for the migration work and communicated progress to leadership every week.

*Result:* Latency dropped back within our SLA targets, which we verified on internal dashboards, and customer complaints stopped. The initiative also built the team's confidence in tackling infrastructure problems proactively, which changed how we prioritised future work.

---

Q: How have you handled a team member who was underperforming?

*Situation:* A senior engineer on my team was consistently missing code review deadlines and output quality had dropped noticeably over two quarters.

*Task:* I needed to address this directly without losing a technically strong person who had historically been a high performer.

*Action:* I had a private, direct conversation and asked what was going on. It turned out they were dealing with a personal situation that was affecting their focus. We agreed on a temporarily reduced scope with bi-weekly check-ins, and I connected them with available HR support.

*Result:* The engineer returned to full capacity after about six weeks. They later told me that conversation was the reason they did not quit. They went on to lead our next major API migration.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

The STAR format is your foundation. Every behavioral question at Lithic should be answered with a specific story, not a general principle. Vague answers like 'I always communicate clearly' do not land in EM interviews at this level.

For technical trade-off questions, use a structured framing. State the options you considered, explain how you weighted reliability, developer experience, compliance, and speed to market, then share the outcome and what you learned.

For people and culture questions, show two things: what you did AND what changed as a result. Interviewers want to see that your actions had a real effect on the team or the individual, not just that you followed a process.

Calibrate your answer length. Most EM questions deserve a two to three minute answer. Shorter reads as shallow. Longer loses the interviewer. Practise trimming your stories to their essential arc before the interview.

For 'how do you think about X' questions, give a clear opinion first, then support it with a concrete example. Lithic is a builder culture. They want EMs who have clear engineering values, not ones who hedge everything.

Numbers matter but context matters more. If you mention a result, explain why it mattered to the business. 'We reduced latency' is weaker than 'we reduced latency and that unblocked a deal we had been working toward for months.'

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Lithic EMs are expected to operate at the intersection of strong engineering judgment and real people leadership. Based on publicly available information about Lithic's engineering culture and what candidates report from interviews, here is what they typically look for:

Fintech domain fluency. You do not need prior card-issuing experience, but you should understand concepts like authorisation flows, PCI compliance, chargebacks, and why reliability is non-negotiable in payments. Surface this understanding naturally in your examples.

Ownership without ego. Lithic's culture values people who own outcomes, including failures, and who attribute success to their teams. Interviewers watch closely for blame-shifting or vague responsibility.

Technical depth you can translate. You will not be expected to write production code as an EM, but you should be able to speak to architecture decisions your team made and explain why they were the right call at the time.

Cross-functional credibility. EMs at Lithic work closely with product, compliance, and sometimes directly with enterprise customers. Show that you can operate in those conversations and move things forward.

Clear communication under pressure. Payments companies face incidents. Interviewers want to know you can stay calm, communicate clearly, and lead a team during an outage or a difficult compliance review.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Start with the product. Go through Lithic's developer documentation and understand how the card issuing API works. Know what a virtual card is, how authorisations flow, and what compliance layers are involved. This knowledge makes your examples land with credibility.

Map your own stories before the interview. List experiences from your career that cover: shipping a major feature under pressure, handling a difficult people situation, a technical failure and what you learned, a cross-functional conflict, and a time you raised the engineering bar on your team. Write each one in STAR format.

Practise out loud. EM interview answers need to be clear at speaking pace, not just on paper. Record yourself or practise with a peer. Cut any story that takes more than three minutes to tell.

Prepare smart questions. Asking 'what does success look like in the first 90 days?' or 'how does the EM role work with the compliance team day to day?' signals that you understand the Lithic context, not just the generic EM job description.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Giving generic leadership answers. Saying 'I believe in servant leadership' or 'I always empower my team' without a specific story is a fast path to rejection at Lithic. Every answer needs a real example behind it.

Ignoring the fintech context. Candidates who treat a Lithic EM role like any other software company EM role miss the point. Compliance, reliability, and API quality are not optional concerns here. Weave that understanding into your answers naturally.

Underselling failures. Lithic interviewers are not looking for a perfect track record. They want to see how you handle hard situations. Candidates who only talk about wins come across as either dishonest or inexperienced.

Being vague about your technical role. Some EMs swing too far into 'I focus on people, not code.' At Lithic, you need to show an informed technical opinion. You do not need to have written the code, but you need to have understood it.

Not asking questions. A candidate who asks nothing at the end of an interview typically leaves a weak impression. Have a few specific questions ready about the team's current challenges, the product roadmap, or how the EM role interfaces with compliance and sales.

Rushing through answers. Candidates report that Lithic interviewers prefer depth over breadth. One well-told story with a clear result beats three thin summaries.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Lithic Engineering Manager interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically includes a recruiter screen, a hiring manager conversation, and a panel round with two or more interviewers covering behavioral and technical topics. Some candidates also report a final conversation with a senior leader. The exact structure can vary by team and level, so confirm the format with your recruiter after the first call.

Do I need fintech experience to be considered for an EM role at Lithic?

Not necessarily, but you should demonstrate genuine understanding of fintech concerns like compliance, payment reliability, and API developer experience. Candidates who have led teams in adjacent domains such as banking technology, identity platforms, or developer-facing APIs have reportedly done well. The key is showing that reliability and regulatory constraints are part of how you think, not just buzzwords you drop in an interview.

What salary can I expect for an Engineering Manager role at Lithic?

Lithic does not publicly list India-specific compensation bands. Based on knok's job radar, Engineering Manager market rates in comparable fintech companies range from 35-60 LPA at Manager level, 55-90 LPA at Senior Manager level, and 90-150+ LPA at Director level. For Lithic's specific numbers, Glassdoor and levels.fyi are the most reliable current sources, as sample sizes there are larger.

How technical is the Lithic EM interview?

The Lithic EM interview is not a live coding round, but you will be asked about technical decisions your team made and how you would approach complex engineering trade-offs. Candidates report that some panels include a system design or technical discussion framed around leadership and decision-making rather than code. Reading Lithic's public engineering content and understanding their API architecture before the interview is strongly recommended.

How long does the Lithic hiring process typically take from first screen to offer?

Candidates report timelines of roughly two to four weeks from the first recruiter screen to an offer, though this varies based on team availability and the number of rounds involved. If you have a competing offer with a deadline, communicate that timeline to your recruiter early. Following up after each round is standard practice and generally received positively.

Is there a system design round for Engineering Managers at Lithic?

Candidates report the EM interview at Lithic focuses primarily on leadership and judgment rather than whiteboard system design or live coding. However, some panels include a technical discussion where you walk through architecture decisions or how you would guide your team through a complex design problem. Being able to discuss payment system architecture at a conceptual level, including trade-offs around consistency, latency, and compliance, is useful preparation.
