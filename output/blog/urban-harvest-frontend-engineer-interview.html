02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates at Urban Harvest typically face questions across three themes: React and JavaScript fundamentals, product thinking for consumer and operations interfaces, and how you collaborate across design and engineering. Here are the questions most commonly reported:

  1. Walk us through a React project you built end-to-end. What state management approach did you choose and why?
  2. How would you design a product listing page that loads quickly on a slow mobile connection?
  3. Urban Harvest serves both consumers and internal ops teams. How do you build reusable components that work for both audiences?
  4. How would you implement real-time stock or availability updates on a product page without constant page refreshes?
  5. How do you make sure your UI works well on low-end Android devices common in tier-2 and tier-3 Indian cities?
  6. Describe your approach to frontend testing. What tools have you used and what do you actually test?
  7. How would you debug a page that feels slow or 'janky' on scroll?

  9. How would you design a UI element that communicates limited or expiring stock urgently without annoying the user?
  10. What accessibility considerations do you keep in mind when building consumer product pages?
  11. How do you work with designers when mockups change after development has already started?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How would you design a product listing page that loads quickly on a slow mobile connection?

*Situation:* At my previous company, we had a grocery listing page that took too long to load on a 3G connection, and users were dropping off before they saw any products.

*Task:* I was asked to bring load time down without doing a full redesign of the page.

*Action:* I audited the page with Lighthouse and found three main issues: all product images were loading at full resolution, the entire product list was rendering at once, and we were loading a large JavaScript bundle upfront. I introduced lazy loading for images, added virtual scrolling so only the items visible on screen render in the DOM at any time, and code-split the page so only the listing component loaded on the first visit.

*Result:* Load time dropped noticeably on 3G. Bounce rate on that page improved as well, which the product team confirmed in their next analytics review.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you handled a tricky API error in a user-facing app.

*Situation:* We had a checkout flow where the payment API occasionally timed out, and our frontend responded by showing a blank screen with no message.

*Task:* I needed to make the error experience clear and recoverable so users did not just abandon their carts.

*Action:* I added a try-catch wrapper around the payment call, defined specific error states for timeout, network failure, and server error, and mapped each one to a friendly message with a retry button. I also added a loading skeleton so the user could see the request was in progress rather than staring at nothing.

*Result:* Support tickets related to the blank checkout screen dropped sharply in the next sprint cycle, and the team adopted the same error-handling pattern across other flows in the app.

---

Q: How do you build reusable components for very different audiences?

*Situation:* At a logistics startup, I worked on both a customer-facing mobile web app and an internal warehouse ops dashboard. Both teams needed similar UI elements, like data tables and status badges, but with very different styling and information density needs.

*Task:* I was asked to create a shared component library that both teams could use without duplicating or forking code.

*Action:* I designed components with props for 'variant' (compact vs. comfortable) and 'theme' (consumer vs. ops), keeping all business logic out of the component layer. I set up Storybook so each team could browse and test components independently before integrating them.

*Result:* Both teams pulled from the shared library for two consecutive quarters. We cut a large amount of duplicate component code, and onboarding new engineers became noticeably faster because there was one place to look for UI building blocks.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioral questions. Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task brief (two or three sentences), spend most of your time on Action (what you specifically did, not 'we'), and close with a concrete Result. If you do not have a specific number, describe the direction of the outcome ('dropped sharply', 'improved noticeably').

Problem decomposition for design questions. When asked to design a UI feature, break it into steps: clarify requirements first, then discuss data flow (how does the component get its data?), rendering strategy (client-side, server-side, or hybrid?), error and loading states, and finally performance trade-offs. This shows you think end-to-end, not just about the happy path.

Think-aloud for live coding. Urban Harvest interviewers typically want to hear your reasoning as you code, not just see the final result. Narrate your approach as you go: 'I will start with the simplest version and then optimize.' If you hit a bug, say what you expect to happen versus what you are actually seeing. This is more valuable to the interviewer than writing perfect code in silence.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Product empathy, not just code quality. Urban Harvest builds tools that real people use under time pressure: consumers buying fresh produce, ops staff managing perishables with short shelf lives. Interviewers look for candidates who ask 'what does the user actually need here?' before jumping to implementation details.

Mobile-first instinct. A large share of Urban Harvest's users likely access the app on mid-range smartphones with variable internet connections. Candidates who naturally reach for lazy loading, image optimization, and minimal bundle size signal they understand the Indian consumer context without needing to be prompted.

Collaboration style. At a company this size, frontend engineers work closely with designers, product managers, and backend engineers on tight timelines. Candidates who talk about how they give and receive feedback, handle ambiguous requirements, and surface issues early tend to stand out over those who only talk about individual technical output.

Ownership. Candidates report that interviewers respond well to stories where you acted without being told to. If you spotted a bug and fixed it, or proposed a performance improvement on your own initiative, say so explicitly and describe the impact.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Fundamentals and company context.
Review core JavaScript concepts (closures, event loop, promises, async/await) and React patterns (hooks, context, memoization). Explore the Urban Harvest consumer app or website if it is publicly accessible. Note the UI patterns they use: how do they show product availability? How do they handle loading and out-of-stock states?

Week 2: Build something relevant.
Build a small product listing page that includes pagination or infinite scroll, image lazy loading, a basic error state, and a search or filter feature. This gives you a concrete project to reference in interviews. Push it to GitHub so you can walk through the code live if asked.

Week 3: Practice out loud.
Do 3-4 mock interviews, ideally with someone who can give honest feedback. Focus on thinking aloud during coding rounds and structuring behavioral answers with STAR. Review common React interview questions and practise explaining concepts without jargon.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping to code before clarifying requirements. In a live coding round, spending a few seconds to confirm the expected input, output, and edge cases can save you from rebuilding halfway through. Interviewers notice this kind of deliberate approach.

Talking about 'we' when describing your work. Interviewers want to know what you personally did. Replace 'we built a dashboard' with 'I built the filter component and worked with the backend engineer on the API contract.' The distinction matters more than most candidates expect.

Ignoring loading and error states. Many candidates demo the happy path and stop there. Urban Harvest's product handles real-world conditions: slow networks, out-of-stock items, and failed payments. Show that you build for these states by default, not as an afterthought.

Over-engineering answers. When asked to design a simple feature, some candidates immediately reach for micro-frontends, complex state machines, or heavy libraries. Start with the simplest working solution and add complexity only if the interviewer explicitly asks you to go further.

Not asking questions at the end. Candidates who ask thoughtful questions about the team's tech stack, current frontend challenges, or how design and engineering collaborate signal genuine interest in the role. Prepare two or three questions before you go into the interview.

How many rounds does the Urban Harvest Frontend Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates typically report 2-4 rounds in total. How should I prepare for a take-home assignment from Urban Harvest?

Focus on code clarity, sensible component structure, and handling edge cases like loading states and empty or error responses. Candidates report that interviewers pay close attention to how you handle real-world conditions, not just whether the happy path works. Include a short README explaining your decisions, especially any trade-offs you made consciously, so the reviewer understands your thinking without having to guess.
