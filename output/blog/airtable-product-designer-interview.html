01 Overview

Overview

Airtable is a no-code platform that lets teams build custom apps on top of a relational database. It sits in a unique space where the same product serves someone tracking a simple to-do list and an enterprise team running complex multi-step workflows. That range makes their design bar distinctive: they hire designers who can hold both empathy and systems thinking at once.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through a project where you simplified a complex workflow for non-technical users.

*Situation:* I was designing an internal operations tool for a logistics team that tracked vendor invoices across three separate spreadsheets and a shared email inbox. The team was non-technical and losing significant time to manual reconciliation every week.

*Task:* My goal was to consolidate the workflow into a single interface that ops coordinators could adopt without formal training.

*Action:* I started with two rounds of contextual inquiry, sitting alongside coordinators during their actual work. I mapped every step of the current flow, ran a card-sorting session to understand how they mentally grouped information, and designed a consolidated view using progressive disclosure so advanced filters were available but hidden by default. I prototyped in Figma and ran three rounds of usability testing before finalising.

*Result:* After launch, the team reported spending less time switching between tools, and error rates on invoice entries dropped based on a two-week audit the ops manager ran before and after the launch. The design pattern was later adopted by two other internal teams.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you used research or data to challenge an assumption the team held.

*Situation:* The product team at my previous company assumed users wanted a drag-and-drop calendar view as the primary way to manage tasks. The PM had already scoped the feature and the timeline.

*Task:* Before design began, I was asked to run a quick research sprint to validate the direction.

*Action:* I conducted five user interviews with our most active task-management users and reviewed session recordings from the existing list view. The interviews revealed that most users preferred keyboard-driven, list-based input because they managed many tasks at once. Drag-and-drop only resonated with a small group planning week-by-week. I brought back the findings with a clear recommendation: build the enhanced list view first and treat calendar as a secondary view.

*Result:* The team pivoted. The list view shipped first, and engagement tracked above the PM's original projections. The calendar view followed as a secondary release. The PM later said the research sprint saved the team from building something most users would have ignored.

---

Q: Describe a design that shipped and later failed. What did you learn?

*Situation:* I designed a notification system for a B2B SaaS dashboard. The goal was to surface important alerts without interrupting active workflows.

*Task:* I had to balance visibility with non-intrusiveness across an interface used by both new and experienced users.

*Action:* I chose a persistent banner at the top of every page. It looked clean in testing. I ran two moderated usability sessions before launch, both with experienced users who were already familiar with the product.

*Result:* After launch, support tickets increased. New users ignored the banner because it looked like a promotional message. Power users found it repetitive. The gap was clear: I had only tested with experienced users. I redesigned the system with a notification tray and badge count, added dismissal logic, and tested with a mixed group of new and experienced users. That version performed significantly better. The lesson I carry forward: always test with the full range of your user population before you ship.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For portfolio walkthroughs: Structure every case study around context (what was the problem and why it mattered), your specific role, the process steps you owned, the tradeoffs you made, and the outcome. Airtable interviewers typically probe the 'why' behind each decision, so prepare to go deeper than what is on your slide.

For design exercises: Candidates report that Airtable exercises often involve redesigning an existing feature or designing for a new persona. Use a 'discover, define, decide' structure: show how you would research the problem space, narrow to a clear problem statement, and then propose one well-reasoned solution rather than several shallow options. Depth of reasoning matters more than breadth of ideas.

For behavioral questions: Use STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) but briefly front-load the result before telling the full story. For example: 'We ended up changing the direction entirely, and here is why.' This gives the interviewer a mental anchor and signals confidence in your decisions.

For 'how would you redesign X' questions: Resist jumping to solutions. Spend the first few minutes asking clarifying questions about the user, the business goal, and the constraints. Interviewers want to see structured thinking before they see any solution.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Airtable interviewers typically look for four qualities.

Systems thinking. Airtable's product is deeply relational. A design change in one view can affect the mental model in another. They want to see that you consider second-order effects of your decisions, not just the immediate screen in front of you.

Empathy for non-technical users. A large part of Airtable's growth comes from people who are not engineers using the product confidently. Designers who default to technical assumptions or design for high user sophistication are a common red flag.

Collaboration and craft. Candidates report that interviewers ask specifically about how you work with engineers and PMs, not just what you shipped. Be ready to talk about handoff practices, moments of negotiation, and times you adapted a design for implementation constraints.

Self-awareness. The design failure question is not a trap. Interviewers want to see that you can name what went wrong and articulate what you would do differently. Candidates who present only wins typically score lower on this dimension.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

  1. Use Airtable actively before the interview. Build a real base, try automations, explore different views. You cannot discuss the product convincingly without firsthand experience.
  2. Prepare three case studies. Each should cover a distinct challenge: one around simplifying for non-technical users, one where research changed a decision, and one where something did not go as planned.
  3. Study the product critically. Note where Airtable makes tradeoffs between simplicity and power. Be ready to discuss those tradeoffs with a clear point of view.
  4. Practice talking through a design problem out loud. Candidates report that some Airtable rounds are live or whiteboard sessions. Practice narrating your thinking for an extended session without slides.
  5. Review your handoff process. Be specific about how you use Figma, what you include in your specs, and how you collaborate with engineers during build and QA.
  6. Follow Airtable's product updates. Read their blog and changelog to understand what they have shipped recently and where the product is heading.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Showing screens without explaining decisions. Presenting a polished portfolio without explaining the reasoning behind each choice signals that you cannot reproduce the thinking. Interviewers will probe until they find the gap.

Jumping to solutions in design exercises. Many candidates skip the problem definition phase entirely. Airtable interviewers want to see structured thinking before they see a solution.

Using design buzzwords without substance. Phrases like 'design system thinking' or 'user-centered approach' mean nothing without a specific example. Replace every buzzword with a concrete story.

Pivoting away from the failure question. Some candidates reframe a genuine failure as a 'challenge we overcame.' Interviewers notice the pivot, and it signals low self-awareness. Name the failure directly and explain what you changed.

Not preparing questions for the interviewer. Candidates report that Airtable interviewers leave meaningful time for your questions. Thoughtful questions about the team's roadmap, design process, or biggest unsolved problems signal genuine interest.

Only presenting solo work. Airtable design is deeply collaborative. If all your case studies feature you working alone, interviewers will wonder how you operate in a cross-functional team.

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Airtable Product Designer interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically includes a recruiter screen, a portfolio review with the hiring manager, a design exercise (take-home or live), and a final panel with cross-functional stakeholders. Some candidates report an additional design critique round in the loop. The total timeline varies, so check in with your recruiter after each stage to understand what comes next.

Does Airtable give a take-home design exercise?

Candidates report that Airtable does include a design exercise, though the format varies. Some describe a take-home prompt with a few days to complete it; others describe a live session with a prompt given at the start of the call. Either way, the exercise typically involves redesigning an existing Airtable feature or designing for a new user scenario.

What salary can I expect as a Product Designer at Airtable in India?

Based on industry surveys and publicly reported Glassdoor data, Product Designer salaries in India range from 6-12 LPA at entry level (0-2 years) to 14-24 LPA at mid level (3-5 years), 26-40 LPA at senior level (6-9 years), and 36-55+ LPA at Lead or Principal level. Actual offers depend on your experience band, location, and the equity component in the package.

How important is Figma proficiency for the Airtable interview?

Figma proficiency helps, but Airtable primarily evaluates your thinking process, not the visual quality of your output. Candidates report being assessed on how clearly they communicate design decisions and tradeoffs during the exercise. Being fluent in Figma means you can focus on reasoning rather than tooling, especially in live or whiteboard sessions.

How should I prepare my portfolio for the Airtable interview?

Prioritise depth over breadth. Choose two or three projects where you can walk through the full process: the research, the decisions, the tradeoffs, and the outcome. Airtable interviewers typically probe the 'why' behind each design choice, so prepare to go well beyond what is on the slide. Include at least one project where something did not go as planned.
