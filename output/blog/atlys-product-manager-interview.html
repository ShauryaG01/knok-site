01 Overview

Overview

Atlys is a Bangalore-based travel-tech startup that digitises visa applications, letting travellers apply through a consumer app in minutes instead of days. The product spans consumer UX, government API integrations, payment flows, and compliance, which makes the PM role unusually broad and the interview unusually specific.

Candidates typically go through a multi-stage process that includes an initial screening, a product sense or case study discussion, and one or more rounds with senior stakeholders. The case study component, candidates report, is usually grounded in a real Atlys scenario rather than a generic prompt.

Preparation should centre on three things: the Atlys app itself (download it and go through a visa application), their publicly announced market expansions from 2024-2026, and the friction points that first-time international travellers commonly face. PM compensation at Atlys is not separately published, but industry surveys suggest mid-level PM roles at comparable growth-stage startups in Bangalore commonly align with the 24-40 LPA band, and senior roles with 40-60 LPA.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly in Atlys PM interviews, based on what candidates publicly report.

  1. Walk me through the Atlys product. What works well and what would you change first?
  2. How would you measure the success of a new visa-on-approval feature?
  3. A government API that powers a large share of our visa applications goes down. Walk me through how you handle this.
  4. Design a feature that helps first-time international travellers feel confident completing their Atlys application.
  5. How would you prioritise between improving document upload accuracy and adding a new visa corridor?
  6. You have conflicting inputs from the compliance team and the design team on a new feature. How do you resolve it?
  7. Atlys wants to expand to a new country. How do you decide which one to enter first?
  8. A key metric, say application completion rate, drops sharply week-over-week. Walk me through your diagnosis.
  9. How would you build a referral or word-of-mouth loop for Atlys given that visa applications are infrequent events?
  10. Describe a product you shipped end to end. What would you do differently?
  11. How do you work with a sales or BD team to align product priorities around enterprise or B2B use cases?
  12. What is the biggest product risk Atlys faces in the coming year, and how would you address it?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: A key metric drops sharply week-over-week. Walk me through your diagnosis.

*Situation:* At my previous company, our checkout completion rate dropped noticeably over a single week and no one had flagged it until finance raised the revenue shortfall.

*Task:* I needed to diagnose the root cause quickly, communicate to leadership, and propose a fix, all without creating panic.

*Action:* I segmented the drop by platform (Android vs iOS vs web), by country, and by product flow to isolate where it was concentrated. I found it was almost entirely on one Android version that had shipped a UI update the week before. I pulled session recordings and saw users were confused by a new document upload screen. I looped in engineering and design for an emergency patch and set up a daily metric review until we recovered.

*Result:* The patch shipped within days and completion rate returned to baseline. I also put in place an automated alert so a drop of that kind would page the on-call PM rather than wait for finance to notice.

---

Q: Design a feature that helps first-time international travellers feel confident using Atlys.

*Situation:* During a product review at a past role, we found that first-time users abandoned the application flow at a much higher rate than repeat users because they were uncertain whether they were filling in details correctly.

*Task:* I was asked to propose and scope a 'confidence layer' for first-time users without adding friction for returning users.

*Action:* I proposed an inline progress validator that checks each document field in real time (passport number format, photo size, expiry date) and gives a green tick before the user moves on. I paired it with a 'what happens next' timeline so users could see exactly when to expect a visa decision. I ran a lightweight usability test with a small group of first-time travellers before writing the PRD, then worked with engineering to ship a phased rollout.

*Result:* The rollout cohort showed a meaningful improvement in document-screen completion. Leadership used the data to justify expanding the feature to all new users in the following quarter.

---

Q: How would you prioritise between improving document upload accuracy and adding a new visa corridor?

*Situation:* At a startup I worked at, the BD team kept pushing for new market expansion while the ops team flagged high rejection rates caused by bad document uploads.

*Task:* I needed to make a defensible prioritisation call with limited engineering bandwidth.

*Action:* I built a simple impact-effort matrix and layered in business context. I looked at the ops cost per rejection: support time, re-upload cycles, and customer refunds. I then modelled the revenue potential from the new corridor using publicly reported traveller volumes for that route. Improving upload accuracy had a higher near-term ROI because each rejection cost us both a refund and the customer relationship. I proposed a short sprint to fix upload accuracy first, with the new corridor kicking off in parallel on the BD side so engineering could begin right after.

*Result:* The fix reduced ops escalations noticeably and gave us a cleaner foundation before we launched the new corridor.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the baseline for behavioural questions. Keep each component tight: one sentence for situation, one for task, two or three for action, and one clear result with a metric if you have one.

The Metric Tree works well for 'a metric dropped' questions. Start at the top-level metric, break it into component rates (impressions, clicks, completions, conversions), then segment each by device, geography, user cohort, and time. Atlys interviewers appreciate this structured breakdown because their funnel spans multiple countries and platforms.

Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD) fits feature design questions. Frame the user need as 'when I am [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome].' For Atlys, a strong JTBD framing would be: 'when I am applying for my first visa, I want to know my documents are correct before I submit, so I can avoid rejection and not lose my travel deposit.'

RICE or weighted scoring is useful when asked to prioritise. Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort. Show your reasoning even if the numbers are rough estimates, because Atlys interviewers care more about your thought process than the exact output.

Regulatory constraint framing is specific to Atlys. Whenever you propose a feature, anticipate the compliance angle: what government data is involved, what consent or audit trail is needed, and what happens if the API or rule changes. Candidates who consistently miss this step stand out negatively in debrief.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Deep product empathy, not generic answers. Atlys interviewers have heard 'improve the onboarding flow' many times. They want you to have used the app, noticed a specific friction point (the photo guidelines screen, for example), and to have a concrete hypothesis about why it exists and how to fix it.

Comfort with regulatory and ops complexity. Atlys is not a pure consumer app. Visa rules change, government portals go down, and embassies have processing windows. Interviewers look for candidates who treat compliance as a product constraint to engineer around, not a blocker to hand off to legal.

Structured thinking under pressure. The case study rounds are often ambiguous by design. Interviewers want to see you ask one clarifying question, state your assumption clearly, and then move forward rather than waiting for perfect information.

Cross-functional leadership. PM roles at Atlys are lean, meaning you will work directly with ops, compliance, customer support, and growth teams. Give examples where you aligned people with different incentives, not just examples where you wrote a PRD and engineering built it.

Ownership and a bias to ship. Atlys moves fast. Interviewers value candidates who can release a v1, learn from it, and iterate, rather than candidates who want to perfect a spec before releasing anything.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know the product.
Download the Atlys app and go through at least one visa application end to end, even if you do not complete a real submission. Note every screen where you felt uncertain or slowed down. Read Atlys's public blog, LinkedIn posts, and press from 2024-2026 on new markets or features they have launched. Build a list of friction points and things that work well, with a one-line hypothesis for each.

Week 2: Practice core question types.
Practice at least one metric-drop question daily using the Metric Tree framework. Write out two or three STAR stories specific to cross-functional work, regulatory constraints, or ambiguous data situations, since these come up most often at Atlys. Time yourself and aim for a tight three-minute answer per story.

Week 3: Case study prep.
Practice end-to-end product case studies using real Atlys scenarios: 'how would you improve document upload accuracy?' or 'how would you decide which country to expand to next?' Write your full approach including what data you would look at, what you would build, and how you would measure success. Get a peer to give you feedback on whether your compliance thinking comes through clearly.

Before the interview.
Review Atlys's publicly announced milestones and metrics (check their LinkedIn and press releases). Prepare two questions for the interviewer that show genuine research, not generic questions like 'what does success look like.' Have your STAR stories ready for: a product you shipped, a stakeholder conflict you resolved, and a data-driven decision you made.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

  1. Treating Atlys like a generic consumer app. Candidates who pitch features without acknowledging the compliance, government API, or embassy-side constraints come across as naive. Always ask yourself: what is the regulatory angle here?
  1. Being vague about metrics. Saying 'engagement improved' is not enough. Even if you lack exact numbers, frame your result as 'we saw a meaningful lift in application completion rate, which we tracked as the primary success metric for that sprint.'
  1. Not using the Atlys app before the interview. This is basic and yet many candidates skip it. Interviewers notice immediately when someone has not used the product.
  1. Over-indexing on features, under-indexing on outcomes. Atlys interviewers are more impressed by a candidate who explains why a feature matters (user outcome, business metric) than by a candidate who rattles off a long feature list.
  1. Ignoring ops and support teams. Atlys's product loop includes customer support for visa rejections and embassy escalations. Candidates who only think about the app and ignore downstream ops give an incomplete picture.
  1. Giving textbook frameworks without substance. RICE and JTBD are well known. If you use them, fill them with real numbers or real Atlys context. An empty framework answer ('I would score each by reach, impact, confidence, and effort') adds no signal for the interviewer.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Atlys PM interview typically have?

Candidates publicly report a process that spans several rounds, typically including an initial screening, a product sense or case study discussion, and one or more rounds with senior stakeholders. The exact structure varies by level and team, so ask your recruiter for the specific format once you have a confirmed slot. Atlys has 29 open roles across functions as of July 2026, and the process may differ slightly by team.

What salary can I expect for a PM role at Atlys?

Atlys does not publish salary bands publicly. Based on Glassdoor data and industry surveys for comparable growth-stage startups in Bangalore, PM compensation commonly falls in the 24-40 LPA range for mid-level roles (3-6 years experience) and 40-60 LPA for senior roles. Your actual offer will depend on your level, the specific team, and your negotiation. Cross-check with levels.fyi for recent data points before your offer discussion.

Does Atlys give a take-home assignment?

Candidates report that Atlys typically includes a case study component, which may be a take-home assignment or a live case study during the interview depending on the role and seniority. The case study is usually grounded in a real Atlys scenario rather than a generic product design prompt. Treat any take-home seriously: a well-structured, Atlys-specific answer is far more valuable than a polished but generic response.

How important is travel industry experience for an Atlys PM role?

Direct travel industry experience is helpful but not required. What matters more is your ability to understand the specific constraints of Atlys's product: government integrations, compliance requirements, and a user base that is often anxious and time-sensitive. Candidates from fintech, marketplace, or consumer app backgrounds commonly do well if they can clearly map their past experience to the Atlys context during the interview.

What should I know about Atlys's product before the interview?

Download the app and go through a real visa application, even partially. Know the key markets Atlys currently covers, any recently launched features (check their LinkedIn and press from 2024-2026), and the core value proposition: applying for a visa from your phone in minutes. Be ready to discuss a specific part of the product you would improve and why, backed by a user need rather than a feature idea alone.

How can I stand out in an Atlys PM interview compared to other candidates?

Candidates who stand out typically do three things: they have clearly used the product before the interview, they treat regulatory complexity as a product design problem rather than someone else's concern, and they quantify their past impact even when exact numbers are not available. Showing that you understand the difference between a pure consumer app and a compliance-heavy consumer app is the single biggest differentiator, based on what candidates and interviewers publicly share.
