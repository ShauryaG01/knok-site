02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up frequently in Zilo UI/UX Designer interviews, based on what candidates typically report for product-company design roles:

  1. Walk us through a project from your portfolio that you are most proud of.
  2. How do you approach a design problem when you have very little user research to start with?
  3. Describe a time you disagreed with a product manager or developer about a design decision. How did you handle it?
  4. How do you decide when a design is ready to ship?
  5. What design and prototyping tools do you use, and why do you prefer them?
  6. Tell us about a project where you had to simplify a complex user flow.

  9. How do you handle engineering constraints that force you to change your original design?
  10. Walk us through your user research process when you are working against a tight deadline.
  11. How do you maintain design consistency across a product when multiple teams are contributing?
  12. What does good design mean to you, and how do you measure whether you have achieved it?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through a project from your portfolio that you are most proud of.

*Situation:* At my previous company, the mobile onboarding flow had a high drop-off problem. Users were leaving before completing registration, and the team did not have a clear picture of why.

*Task:* I was asked to redesign the onboarding experience from scratch, with the goal of getting more users to the core value of the product faster.

*Action:* I started with a round of user interviews to understand where friction was happening. I mapped the existing flow, identified the main drop-off points, and built prototype variations. I ran quick usability tests with a small group, gathered feedback, and iterated before handing designs off to engineering with a detailed spec.

*Result:* The redesign shipped within the next product cycle. The team saw a clear positive trend in onboarding completion after launch, and the product lead used the project as a template for future redesigns. The biggest personal win was learning to tie every design decision back to a user or business goal.

---

Q: Describe a time you disagreed with a product manager about a design decision.

*Situation:* A product manager wanted to add a promotional banner on the home screen of an app I was designing. The business case was clear, but I felt it would push the primary action users came for below the fold.

*Task:* My job was to advocate for the user experience while still meeting the business goal.

*Action:* Instead of simply saying no, I set up quick usability sessions with a small group of existing users. I showed both layouts (with and without the banner) and documented their reactions. I then presented the findings to the PM along with an alternative layout that gave the banner space without burying the core action.

*Result:* The PM agreed to test my alternative layout. The product team ran an A/B test and the revised layout performed better on the key engagement metric. It was a good reminder that data is more persuasive than opinion in a room full of stakeholders.

---

Q: How do you handle engineering constraints that force you to change your original design?

*Situation:* I had designed an animated transition for a mobile feature that I felt made the experience feel polished and intuitive. The engineering team flagged that the animation would require significant extra effort and could delay the launch.

*Task:* I needed to find a solution that preserved the intent of the design without blocking the release.

*Action:* I set up a short call with the lead developer to understand exactly what was complex about the animation. Based on that conversation, I redesigned the transition using a simpler approach that achieved a similar feel at a fraction of the implementation cost. I also documented the original vision so it could be revisited in a future sprint.

*Result:* The feature launched on time with the simplified transition. The developer appreciated being involved in the problem-solving early, and I adopted the habit of doing a quick feasibility check with engineering before finalising any motion design.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use the STAR format for behavioural questions. Every story should have a clear Situation (the context), Task (your specific role), Action (what you personally did), and Result (the outcome, tied to user or business impact where possible). Keep each verbal answer focused and under three minutes.

For design process questions, use a 'Discovery to Delivery' frame. Start with how you understand the problem (research, stakeholder input, data), move to how you explore solutions (sketches, wireframes, prototypes), and end with how you validate and hand off (testing, specs, iteration post-launch). This shows end-to-end thinking, not just visual output.

For 'how do you decide' questions, show your reasoning. Interviewers at product companies want to hear that you weigh user needs, business goals, and technical constraints together. Name the trade-offs you considered, not just the decision you made.

For portfolio walkthroughs, lead with the problem, not the visuals. Open with: 'The problem users were facing was...' before showing any screen. This signals immediately that you are a problem-solver, not just a visual stylist.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Design thinking over design aesthetics. At a product company like Zilo, interviewers are looking for candidates who can articulate why they made a design decision, not just what the final design looks like. If your portfolio walkthrough focuses only on visuals, you will likely be asked to go deeper.

Collaboration and communication skills. UI/UX Designers at product companies work closely with PMs, engineers, and business stakeholders. Interviewers want evidence that you can give and receive feedback constructively, push back with data, and adapt without losing sight of the user.

User empathy grounded in research. 'I thought users would prefer this' is a weak answer. 'I ran user interviews and found that...' is a strong one. Even lightweight research methods (quick usability tests, guerrilla sessions) show that your design decisions are rooted in actual user behaviour.

Ownership and follow-through. Candidates who can describe what happened after a design shipped (metrics, user feedback, iterations) stand out from those who only talk about the process up to handoff.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Portfolio polish. Pick three to four projects that show range: ideally one research-heavy project, one where you navigated constraints, and one where you can point to a measurable outcome. For each project, prepare a short story using the Discovery to Delivery frame described above.

Week 2: Tools and process review. Brush up on Figma (components, auto-layout, prototyping), and be ready to talk about when you use wireframes versus high-fidelity mockups. If you have used tools like Maze, Hotjar, or Mixpanel for validation, prepare a specific example for each.

Week 3: Research and practice. Look at Zilo's product (if it is publicly available) and identify one or two UX improvements you would propose. This shows genuine interest and gives you a concrete talking point. Practice answering the questions above out loud, ideally recording yourself so you can review your pace and clarity.

Before the interview. Prepare a few questions to ask the interviewer: what does the design team's sprint process look like, how does design work with engineering on handoff, and what does a successful first few months look like for this role.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Showing the 'what' without the 'why.' The most common mistake in a portfolio walkthrough is jumping straight to screens. Always start with the problem you were solving and the user you were designing for.

Claiming sole credit for team work. Interviewers notice when a candidate says 'I did' for everything. It is fine to say 'I led the research' or 'I was responsible for the interaction design' while acknowledging the broader team. Overclaiming raises a flag about how you will collaborate.

Vague answers to process questions. Saying 'I always start with user research' without a specific example sounds hollow. Ground every process answer in a real project from your experience.

Not asking about the design team's maturity. Candidates sometimes accept a role without understanding whether design has a real seat at the table. Asking how design decisions get made and who has final say signals that you take the craft seriously.

Ignoring the design task brief. If Zilo gives you a take-home or whiteboard task, read the brief carefully before you start. Candidates commonly lose points not because of poor design skills, but because they solved a different problem than the one asked.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many interview rounds does Zilo typically have for a UI/UX Designer role?

Candidates report a process that typically involves two to three rounds: a screening call with HR or a recruiter, a portfolio presentation with the design or product team, and a design challenge (either take-home or live). The exact number of rounds can vary depending on the seniority of the role and the hiring team's current process. Always confirm the format with your recruiter at the start of the process.

Is there a design assignment in the Zilo UI/UX interview process?

Candidates commonly report some form of design task, either a take-home assignment or a live whiteboard session. Take-home tasks typically ask you to redesign a specific flow or solve a product problem from scratch. Treat the brief carefully: define the problem clearly, state your assumptions, and walk through your thinking as well as your output.

What tools should I know before interviewing at Zilo?

Figma is the industry-standard tool for most product companies and you should be comfortable with components, auto-layout, and interactive prototyping. Familiarity with user research and testing tools (such as Maze or Lookback) is a plus but rarely a hard requirement. Be ready to explain your tool choices: interviewers care more about your reasoning than the specific app you use.

Does Zilo focus more on visual design skills or UX research in the interview?

Based on what candidates report for product-company design roles, the balance tends to lean toward UX thinking and problem-solving over pixel-perfect visuals. You should be able to show strong craft through your portfolio, but the questions you face in the interview are more likely to probe your process, your collaboration style, and how you handle constraints. Both skills matter and neither alone is enough.

How should I present my portfolio in a Zilo interview?

Pick three to four projects and structure each as a short story: the user problem, your role, the process you followed, and the result. Start every walkthrough with the problem, not the visuals. If possible, include one project where you can point to a concrete outcome such as improved task completion or positive user feedback after launch. Keep each case study brief so there is time for the interviewer to ask follow-up questions.
