03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Structure every answer with STAR: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep each story to three minutes or less when spoken aloud. Own your actions with 'I', not 'we'.

---

Q: How do you manage competing priorities when your team is stretched across multiple launches?

*Situation:* At my previous company, two major product initiatives were both scheduled to ship in the same quarter. One was a direct customer commitment and the other was tied to a partner go-to-market window. Neither could be dropped.

*Task:* I had a team of eight engineers and needed to deliver both without slipping timelines or burning people out.

*Action:* I mapped out all dependencies, split the team into two focused squads with clear owners for each initiative, and identified the shared infrastructure work both streams needed. I set up a weekly cross-squad sync specifically to surface blockers early. I also negotiated with the product manager to push one non-critical feature to the following sprint, which freed up two engineers for the higher-risk workstream.

*Result:* Both initiatives shipped on time. The team reported feeling less stressed because clear ownership reduced context switching. The PM adopted the squad model for the next quarter.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you pushed back on a product requirement because it was technically risky.

*Situation:* A product manager proposed adding real-time personalisation to our checkout flow, estimating it would take a week based on what looked like a simple UI change.

*Task:* My team owned the backend, and I could see the requirement would touch three core services with no clean rollback path if something went wrong in production.

*Action:* I wrote a short technical risk doc, walked the PM through the dependency chain, and proposed a two-phase approach: ship a simpler version in week one behind a feature flag, then release the full personalisation in week three after a staged rollout. I brought in my tech lead to co-present the risks so the PM heard the concerns directly from the engineer closest to the code.

*Result:* The PM agreed to the phased plan. During week two we found a data consistency bug in staging. Because of the feature flag, we fixed it without delaying the initial release. The PM later said the two-phase approach became the team's default for risky features.

---

Q: Walk us through a time you grew an engineer from mid-level to senior.

*Situation:* I had a strong mid-level engineer who was technically solid but consistently avoided leading design discussions and deflected ownership of ambiguous problems.

*Task:* I wanted to help them build the ownership and communication habits needed for a senior promotion, without setting them up to fail publicly.

*Action:* I started by giving them a small, well-scoped project to own end-to-end, including writing the design doc and presenting it to the team. I reviewed the doc with detailed feedback before it went public. Over the following two quarters I gradually increased the ambiguity and scope of their projects, introduced them to cross-functional stakeholders, and made them the go-to person for one technical domain. I also paired them with a senior engineer on two projects so they could observe ownership in action.

*Result:* They were promoted to senior engineer within a year. In their promotion feedback, three colleagues specifically called out their improved ability to take ownership of unclear requirements and drive decisions independently.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Two frameworks cover most EM interview questions at companies like Skai.

STAR for behavioral questions (Situation, Task, Action, Result): Every story needs a clear before-state, a specific challenge you personally owned, concrete actions you took (say 'I', not 'we'), and a measurable or observable result. Probe-heavy interviewers will follow up if your actions are vague, so be specific about what you personally decided or did.

The Manager's Triangle for leadership philosophy questions: When asked how you operate as a manager, structure your answer across three dimensions: team health (how you build and retain people), technical quality (how you maintain standards without micromanaging), and delivery (how you ship reliably). Strong EM candidates show all three in balance, not just one.

For technical judgment questions (architecture, reliability, scalability): Use a 'constraints first' approach. Name the constraints that matter most in the specific context (latency, cost, fault tolerance, team skill), then walk through the trade-offs. This signals senior-level thinking rather than jumping straight to a solution.

For cross-functional conflict questions: Lead with the shared business outcome both sides were trying to reach, then explain how you bridged the gap. Interviewers want to see that you collaborate and build alignment, not that you win arguments.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Skai's systems power advertising for large enterprise clients, where errors translate directly into client revenue loss. Interviewers look for a few specific signals in EM candidates.

Ownership without ego. They want managers who take full accountability for outcomes, including failures, and who build that same accountability in their teams. Stories where you attribute failure to external teams or circumstances without a clear personal learning will hurt your candidacy.

Technical credibility. You do not need to write production code daily, but you need to catch architectural risks in design reviews, discuss trade-offs with strong senior engineers, and earn their respect. Prepare to talk about a technically complex problem your team solved and your specific role in shaping the approach.

Customer awareness. Skai is a B2B platform with enterprise clients who have strict SLA expectations. Candidates who connect engineering decisions to client impact and reliability goals tend to resonate more than those who focus purely on internal metrics.

Structured thinking under pressure. Expect scenario-based questions with incomplete information, such as a production outage, a conflicted team, or a late project. Think out loud, state your assumptions clearly, and show your reasoning process before landing on a decision.

People development track record. Concrete examples of engineers you have promoted, junior talent you have mentored, or teams you have scaled carry significant weight at this level.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused two to three week plan covers most of what you need.

Understand Skai's product and engineering context. Read their engineering blog, review recent product announcements, and understand how commerce media platforms work technically. Know the difference between retail media, paid search, and social advertising at a conceptual level. Scan their open roles to understand the tech stack and domains the engineering teams currently work in.

Build your story bank. Write out eight to ten STAR stories covering: a delivery win under pressure, a team conflict you resolved, a technical decision you owned, an engineer you developed, a time you failed and what you learned, a cross-functional challenge, and a time you improved a process. Practice saying each story out loud until it runs to three minutes or less.

Do mock interviews and develop your point of view. Run at least two mock behavioral interviews with someone who will give you honest feedback. Prepare two or three strong opinions on engineering management topics such as technical debt strategy, on-call culture, or your hiring bar. Strong interviewers want to see that you have a perspective, not just that you can answer questions correctly.

Prepare sharp questions for each interviewer. Questions about the specific technical challenges the team is currently facing, or how engineering and product collaborate on roadmap, tend to land well. Avoid generic questions that any company could answer.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

These are the patterns that most often cost EM candidates an offer at product-led companies.

Telling 'we' stories instead of 'I' stories. Saying 'we built' or 'we decided' makes it impossible for the interviewer to assess your specific contribution. Own your actions and decisions explicitly.

Skipping the result. Many candidates tell a detailed situation and action but trail off without a clear outcome. Always close the loop: what changed, what was the measurable impact, what did you or the team learn?

Focusing only on technical output. Interviewers at the EM level care as much about how you grew your people and built team culture as about what shipped. If all your stories are about systems and none are about developing humans, that is a visible gap.

Being vague about conflict. When asked about disagreements or difficult situations, candidates often sanitise the story until no real tension is visible. Name the actual conflict clearly, then explain how you resolved it. Interviewers can tell when a story has been smoothed over.

Under-preparing questions for the interviewer. Generic questions signal low interest. Specific, informed questions signal that you have done your homework and are genuinely evaluating the role.

Dropping Skai product names without substance. Mentioning retail media or commerce media without being able to explain what makes them technically interesting can backfire. If you reference their product domain, be ready to go one level deeper.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Skai Engineering Manager interview typically have?

Candidates report a process that typically includes a recruiter screen, a hiring manager conversation, and then two to four deeper rounds covering behavioral leadership, technical judgment, and sometimes a cross-functional panel. The exact structure varies by team and level. Confirm the format with your recruiter at the start so you can prepare each round appropriately.

Does Skai ask coding questions in the Engineering Manager interview?

At the EM level, candidates typically do not go through competitive coding rounds at companies like Skai. The technical component is usually a system design or architecture discussion where you show you can reason through trade-offs and guide a team technically. That said, the process can vary by team, so confirm with your recruiter before you begin preparing.

What salary can I expect for an Engineering Manager role at Skai in India?

Based on knok jobradar data, Engineering Manager roles in India range from 35-60 LPA, Senior Manager roles from 55-90 LPA, and Director-level roles at 90-150+ LPA. Actual offers vary based on your experience, years in leadership, and the team's budget. Glassdoor and levels.fyi can give you additional data points to calibrate your expectations and anchor your negotiation.

How important is ad tech or martech domain experience for this role?

Domain experience in ad tech is helpful but candidates report it is not always a hard requirement. What matters more is your track record leading teams that build high-scale, data-intensive systems and your experience with enterprise or B2B products. If you are coming from a different domain, show that you have researched Skai's business model and can connect your past experience to the challenges they face.

What is the best way to prepare for Skai's behavioral interview rounds?

Build a story bank of eight to ten specific STAR stories before you start interviewing. Cover a range of situations: delivery under pressure, a team conflict you resolved, an engineer you developed, a technical decision you owned, and a failure with clear learnings. Practise out loud until each story lands in three minutes or less. Generic or overly polished answers rarely generate the kind of back-and-forth that leads to strong interviewer feedback.
