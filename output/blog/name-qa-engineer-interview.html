What the interview process looks like

Candidates report that interviews typically run across multiple rounds: an initial HR call, one or two technical rounds covering testing concepts and automation, a practical exercise such as writing test cases or scripts, and a final round with a manager or team lead. Senior roles commonly add a test architecture or system design discussion. The exact format varies by company, so confirm the structure with your recruiter before you start preparing.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Testing fundamentals

  1. What is the difference between verification and validation? Give an example from a project you worked on.
  2. Walk me through the software testing life cycle. How does your team hand off between QA and development?
  3. What is the difference between smoke testing, sanity testing, and regression testing? When would you use each?
  4. How do you decide what to automate versus what to keep as manual testing?
  5. Explain boundary value analysis and equivalence partitioning with a real example from your work.

Automation and tooling

  1. Which test automation framework have you worked with most? Why did your team choose it over alternatives?
  2. How do you handle flaky or unreliable tests in your automation suite? Walk through a specific case.
  3. How do you approach API testing, and which tools have you used for it?

Behavioural and process

  1. Tell me about a critical bug you found that had real business impact. How did you handle it?
  2. How do you prioritize test cases when the release deadline is tight and you cannot cover everything?
  3. Describe a situation where a developer disagreed that something you raised was a bug.
  4. How do you keep test cases current when the product is changing rapidly?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a critical bug you found that had real business impact.

*Situation:* I was testing a payments module for an e-commerce platform two weeks before a major seasonal sale.
*Task:* My responsibility was end-to-end validation of the checkout flow, including edge cases around coupon codes and wallet balances.
*Action:* During boundary value testing, I applied a coupon that brought the order total to exactly zero. The system processed the order, deducted inventory, and sent a confirmation email, but skipped the payment capture step entirely. I documented the defect with precise reproduction steps, a screen recording, and the database state before and after the transaction. I flagged it as critical and escalated to the tech lead that evening rather than waiting for the next standup.
*Result:* The fix was deployed within a day. The team estimated the issue could have caused substantial losses during the sale period. The defect also led to a permanent 'zero-value order' test case being added to the regression suite.

---

Q: How do you prioritize test cases when time is limited?

*Situation:* At a previous company, a client demo moved up the release window by two days mid-sprint.
*Task:* I had to compress the planned test cycle without creating blind spots in coverage.
*Action:* I sorted test cases by two factors: business risk (what breaks if this fails for real users?) and change impact (which areas of code were actually modified in this sprint?). High-risk, high-change areas went first. Stable legacy flows with no recent changes were deferred. I logged every deferred case explicitly so the entire team knew what was not covered. I also automated the three most repetitive regression checks that week to recover time in future cycles.
*Result:* We delivered on schedule, the demo went smoothly, and the deferred areas were covered in the following sprint with no production issues reported.

---

Q: Describe a time a developer disagreed that something you raised was a bug.

*Situation:* I raised a defect where a date picker allowed users to select past dates on a future-booking form. The developer marked it 'not a bug' because the requirement did not explicitly restrict past dates.
*Task:* I needed to resolve this without creating conflict while making sure the right call was made for users.
*Action:* Rather than arguing, I went back to the user story. The acceptance criteria included the phrase 'valid future booking date.' I also checked three competitor apps and found all of them blocked past date selection. I scheduled a short three-way call with the developer and the product owner to present both data points together.
*Result:* The product owner confirmed it was a defect and the fix went into the same sprint. Afterward, the developer and I agreed to write more explicit acceptance criteria on all date-related stories going forward.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For 'explain a concept' questions

Use Define-Example-Impact. Define the concept in one sentence, give a concrete example from your own work, then explain why it matters in practice. This keeps answers focused and shows you can apply theory rather than just recite it.

For behavioural questions

Use STAR: Situation (brief context), Task (your specific responsibility), Action (exactly what you did, using 'I' not 'we'), Result (a concrete, observable outcome). Candidates who stay at the 'we did this' level make it impossible for interviewers to assess individual contribution. Own your specific actions and mention the team only as context.

For 'what would you do' scenario questions

Think aloud. State your first assumption, describe your approach step by step, and name where you would ask for clarification or escalate. Interviewers are evaluating your thought process, not just the final answer. Saying 'I would first confirm X before deciding Y' scores better than a confident but incomplete answer.

For automation architecture questions

Structure your answer around three layers: what you test (scope and coverage strategy), how you test it (framework and tooling rationale), and how you maintain it (folder structure, tagging, CI/CD integration). Candidates who list only tool names without discussing maintainability typically score lower on senior-level panels.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Precision over volume

QA interviewers care far more about the quality of your reasoning than the number of tools you can name. A candidate who explains exactly how they diagnosed a flaky test, step by step, will outscore someone who lists ten automation tools but cannot describe a specific debugging approach.

Risk communication in plain language

Teams want QA engineers who can explain risk to a product manager or business owner without using technical jargon. Expect at least one question about how you report defect status or test coverage gaps to non-technical stakeholders. Practise explaining a complex bug in two plain sentences before your interview.

Ownership beyond test execution

More companies now expect QA engineers to be involved early, in requirement reviews and design discussions, not just at the end of a sprint. Candidates who give examples of catching ambiguity in requirements before any code was written tend to stand out, particularly at product-focused companies.

Automation that runs reliably in production pipelines

For mid-level and senior roles, interviewers want evidence that your automation suite is actively maintained and runs in CI/CD. Be ready to describe your pipeline setup, how often tests execute, and what your team does when the suite breaks unexpectedly.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Foundations and honest gap analysis

List your weak areas before you study anything. Most candidates are strong in either manual testing or automation, but not both. Spend this week on testing fundamentals (STLC, test design techniques, defect life cycle) and SQL basics, since database queries come up frequently across all experience levels.

Week 2: Automation and tooling depth

Pick the framework you know best and build a small end-to-end test suite for a publicly accessible website. Practise explaining your design choices aloud. Know why you chose your tool over an alternative for that specific context. Review how to connect your tests to a CI pipeline using GitHub Actions or a similar tool.

Week 3: Behavioural stories and mock practice

Write STAR stories for five scenarios: a critical bug you caught, a disagreement with a developer, a tight deadline, a defect you missed that reached production, and a process improvement you drove. Practise saying these aloud rather than reading them. Do at least one mock interview with a peer or mentor before the real thing.

Day before the interview

Re-read the job description and map your prepared stories to the skills listed. Write two genuine questions to ask the interviewer about the team's current testing challenges or tooling decisions.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating testing and quality assurance as the same thing

Many candidates describe their role purely as executing test cases. Interviewers at mature engineering teams probe whether you see quality assurance as a broader responsibility that includes preventing defects upstream, not just finding them at the end.

Listing tools without any depth

Saying 'I know Selenium, Cypress, Postman, JIRA, and TestNG' without being able to explain a specific implementation or debugging story makes the answer forgettable. Know two tools deeply and be ready to explain trade-offs between them.

Using 'we' throughout every behavioural answer

This is one of the most common patterns that costs candidates offers. If every sentence starts with 'we did' or 'our team handled,' the interviewer cannot assess your individual contribution. Use 'I' for your own actions and mention the team only as background context.

Stopping a bug story at the fix

A defect story that ends with 'the developer fixed it' misses the point. Connect the bug to the business risk it carried or the user experience it affected. That context is what makes the story memorable and credible.

Having no questions for the interviewer

Candidates who say 'no, I am good' when asked 'do you have questions for us?' typically leave a weaker impression. Prepare two genuine questions about the team's quality processes, their biggest current testing challenge, or how they measure engineering quality.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does a QA Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report that most companies run between three and five rounds, though the number varies by company size and role seniority. A common sequence is an HR screen, one or two technical rounds, and a final managerial or team-fit discussion. Senior and lead roles often add a test architecture round. Always confirm the structure with your recruiter before you start preparing.

Do QA Engineer interviews include coding tests?

It depends on the role. Mid-level and senior positions at product companies typically include an automation or coding exercise, often writing test scripts in Java, Python, or JavaScript. Manual QA roles at service companies may skip coding entirely and focus on test case design and defect reporting. If the job title includes 'automation,' 'SDET,' or 'test engineer,' expect a coding component and prepare accordingly.

What salary can I expect as a QA Engineer in India in 2026?

Based on knok jobradar data from July 2026 across 459 tracked openings, entry-level roles (0-2 years) show a range of 4-9 LPA, mid-level (3-5 years) 9-17 LPA, senior (6-9 years) 17-30 LPA, and lead roles 28-45+ LPA. These are observed ranges, not guarantees. For company-specific benchmarks, publicly reported data on Glassdoor and levels.fyi can provide additional context.

Is Selenium still worth learning, or should I focus on Playwright or Cypress?

All three remain in active use across Indian companies as of 2026. Selenium is still dominant in large enterprise and legacy product stacks. Playwright has grown quickly for modern web testing because of its speed and cross-browser support. Cypress is popular at frontend-heavy teams. The practical approach is to be solid in one and comfortable explaining trade-offs with at least one other, since interviewers often ask why you chose a specific tool.

How should I answer if I missed a bug that reached production?

Be honest and own the gap without over-explaining. Interviewers respect candidates who reflect without deflecting. Use the STAR format: briefly describe what happened and why the gap occurred (missing coverage, unclear requirement, time pressure), then spend most of your answer on the concrete process change you put in place afterward. Ending with a specific improvement you drove shows maturity and self-awareness.

What is the difference between a QA Engineer and an SDET role in terms of interview expectations?

A QA Engineer interview focuses on testing strategy, test design techniques, defect management, and some automation. An SDET (Software Development Engineer in Test) interview leans heavily on coding ability, framework design, and sometimes contributing to production tooling. SDET interviews typically expect stronger knowledge of data structures and algorithms. If the job title says SDET or 'automation engineer,' prepare for a more code-intensive process than a standard QA role.
