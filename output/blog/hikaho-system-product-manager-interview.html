03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format for all behavioural and product experience questions. Here are three model answers.

---

Q: Tell us about a product you shipped that did not meet expectations.

*Situation:* I was PM for a mobile notifications feature designed to improve daily active usage on our platform. We shipped on schedule after a focused sprint.

*Task:* Shortly after launch, engagement on the feature was flat. My task was to diagnose the problem quickly and decide the right next step.

*Action:* I pulled session data and ran user interviews with a small sample. We found that notifications were firing at times users were busy, so they dismissed them without opening. I worked with engineering to add a quiet-hours preference, tested the change with a subset of users, then rolled it out more broadly.

*Result:* Open rates improved after the fix. More importantly, I added a post-launch review step to our standard process so the team catches early signal instead of assuming launch equals done.

---

Q: How do you prioritise when sales, engineering, and users all want different things?

*Situation:* At my previous company, the enterprise sales team was pushing hard for a custom reporting module. Engineering flagged it as high-effort. Meanwhile, user research pointed to a simpler onboarding gap as the bigger retention problem.

*Task:* I needed to make a clear call and get all three groups aligned behind one direction.

*Action:* I mapped each request against our quarterly OKRs and estimated impact using data we already had. The onboarding gap tied directly to our retention target. I put together a short document showing the trade-off, proposed a phased approach where we fix onboarding first and schedule the reporting module for the following quarter, and held a joint review with the sales and engineering leads.

*Result:* Both teams accepted the plan. Retention improved after the onboarding fix, which also gave us a stronger case to invest in the sales request next quarter.

---

Q: Describe a time you made a product decision with incomplete data.

*Situation:* We were deciding whether to expand our product to a second city before we had complete data from the first launch.

*Task:* Leadership wanted a recommendation within a week. Full data would have taken another month to collect.

*Action:* I identified which data points were must-have versus nice-to-have for the decision. I used the available cohort data, ran a quick competitive scan, and defined clear success criteria along with a rollback plan upfront so the decision felt lower-risk to stakeholders.

*Result:* Leadership approved the expansion. We hit the success criteria in the first month, and having the rollback plan documented reduced anxiety across the team.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (the baseline): Every behavioural answer should follow Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task brief, spend most of your time on Action, and always close with a measurable or observable Result. Candidates who skip the Result leave interviewers guessing.

CIRCLES (for product design questions): Comprehend the situation, Identify the customer, Report the customer's needs, Cut through prioritisation, List solutions, Evaluate trade-offs, Summarise your recommendation. Use this when asked 'how would you design X' or 'design a product for Y.'

The Metrics Framework: When asked about success or diagnosis questions, structure your answer in steps: define the goal, name the key metric and its supporting metrics, describe how you would instrument and track them, and state what a bad signal looks like and how you would respond. This shows analytical rigour without overcomplicating your answer.

The Prioritisation Stack: When asked 'how do you prioritise,' show a clear hierarchy: company strategy first, then OKRs or goals for the current period, then user impact, then effort. Naming your framework out loud ('I use a value-effort matrix anchored to our OKRs') sounds far more structured than listing features you personally like.

The Stakeholder Alignment Script: State the conflict clearly, show you heard all sides, explain the criteria you used to decide, and describe how you communicated the outcome to the side that did not get what they wanted. Interviewers want to see that you close the loop, not just make the call.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Structured thinking, not scripted answers: Interviewers at companies like Hikaho System typically value candidates who think out loud in a clear framework rather than jumping straight to a conclusion. Pause, label your structure ('I will break this into three parts'), then proceed.

Data fluency: PM roles require real comfort with numbers. You do not need to be a data scientist, but you should talk about metrics, funnels, cohorts, and A/B tests without hesitation. Saying 'I would check the data' without specifying which data or what you are looking for loses points quickly.

Ownership, not just participation: There is a clear difference between 'we built this together' and 'I drove this decision by doing X.' Interviewers want to understand your specific contribution. Use 'I' intentionally without dismissing your teammates.

Comfort with trade-offs: Good PMs say no and explain why. If every answer involves building everything for every user, interviewers will flag a lack of judgment. Show that you have a clear mental model for trade-offs and can defend it under pressure.

Genuine interest in the company: Candidates who have used the product, read recent news, or thought about the competitive landscape come across as motivated. Generic answers signal low interest, which matters more when only one seat is available.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Know the company and the role
Read everything public about Hikaho System: their website, recent news, and any product or app reviews you can find. List the core problems their product solves and who the end user is. Note one or two things you would improve and prepare to discuss them with specifics, not just opinions.

Step 2: Build your story bank
Write out several work stories using the STAR format before your interviews. Cover a product win, a product failure, a difficult stakeholder situation, a data-driven decision, and a prioritisation call. Practise saying each one out loud. Recording yourself once and listening back reveals filler words and gaps in logic you miss when speaking.

Step 3: Case and metrics practice
Work through several product design cases and some metrics or diagnosis cases. Use the CIRCLES and Metrics frameworks described in the answer frameworks section. Focus on structuring your answer before diving into solutions. Interviewers at technology companies often care more about how you think than which answer you land on.

Step 4: Mock interviews and logistics
Do mock interviews with a peer or a PM community before your actual rounds. Check your audio and video setup if any rounds are remote. Prepare a few sharp questions for the interviewer, covering the team's current challenges, how product and engineering collaborate day-to-day, and how success is measured for this role in the early months.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping the Result in STAR answers: Candidates often give rich context but forget to close with what actually happened. Always end with a concrete outcome, even an imperfect one. No result means no proof.

Treating every metric drop the same way: When asked 'a metric dropped, what do you do,' jumping straight to 'I would check the dashboard' is too thin. Walk through a structured hypothesis tree: external factors, recent product changes, data pipeline issues, and segment-level breakdowns before reaching a conclusion.

Using jargon without grounding it: Words like 'synergy,' 'alignment,' and 'ecosystem' without concrete examples signal surface-level thinking. Every claim should connect to something you actually did, decided, or shipped.

Not asking clarifying questions on case studies: Jumping into a product design case without asking about the user, the business goal, and constraints is a common misstep. Interviewers often reward the pause and the clarifying question over an immediate answer.

Generic company research: Saying 'I admire your culture of innovation' tells the interviewer nothing. Reference a specific product decision, a customer segment, or a market the company operates in. With only one PM seat open at Hikaho System, standing out on genuine interest matters more than it would at a high-volume hiring company.

Underselling your own scope: Candidates who say 'we decided' for every question make it hard to assess individual impact. Be specific about your role without throwing teammates under the bus.
