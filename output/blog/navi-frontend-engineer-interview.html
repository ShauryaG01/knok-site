01 Overview

Overview

Navi is a Bangalore-based fintech company offering personal loans, home loans, health insurance, and mutual funds through its app. With 60 open roles tracked by knok's job radar as of mid-2026, it is one of the more active hiring companies for frontend engineers right now.

Interviews at Navi typically run three to five rounds. Candidates report a recruiter screening, one or two technical rounds covering React and JavaScript, a machine coding or take-home assignment, and a final round on product thinking or culture fit. Confirm the exact structure with your recruiter after the first call.

The fintech context sets Navi apart from a typical product company interview. Your code will directly touch loan applications, KYC flows, and payment screens. Interviewers probe whether you think about UX failure states, data security, and accessibility, not just component architecture.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up most often in Navi frontend interviews, based on what candidates report across forums and review sites.

  1. Walk us through a complex React component you built from scratch. How did you manage its state and side effects?
  2. How would you design the UI for a multi-step loan application that minimises user drop-off at each stage?
  3. Navi handles sensitive financial data on the client side. How do you prevent XSS attacks and protect user data in the browser?
  4. Explain how React's reconciliation algorithm works and describe a real case where you avoided unnecessary re-renders in production.
  5. A customer needs to see their real-time EMI schedule. How would you fetch, cache, and keep that data fresh without calling the API too frequently?
  6. How do you make a financial form with OTP, PAN, and Aadhaar inputs accessible to users who rely on screen readers?
  7. Describe a situation where you improved page load performance on a production frontend. What metrics did you track and what specifically did you change?
  8. Navi's products reach Tier 2 and Tier 3 city users on low-end Android devices with slow connections. How would you optimise the frontend for them?
  9. How would you structure a shared Design System component library so that multiple product teams can adopt it without conflicts or duplication?
  10. How do you manage the frontend when the backend team ships a breaking API change without advance notice?
  11. How would you test a multi-step insurance onboarding form, from unit tests through to end-to-end coverage?
  12. Tell us about a time you disagreed with a product or design decision that affected the UI. What did you do?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time you improved page load performance on a production app.

*Situation:* At my previous company, the main dashboard for our financial product was taking several seconds to become interactive on a mid-range Android device.

*Task:* I was asked to bring the load time down significantly without a full rewrite of the page.

*Action:* I profiled the page in Chrome DevTools and found two main issues: an oversized JavaScript bundle and unoptimised images. I split the bundle using React.lazy and Suspense to load non-critical parts on demand, deferred third-party scripts until after the main content loaded, converted images to WebP, and added lazy loading for below-the-fold elements.

*Result:* The page became interactive much faster, and the product team saw a measurable improvement in session duration in the analytics the following week.

---

Q: How did you handle a situation where the backend team shipped a breaking API change mid-sprint?

*Situation:* Our backend team released a new version of the user profile API that renamed several fields while I was building a profile page that depended on them.

*Task:* I needed to keep the existing page stable for users while migrating to the new schema within the same sprint.

*Action:* I created a thin adapter layer in the frontend that mapped the old field names to new ones, wrote unit tests for the adapter so the mapping stayed verified automatically, and coordinated with the backend team to agree on a joint cutover date. I also raised the versioning gap in our next retrospective and proposed a lightweight contract-first approach for future API changes.

*Result:* The page stayed live with no regression for users, and the team adopted a simple versioning agreement for subsequent releases.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you pushed back on a product decision that affected the UI.

*Situation:* A product manager wanted to add an auto-scroll feature to our loan application form that would jump users to the next field automatically after each input.

*Task:* I believed this would hurt usability and break screen reader navigation, but I needed to make the case without simply saying 'no.'

*Action:* I raised the concern in the planning meeting, referenced two accessibility guidelines that explicitly caution against auto-scroll in forms, and proposed a quick usability check with a small group of internal users before committing to the feature.

*Result:* The team agreed to the test. The results confirmed the concern clearly, and we shipped a standard 'Next' button instead. The PM later mentioned that having concrete user data made the decision far easier than a debate on principles alone.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For technical questions: Lead with your decision or approach, then explain the trade-off you evaluated before choosing it, then close with what you learned or would do differently next time. Interviewers at product companies like Navi want to follow your reasoning, not just hear the textbook answer.

For UI system design questions: Use a top-down structure. Start with the user journey (what does the person on the app actually need to accomplish?), then move to component structure, state management, API interaction, and finally performance and error handling. Anchoring your design in the user's task signals the product thinking Navi values.

For behavioural questions: Use STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result), but keep it tight. One sentence each on Situation and Task is enough context. Go deeper on Action, which reveals your thinking, and Result, which shows impact. Candidates often spend too long on setup and run out of time before explaining what they actually did.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Candidates report that Navi's frontend interviewers consistently look for three things above everything else.

Product empathy. Navi builds financial products for users who may be using a loan app for the first time. Interviewers want to see that you think about the person at the end of the screen, including what a confusing error message costs them or why a KYC step with too many taps leads to abandonment. Bring this perspective into technical answers wherever you naturally can.

Performance awareness. Their user base spans low-end Android devices and variable network quality. Any answer about UI architecture should naturally touch on bundle size, lazy loading, skeleton screens, or network-aware rendering. If you skip this, interviewers will probe until you address it.

Code quality and maintainability. Navi's frontend code is shared across multiple product teams. Interviewers look at how you structure components, name variables, handle edge cases, and approach testing. A working solution that is hard to read or extend is treated as a red flag, especially at the senior level.

Bonus: candidates who show even basic awareness of accessibility (semantic HTML, ARIA roles, WCAG basics) and frontend security (XSS prevention, safe local storage handling) consistently report stronger feedback from Navi's technical rounds.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Core React and JavaScript. Go deep on hooks (useState, useEffect, useCallback, useMemo, useRef), the reconciliation process, and common performance pitfalls like stale closures and missed dependency arrays. Practice a few JavaScript problems daily covering closures, prototypes, and async patterns to stay sharp on fundamentals.

Week 2: UI system design. Practice designing a multi-step loan application flow, a real-time EMI dashboard, and a shared Design System component library. For each scenario, think through state management choices, API interaction patterns, loading and error states, and what happens on a slow or dropped network.

Week 3: Machine coding practice. Build a few small apps from scratch: a multi-step form with validation, a data table with filtering and pagination, and a modal-based workflow. Focus on clean structure and test coverage rather than visual polish. Time yourself so you know your pace under interview conditions.

Before the interview: Open Navi's app on your phone and use it as a real user would. Note one or two things you found intuitive and one thing you would change. Interviewers respond well to candidates who have actually used the product and can discuss it concretely.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

  1. Treating Navi like a generic tech company. Their product is fintech. Generic React answers with no reference to financial UX, security, or the stakes of a failed transaction will feel thin to an interviewer who works on loan disbursals every day.
  1. Answering 'what' without 'why.' Saying 'I used Context API for state management' is not enough. Interviewers want to hear why you chose that over Redux or Zustand, and what trade-off you accepted by doing so.
  1. Ignoring performance and accessibility. Both come up in nearly every Navi frontend interview, candidates report. Not mentioning them at all suggests you have not built products at scale or for diverse users.
  1. Over-engineering the machine coding round. Interviewers want clean, working code with a sensible structure. A simple solution done well beats a complex one left half-finished when time runs out.
  1. Not engaging during the questions section. The 'do you have questions for us' portion is an opportunity. Ask about the team's current technical challenges, how they handle Design System governance, or what a typical sprint looks like. Candidates who skip this section consistently leave a weaker impression.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many interview rounds does Navi typically have for a Frontend Engineer role?

Candidates report typically three to five rounds for frontend positions. This usually includes a recruiter screening, one or two technical rounds on React and JavaScript, a machine coding or take-home assignment, and a final round on product thinking or culture fit. The exact count varies by team and seniority level, so confirm the process with your recruiter after the first call.

Does Navi use React or React Native for its frontend work?

Navi's primary product is a mobile app, and candidates report that the team works with React Native for consumer-facing features alongside React for internal or web-based tooling. The core concepts overlap significantly, so strong React fundamentals will carry you through the interview regardless of which framework a specific team uses.

What should I expect in the machine coding round at Navi?

Candidates report that the machine coding round typically involves building a small but complete frontend feature, such as a multi-step form, a filterable data table, or a simple dashboard widget. The focus is on clean code structure, edge case handling, and test coverage rather than visual styling. A simpler solution finished cleanly is better than an ambitious one left incomplete when time runs out.

What is the typical timeline from application to offer at Navi?

Candidates publicly report the process taking two to four weeks from first contact to offer, though this varies by team and urgency. Following up with your recruiter after each round is normal and expected. If you have a competing offer with a deadline, mention it early so the team can adjust their timeline if they are able to.

Does Navi hire freshers or entry-level frontend engineers?

Navi does hire across experience levels, and the salary data shows an entry band of 5-11 LPA for 0-2 years of experience. That said, even entry-level candidates are expected to show solid React fundamentals and at least one project that demonstrates real product thinking rather than tutorial clones. A live project or a well-documented GitHub portfolio makes a significant difference at this level.

How important is product thinking compared to pure coding ability at Navi?

Both matter, but candidates consistently report that Navi's interviewers probe product thinking more than most product companies do. Being able to speak about why a user might drop off a loan application, what a confusing error state costs the business, or how accessibility affects Navi's target audience is as important as writing efficient code. Technical ability typically gets you to the final round; product thinking often decides the offer.
