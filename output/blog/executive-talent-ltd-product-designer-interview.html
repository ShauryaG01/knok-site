01 Overview

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Interviewers at Executive Talent Ltd typically probe process thinking, stakeholder collaboration, and adaptability across industries. Candidates report that the 'why behind your decisions' matters more than the polish of your final output. Here are the questions most commonly reported:

  1. Walk us through a product or feature you designed end-to-end. What was the hardest design decision you made, and why?
  2. How do you approach designing for users with low digital literacy or limited access to smartphones?
  3. Tell us about a time a stakeholder or client disagreed with your design direction. How did you respond?
  4. How do you balance user needs against tight business timelines or budget constraints?
  5. Describe your research process. Which methods do you use most, and how do you choose between them?
  6. How do you decide when a design is ready to hand off to developers?
  7. Walk us through one or two projects from your portfolio. Which are you proudest of, and what would you change today?
  8. How do you make sure your designs are built the way you intended? Describe your working relationship with the engineering team.
  9. Tell us about a design that underperformed after launch. What did you do next?
  10. How do you keep your skills and tools current? What has changed in your practice in the past year or two?
  11. How would you approach designing a feature for a product or user segment you have no prior context on?
  12. Describe a situation where you had to make a design decision quickly with very little data or user input.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for most behavioral questions. These three sample answers are built around questions that come up frequently in Executive Talent Ltd interviews.

---

Q: Tell us about a time a stakeholder pushed back on your design direction.

*Situation:* I was redesigning the onboarding flow for a B2B SaaS product. The product manager wanted to cut the number of steps to speed up time-to-activation.

*Task:* My research showed that skipping a key data-entry step led users to configure the product incorrectly, which created a spike in support tickets. I needed to make the case for keeping the step without delaying the launch.

*Action:* I pulled two months of support ticket data and mapped it directly to the skipped step. I then built a prototype of a 'smart default' version of the step that reduced user effort without removing it, and presented both options to the PM with clear business trade-offs laid out.

*Result:* The team agreed to keep the step using the smart-default design. Support tickets for that issue dropped, onboarding completion held steady, and the PM began using the same trade-off framing in other product discussions.

---

Q: Tell us about a design that did not perform as expected after launch.

*Situation:* I redesigned the main navigation for a consumer app to reduce the number of taps to reach key features. I was confident the new structure was more intuitive.

*Task:* After launch, session data showed users were actually spending more time searching for features, not less. I needed to understand why and respond quickly.

*Action:* I ran five quick usability sessions with existing users and found that the labels I had chosen made sense to me but did not match the words users used to describe those features. I had also underweighted how familiar users were with the original navigation.

*Result:* We shipped a revised version within two weeks using labels grounded in user language. Engagement recovered, and I added a label-testing step to our standard design process so the team would catch this type of issue earlier.

---

Q: How do you design for users with low digital literacy?

*Situation:* I was designing a financial product targeting first-time smartphone users in smaller cities.

*Task:* The existing flow assumed users were comfortable with common app patterns like swipe gestures, tab bars, and modal overlays. Our research showed many users in this segment were not.

*Action:* I ran contextual interviews with first-time users across two locations and mapped every point where they hesitated or guessed incorrectly. I rebuilt the core flow using only tap-based interactions, larger touch targets, plain-language labels tested out loud with users, and progressive disclosure so screens never felt overwhelming.

*Result:* Task completion in testing improved significantly compared to the original design. The product team adopted the accessibility-first checklist I created as a standard for all new features going forward.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For portfolio walkthroughs, structure your answer around: what the product was and who the users were, the specific problem you were solving, your process (research, ideation, iteration), the key decisions you made and why, the outcome, and what you would do differently now. Interviewers at a staffing firm like Executive Talent Ltd often probe the 'why' at each step, so be ready to justify decisions rather than just describe them.

For conflict and pushback questions, lead with listening and evidence rather than 'I convinced them I was right.' Show that you understand business goals and can translate user needs into language stakeholders care about, such as reduced support costs, higher retention, or faster activation.

For process questions (research methods, handoff, design QA), be specific about tools and steps but also show judgment. Saying 'I always do user interviews before starting' is less convincing than 'I match the method to the question. For this problem, a five-person usability test was enough because we were testing recognition, not preference.'

For design challenges or take-home briefs, candidates report that interviewers look for a clear problem framing, explicit assumptions, a documented process (even rough sketches count), and an honest reflection on what you would test or improve with more time. A polished final screen with no process shown is typically scored lower than a clear thinking journey with a rougher visual output.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates report from Executive Talent Ltd Product Designer interviews, interviewers are consistently looking for four things.

Clear thinking over beautiful output. Because Executive Talent Ltd places designers with clients across multiple industries, interviewers need confidence that your process holds up in unfamiliar product contexts. They want to see how you frame problems and make decisions when you do not have full information, not just when you already know the domain.

Stakeholder communication skills. Designers placed by a talent firm often work directly with client teams from day one. Expect questions about how you explain design decisions to non-designers, how you handle pushback, and how you manage expectations when timelines compress unexpectedly.

Ownership and honest reflection. Interviewers tend to respond well to candidates who can talk clearly about what went wrong in a past project and what they changed as a result. Presenting only successes can read as a lack of self-awareness or growth mindset.

Solid developer collaboration. Questions about your handoff process and how you handle gaps between design intent and the built product come up regularly. Prepare a specific story about catching a discrepancy and resolving it constructively with the engineering team.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

One week before your interview:

  1. Revisit every project in your portfolio and prepare a 5-7 minute walkthrough for your top two or three pieces. Focus on decisions and outcomes, not just deliverables.
  2. Research Executive Talent Ltd: look at their LinkedIn page, the sectors they recruit for, and the types of clients they serve. This helps you speak their language during the interview.
  3. Write out STAR-format answers for the 12 most common questions listed above. Practice saying them out loud, not just writing them down.
  4. Prepare at least two concrete examples of handling stakeholder disagreement, designing with incomplete data, and a design that underperformed after launch.

Two to three days before:

  1. If you received a take-home brief, make sure your process documentation is clear and easy to follow, even if the visuals are still rough.
  2. Prepare three questions to ask the interviewer. Good options: 'What does success look like in the first three months?', 'What is the biggest design challenge the team is working on right now?', and 'How does the design team collaborate directly with clients?'

Day of the interview:

  1. Keep your portfolio open and ready to share your screen if the interview is remote.
  2. Have your resume visible so you can reference specific roles and dates quickly if asked.
  3. Test your audio and screen-share setup well before the scheduled start time.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Showing only finished screens. Interviewers at talent firms want to see your thinking process. A Figma file full of polished UI with no sketches, research notes, or iteration history makes it hard for them to assess how you think. Always show the messy middle.

Vague answers to process questions. Saying 'I do user research and iterate' tells an interviewer very little. Be specific: which methods, how many participants, what you learned, and how it changed the design direction.

Not framing the problem before jumping into a design challenge. Diving straight into solutions without stating your assumptions or asking about constraints reads as a lack of product thinking. Spend the first few minutes defining the problem before sketching anything.

Treating it like a single-product or agency interview. Executive Talent Ltd places designers across multiple client industries. If you only discuss one vertical or product type, you may seem too narrow. Show that your design process works in new and unfamiliar contexts.

Underestimating the collaboration questions. Many candidates prepare heavily for portfolio questions but get caught off guard by 'tell us about a conflict with a PM or developer.' Prepare at least two concrete stories about navigating disagreement professionally and constructively.

Skipping the follow-up. Candidates report that a short, specific thank-you note referencing something from the conversation is noticed at staffing firms, where communication skills are part of what they assess in a designer candidate.
