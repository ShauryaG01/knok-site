01 Overview

Overview

Glean is an enterprise AI search company, helping organizations find and connect knowledge across tools like Slack, Google Drive, and Confluence. With 146 open roles as of July 2026, Glean is actively scaling its engineering leadership. An Engineering Manager here typically owns a team shipping features that touch Glean's core AI and search capabilities, so you need credibility on both the people side and the technical side.

Candidates report that the interview process typically involves multiple rounds covering leadership philosophy, technical depth, cross-functional collaboration, and culture fit. Preparation should be thorough because Glean tends to reward depth and specificity over generic answers. For context, Engineering Manager salaries are publicly reported in the 35-60 LPA range, with Senior Manager roles in the 55-90 LPA range.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These are the questions candidates most commonly report from Glean Engineering Manager interviews:

  1. Glean builds AI-powered search for enterprises. How have you led teams building products with AI or ML components?
  2. How do you balance shipping fast with maintaining engineering quality and reliability?
  3. Tell us about a time you hired or developed an engineer who had strong potential but needed significant growth.
  4. Glean serves large enterprise customers. How have you managed engineering commitments tied to enterprise contracts or customer relationships?
  5. Describe a situation where you had to make a significant technical trade-off under pressure. How did you decide?
  6. How do you set technical direction for a team working in a new or ambiguous problem space?
  7. Tell us about a time you drove alignment between engineering, product, and sales or customer success on a high-stakes initiative.
  8. How do you handle underperformance, especially when someone is technically strong but struggling in other areas?
  9. Glean's mission is to make knowledge accessible across organizations. How do you foster knowledge sharing within your own team?
  10. How do you keep engineers motivated and focused during periods of rapid growth or organizational change?
  11. Describe a time you improved your team's engineering processes or delivery speed. What changed and what was the outcome?
  12. How do you build a culture of ownership and accountability on an engineering team?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How have you led teams building products with AI or ML components?

*Situation:* My team was asked to add a semantic search capability to our product within a tight delivery window, and few engineers on the team had prior ML experience.

*Task:* I needed to ship a working feature while managing the learning curve and keeping stakeholders confident.

*Action:* I brought in one senior ML engineer to anchor the technical direction and structured the work so the rest of the team could contribute without needing deep ML expertise. I set up a weekly internal session where the ML engineer shared knowledge with the broader team. I also worked with the product manager to scope a minimum viable version we could ship and measure before adding complexity.

*Result:* We shipped the initial version on schedule. User engagement with the feature was positive, and the team's comfort with ML-adjacent work improved visibly over the following sprints.

---

Q: How do you handle underperformance when someone is technically strong but struggling in other areas?

*Situation:* An engineer on my team was consistently missing delivery commitments and seemed disengaged, despite having solid technical skills.

*Task:* I needed to address the gap without losing a valuable contributor.

*Action:* I had a direct, private conversation to understand what was happening. The engineer shared they felt their work lacked impact and were unclear on how it connected to company goals. I worked with them to redefine their scope, gave them ownership of a higher-visibility initiative, and set up regular check-ins to track both delivery and engagement.

*Result:* Within one quarter, their output and attitude both improved. They went on to mentor a junior engineer on the team. The experience reinforced how much clarity of purpose matters for engineers.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you drove alignment between engineering, product, and sales on a high-stakes initiative.

*Situation:* Sales had committed to a large enterprise customer that a specific feature would be delivered by a certain date. Engineering had significant concerns about the scope and the timeline.

*Task:* I needed to broker an agreement that protected engineering's integrity without damaging the customer relationship.

*Action:* I organized a working session with engineering, product, and sales leads. I laid out the technical constraints clearly and translated them into business language so sales could communicate realistic expectations to the customer. I proposed a phased delivery plan: a smaller, high-value version first, with the full feature to follow.

*Result:* The customer accepted the plan. We delivered the first phase on time and the customer renewed. The approach became a template the team used for similar situations going forward.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For leadership and people questions: The STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) works well. Keep the Situation brief, spend most of your time on Action, specifically what you personally did rather than what the team did as a whole. Glean interviewers want to understand your judgment and your direct contribution.

For 'how do you think about X' questions: Use a 'Principle, then Example' structure. State your approach or belief clearly first, then anchor it with a real story from your past. This shows both self-awareness and practical experience.

For technical judgment questions: Walk through your decision-making step by step. Show your reasoning process, not just your conclusion. Glean values clear, structured thinking, especially in ambiguous situations.

For cross-functional questions: Demonstrate that you understand other teams' incentives and pressures. Interviewers want to see that you can build alignment through influence and clear communication, not just by using your title.

One rule across all frameworks: Be specific. Vague answers like 'I worked with the team to find a solution' are not compelling. Name what you actually decided, what you did, and what the outcome was.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Glean interviewers typically look for Engineering Managers who combine strong technical credibility with genuine people leadership. Candidates report that the bar is high on both dimensions. You should be able to discuss system design decisions, technical trade-offs, and architecture choices at a level that earns senior engineers' respect, while also showing concrete examples of how you have grown people and shaped team culture.

Glean moves fast and competes in the enterprise AI space, so comfort with ambiguity and a bias toward action are valued. The company serves large enterprise clients, so demonstrating awareness of reliability, customer commitments, and operational discipline also matters.

Culturally, Glean emphasizes transparency and knowledge sharing as core values. These are, after all, central to what their product does. Expect questions that probe whether you genuinely live those values within your own team. Interviewers often look for managers who are direct, who give honest feedback, and who take ownership when things go wrong rather than deflecting.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Understand Glean's product deeply. Try a demo or watch Glean's public product videos. Know what Glean does, who it serves, and how it differs from competitors. Candidates who show genuine product curiosity make a strong impression.

Build a strong set of leadership stories. Prepare stories covering: hiring and developing engineers, handling underperformance, cross-functional alignment, technical decision-making under ambiguity, and managing through rapid change. Each story should be specific and follow the STAR format.

Brush up on AI and ML fundamentals at a conceptual level. You do not need to be an ML researcher, but you should be comfortable discussing topics like retrieval-augmented generation, embedding models, and search relevance at a level that supports credible conversations with engineers.

Read Glean's engineering blog and public talks. Glean engineers and leaders have shared perspectives on scaling systems, AI product challenges, and engineering culture. Reading these helps you speak Glean's language in the interview.

Prepare thoughtful questions. Ask about team structure, how engineering collaborates with product and sales, and how success is measured for Engineering Managers. Thoughtful questions signal genuine interest and senior-level thinking.

Practice out loud. Run through your stories with a peer or record yourself. Verbal fluency matters as much as content in leadership interviews.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Being too vague. The most common mistake in EM interviews is giving answers that describe what the team did rather than what you personally decided and did. Be specific about your role and your reasoning.

Treating technical and people questions as separate. Glean wants managers who integrate both. When answering a technical question, mention how you involved or developed your team. When answering a people question, show technical awareness.

Not knowing Glean's product. Candidates who come in without a clear understanding of what Glean does struggle to connect their experience to Glean's specific challenges. This is easy to fix with preparation.

Over-optimizing for structure at the cost of authenticity. STAR format is useful, but robotic, formulaic answers can feel rehearsed. Let the story breathe and be honest about what went wrong and what you learned.

Underselling results. Many engineers who move into management minimize their impact. If you improved delivery speed, retained a key engineer, or grew your team's capabilities, say so clearly.

Asking weak closing questions. Ending with 'so what does a typical day look like?' signals low preparation. Ask about engineering culture, current team challenges, or how Engineering Manager success is defined and measured.

Candidates report that the Glean EM process typically involves multiple rounds: a recruiter screen, one or more leadership and behavioral interviews, a technical discussion, and a final panel-style set of conversations. The exact number varies by team and level. Checking recent candidate experiences on Glassdoor gives the most current picture of what to expect.

Does Glean ask coding questions to Engineering Managers?

Candidates report that Glean EM interviews focus more on technical judgment and system design than on live coding. You are typically expected to discuss architectural trade-offs, technical decision-making, and how you partner with engineers on complex problems. The bar for technical credibility is high, so be prepared to discuss your engineering background in depth.
