01 Overview

Overview

Belsimpel is one of the Netherlands' leading telecom and electronics retailers, built on a product-squad engineering culture with strong ownership values. The company's careers platform runs under the brand 'Werken bij Belsimpel.' As of July 2026, 57 open roles are listed across the organization, spanning engineering, product, and operations.

The Engineering Manager position typically owns one or more cross-functional squads, working closely with product managers and designers. You are accountable for hiring, team health, technical direction, and delivery outcomes. It is a people-first role, not a senior individual contributor role.

Candidates typically report a process of three to five stages: an initial HR or recruiter call, one or two rounds focused on leadership scenarios and people management, a technical or system design session, and a final conversation with a senior leader. Since Belsimpel is headquartered in Groningen, the Netherlands, interviews are generally conducted over video. The tone is reported to be direct and practical, with a strong focus on real situations from your past rather than abstract puzzles.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions are compiled from publicly shared interview reports and reflect the themes Belsimpel interviewers typically explore for Engineering Manager candidates.

  1. Tell me about a time you had to manage underperformance in someone you inherited, not someone you hired.
  2. Describe a situation where you pushed back on a product or business decision. How did you frame it and what was the outcome?
  3. Belsimpel runs on autonomous product squads. How have you created real alignment between engineering, product, and design without creating process overhead?
  4. Walk me through a time you had to deliver a project with a team missing key skills. What did you actually do?
  5. How do you balance technical debt reduction with feature delivery when both feel urgent?
  6. How do you run hiring for your team? What specifically do you look for in senior engineers?
  7. Tell me about a technical architecture decision you made or significantly influenced. What trade-offs did you weigh?
  8. How do you give feedback to a technically excellent engineer who is blocking collaboration on the team?
  9. Belsimpel values ownership. How do you foster that in a team without slipping into micromanagement?
  10. Tell me about a project that failed or underdelivered. What did you learn and what would you change?
  11. How do you develop engineers who want to grow into senior or staff roles?
  12. What does a healthy engineering culture look like to you, and how have you actively shaped one?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a time you managed underperformance in someone you inherited.

*Situation:* I joined a team mid-year and inherited a squad of six, including one senior engineer who had been informally flagged as 'not contributing enough.'

*Task:* I needed to assess the situation myself, without acting on secondhand impressions, and either support the person back to strong performance or begin a formal process.

*Action:* I spent the first three weeks doing structured 1:1s with everyone and reviewing code and PR history. I found the engineer was technically capable but had been given no clear ownership for over a year. I set explicit goals for one quarter, gave weekly written feedback, and assigned them a project they owned end to end with a defined outcome.

*Result:* Within two quarters, their output and peer feedback improved noticeably. They shipped a feature that addressed a pain point the team had been deferring for months. No formal performance process was needed.

---

Q: Describe a situation where you pushed back on a product roadmap decision.

*Situation:* Our product team wanted to add three new integrations in one sprint cycle, right before a major platform migration we had already scoped and committed to.

*Task:* I had to communicate the risk clearly without damaging trust with the product team or slowing their momentum.

*Action:* I wrote a short brief, not a slide deck, showing that two of the three integrations touched the same data layer we were migrating. I proposed sequencing one integration before the migration and two after, with concrete dates attached. I shared it in writing before the planning meeting so nobody was caught off guard in the room.

*Result:* The product team agreed to the sequence. The migration went live without incidents. The two deferred integrations shipped within six weeks.

---

Q: Walk me through a technical architecture decision you influenced.

*Situation:* My team was debating whether to use a monorepo or separate repositories for three new microservices that would share core business logic.

*Task:* I needed to guide the decision without overriding the team's technical judgment, while making sure we considered long-term maintainability and onboarding cost.

*Action:* I facilitated a two-hour architecture review with the four engineers who would own the services. I asked each person to write down their top two concerns before we discussed solutions. The real worry turned out to be dependency drift, not deployment complexity. We chose a shared-library approach inside a monorepo with clear ownership boundaries and a lint rule enforcing API contracts.

*Result:* Over a year later, all three services were still on the same dependency versions. New engineers rated the repo structure as one of the clearest they had worked in.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

The STAR structure is the most reliable foundation. Every answer should move through Situation (brief context), Task (what you were responsible for), Action (what you specifically did, not what the team did), and Result (a measurable or observable outcome). Interviewers at Belsimpel typically probe for specifics, so prepare to go one or two levels deeper on any part of the story.

The 'I vs. We' check. Engineering Manager answers often hide behind 'we.' Before your interview, go through each story and replace every 'we did X' with either 'I decided X' or 'I created the conditions for the team to do X.' This one change makes your leadership visible to the interviewer.

The trade-off framing works well for technical and process questions. Instead of presenting one answer as obviously correct, show that you considered at least two approaches, name the trade-offs, and explain what made you choose what you chose in that specific context. This signals engineering maturity.

The ownership framing is especially relevant for Belsimpel given their squad culture. For any question about team dynamics or delivery, anchor your answer to three things: who owned the outcome, how that ownership was made clear, and how you held people accountable without taking over.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Direct communication over polish. Belsimpel's engineering culture is reported to value candor. Interviewers are typically looking for answers that are honest about failure, clear about your own role, and free of corporate hedging.

People leadership depth. This is the most-weighted dimension for an Engineering Manager role. Expect detailed follow-up on how you gave feedback, handled conflict, and developed individual engineers. Surface-level answers get probed hard.

Technical credibility without overreach. You do not need to be the strongest engineer in the room. You do need to show you can hold an honest technical conversation, challenge architecture decisions with good questions, and recognize when a solution carries hidden risk.

Ownership culture fit. Belsimpel runs on autonomous squads. Interviewers will look for evidence that you have created genuine ownership in teams you led, not just delegated tasks. Prepare examples where team members made meaningful decisions independently because of the environment you built.

Clear written communication. Candidates report that some rounds include a written exercise or that interviewers send questions in advance. Structured, direct writing is valued alongside verbal fluency.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Build your story bank. Write out eight to ten STAR stories covering: underperformance, conflict, hiring decisions, architecture trade-offs, delivery under pressure, team culture, and career development conversations. A single rich story can often cover more than one question.

Step 2: Research Belsimpel's engineering context. Look for public engineering blog posts, LinkedIn articles by Belsimpel engineers, or any conference talks they have shared. Pay attention to their tech stack, how squads are described, and what problems they write about publicly. Tailor at least two of your stories to their context.

Step 3: Prepare your pushback examples. Belsimpel's ownership culture means they want managers who challenge up as well as down. Have at least two clear examples of times you disagreed with a decision above you and handled it professionally and directly.

Step 4: Practice out loud. STAR answers that look polished on paper often run too long or feel scripted when spoken. Record yourself answering three questions and listen back for filler words, vague actions, and results that trail off without a clear outcome.

Step 5: Prepare your questions for them. Ask about squad structure, how engineering and product prioritization works in practice, what the biggest current engineering challenge is, and how Engineering Managers are evaluated. Specific questions signal genuine preparation.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Hiding behind 'the team.' The most common mistake Engineering Manager candidates make is describing team outcomes without making their own leadership visible. Every answer needs a clear moment where you say what you specifically decided, initiated, or changed.

Vague results. Saying a project 'went well' or 'improved team morale' is not a result. Even qualitative outcomes can be made concrete: 'we shipped on time for the first time in three quarters' or 'attrition on the team dropped to zero over that year.' Prepare specific outcomes for every story.

Over-indexing on technical depth. Some Engineering Manager candidates try to impress by going deep on system design. Belsimpel is hiring a people leader. If you spend most of your answers on architecture details, you signal misalignment with the role.

Not asking about the team you would own. Candidates who do not ask about the current team, its challenges, and what success looks like in the first six months come across as passive. This is a leadership role. You should be evaluating the opportunity as much as they are evaluating you.

Generic answers to culture questions. Saying you value 'transparency and ownership' without a specific example behind it is noise. Every value claim needs a real story attached to it.

Belsimpel is commonly cited by employees as having a flat, direct culture with genuine product ownership and squad autonomy. Candidates report that the interview process is professional and structured, with a strong focus on leadership depth and ownership culture fit.

How many rounds does the Belsimpel Engineering Manager interview typically have?

Candidates typically report three to five rounds. Candidates report that algorithm-heavy or live-coding rounds are not typical for Engineering Manager positions at Belsimpel. Belsimpel's ownership culture means the culture round typically probes whether you give engineers real autonomy or simply assign tasks and monitor output.
