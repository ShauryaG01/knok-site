01 Overview

Overview

Ava Labs is the core team behind Avalanche, one of the most active blockchain platforms today. They build products like Core wallet, the Avalanche explorer, subnet tooling, and developer SDKs used by millions globally. As of July 2026, Ava Labs has 5 open Product Designer roles, signaling active team growth across product areas.

Product Designers here work on a genuinely interesting challenge: making complex blockchain concepts usable for both crypto-native power users and complete beginners. You might design a transaction confirmation flow one week and a DeFi dashboard the next. The role demands systems thinking, sharp visual skills, and real curiosity about how blockchain technology works in practice.

Candidates report the process typically includes a recruiter call, a portfolio review, a design exercise, and panel interviews with product and engineering team members. The timeline can span several weeks depending on team availability. 02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up consistently in Ava Labs Product Designer interviews, based on what candidates report publicly:

  1. Walk us through a project where you simplified a technically complex product for non-expert users.
  2. How do you approach design for web3 or blockchain products where users have very different levels of crypto literacy?
  3. Describe your process for designing wallet or transaction flows where mistakes can have real financial consequences for the user.
  4. How do you collaborate with engineers day-to-day in a fast-moving product team?
  5. Tell us about a time your design research changed the direction of a product or feature decision.
  6. How would you approach redesigning the Avalanche Core wallet onboarding experience? Walk us through your thinking out loud.
  7. How do you balance shipping fast with maintaining design quality? Give a concrete example.
  8. Describe a situation where you pushed back or advocated for the user when business or engineering constraints conflicted with good UX.
  9. How do you approach accessibility in a crypto-native product where most current users are already technically confident?
  10. What design system or component library work have you done, and how did it improve team output?
  11. How do you measure whether a design succeeded after it ships? What does 'good outcome' look like to you beyond aesthetics?
  12. What draws you to blockchain product design specifically, and how do you keep up with how the space is evolving?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through a project where you simplified a technically complex product for non-expert users.

*Situation:* I was the sole designer on a crypto portfolio tracker that showed users their DeFi positions across multiple blockchains. Early user testing showed that new users were confused by terms like 'liquidity pool,' 'impermanent loss,' and 'gas fees,' and they were dropping off before completing setup.

*Task:* My goal was to redesign the onboarding and dashboard so a user with no prior DeFi experience could understand their own portfolio at a glance, without removing the raw data that power users relied on.

*Action:* I ran several user interviews to map exactly where confusion happened, then created a layered information architecture: plain-language summaries at the top with a 'show technical detail' toggle for more advanced breakdowns. I replaced raw token symbols with human-readable labels and added inline tooltips written in plain language. I worked closely with the frontend engineer to implement a progressive disclosure pattern across the whole app.

*Result:* After shipping, internal metrics showed a meaningful improvement in setup completion. The power-user segment continued using the detailed view, and follow-up surveys rated the onboarding as 'clear and easy to follow.'

---

Q: Describe a situation where you had to advocate for the user when business or engineering constraints pushed back.

*Situation:* The product team wanted to add a mandatory promotional banner to the wallet home screen to promote a new staking feature. Engineering had already scoped it as a persistent element with no dismiss option.

*Task:* I needed to raise a UX concern without derailing the launch timeline or damaging my relationship with the PM.

*Action:* I pulled session data showing that users primarily visited the home screen to check balances and initiate transfers. A persistent banner would bury those primary actions. I mocked up three alternatives: a dismissible card, a contextual prompt that appeared only after a user's first successful transfer, and a subtle entry point in the navigation. I paired the mockups with a short rationale doc so the PM could take concrete options to stakeholders.

*Result:* The team agreed to the dismissible card version. The staking feature still got meaningful visibility, and post-launch session data showed no drop in primary action completion rates.

---

Q: How do you collaborate with engineers day-to-day in a fast-moving product team?

*Situation:* At my previous company, we were shipping a new mobile wallet feature on a tight timeline. The engineering team was in a different time zone, and async communication gaps had led to implementation mismatches in a prior sprint.

*Task:* I needed to set up a collaboration rhythm that caught design gaps early without adding overhead for engineers already under pressure.

*Action:* I introduced a short weekly design review where I walked engineers through new specs before they started building, specifically calling out edge cases and component states that are easy to miss. I also created a shared annotation layer in Figma so engineers could drop questions directly on components rather than sending separate messages.

*Result:* In the following sprint, implementation gaps reduced noticeably. The engineering lead said the Figma annotation workflow saved time during QA, and the practice was adopted across the broader team.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use STAR for every behavioral question. Situation and Task should be brief (two to three sentences each). The Action block is where you spend most of your time: be specific about your process, the tools you used, who you worked with, and the decisions you made. Results should be concrete wherever possible, even if the evidence is qualitative user feedback or internal session data.

For portfolio walkthrough questions, use a simple three-part structure: (1) what problem you were solving and for whom, (2) the key design decisions and the reasoning behind them, (3) what you learned or would do differently now. Ava Labs interviewers care about your reasoning process, not just the visual outcome.

For hypothetical product questions (like 'redesign our onboarding'), think out loud in a structured way. Restate the goal, ask one clarifying question before diving in, then walk through user, business, and technical constraints before proposing any solution. Showing that you think in systems, not just individual screens, is what interviewers here are looking for.

For web3-specific questions, lead with the user problem before the technology. 'Users need to feel confident their transaction is final' lands better than 'we need to show block confirmations.' Ground every answer in user behavior first.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Web3 curiosity, not just credentials. You do not need prior blockchain work experience, but you need genuine interest in the space. Interviewers notice when candidates have actually used Core wallet or explored Avalanche. Familiarity with common crypto UX pain points (seed phrase setup, gas estimation, failed transactions) signals that you can contribute quickly.

Systems thinking over pixel perfection. Ava Labs products are interconnected. Interviewers want to see that you think about how a design decision in one flow affects the rest of the product, how your components fit into a design system, and how a feature will behave in edge cases or at scale.

Comfort with ambiguity and speed. Ava Labs is still a startup in many ways. Interviewers typically look for candidates who can make reasonable decisions with incomplete information, ship something, and iterate. Portfolio projects showing how you handled changing requirements or tight timelines will resonate strongly.

Strong written and verbal communication. Because many teams are distributed, the ability to write clear design rationale and present decisions asynchronously matters as much as the visual work itself. Be prepared to explain your thinking clearly at every stage of the process.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Know the product before your first call.
Download Core wallet and use it. Go through the onboarding flow, attempt a swap or a transfer, and note moments where the UX confused or impressed you. Screenshot these. Exploring Avalanche subnets or looking at the developer tooling will give you even more material to reference.

Prepare your portfolio stories.
Choose three or four projects that best match the Ava Labs context: complex data or dashboards, onboarding for unfamiliar concepts, mobile or web consumer apps, or design system work. For each project, prepare a clear verbal walkthrough covering the problem, your key decisions, the outcome, and what you learned. Practice this out loud until it flows naturally.

Research the company specifically.
Read what Ava Labs has published about their product philosophy and company values. Look at their recent blog posts, product updates, and any design talks from team members. Prepare your answer to 'why Ava Labs' with specific product references, not generic statements about blockchain being the future.

Prepare strong questions.
Come to each interview with at least two thoughtful questions for the interviewer. Asking about the design team's current challenges, how design decisions are made in practice, or what success looks like for a new designer in the first few months shows genuine interest and professionalism.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Leading with technology instead of users. Blockchain is the platform, not the product. Saying 'I want to design for decentralization' without tying it to a real user problem sounds hollow. Always anchor your answers in what the user is actually trying to accomplish.

A portfolio with no process. A collection of polished mockups with no user research, no design decisions explained, and no mention of what shipped is a red flag at a company like Ava Labs where evidence-based design matters. Show your thinking and your process, not just the final screens.

Not knowing the Avalanche product. Candidates who have never opened Core wallet or explored Avalanche will struggle with product-specific questions. Even basic hands-on familiarity signals respect for the team's time and genuine interest in the role.

Underselling collaboration. Product Designers at Ava Labs work closely with PMs and engineers. Answers that position you as a solo creator who 'handed designs off' miss an important signal. Talk specifically about how you build relationships, resolve disagreements, and move fast together.

Vague results in STAR answers. 'Users were happier' or 'the team liked it' are weak closings. Even qualitative outcomes like 'user testing showed fewer drop-offs at the confirmation step' or 'the PM used this decision framework for all future prioritization calls' are far more credible than a vague positive ending.
