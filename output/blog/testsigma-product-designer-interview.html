01 Overview

Overview

Testsigma is a cloud-based test automation platform used by QA engineers and developers to write and run automated tests with minimal coding. With 10 open Product Designer roles as of mid-2026, it is actively building its design team.

Designing at Testsigma means working on a technical B2B product whose core users are QA professionals and developers. Expect interview conversations around simplifying complex workflows, designing for power users, and building scalable design systems.

Candidates report a process that typically includes a portfolio screening, one or two design discussion rounds, and a practical design exercise. The team looks for designers who think in systems, ask sharp questions about user needs, and collaborate closely with engineers. Treat specific process details as approximate, based on what candidates have shared publicly.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a time you redesigned a complex feature based on user feedback.

*Situation:* I was the lead designer on a workflow builder used by QA teams at a B2B SaaS company. Users were dropping off in a key branching-logic section and support tickets kept pointing to the same area.

*Task:* My goal was to identify the root cause and deliver a redesigned solution within a single sprint cycle.

*Action:* I ran five moderated usability sessions with QA engineers and mapped every hesitation point. The core issue was that branching conditions used raw syntax, which confused testers who did not code regularly. I replaced the syntax input with plain-language dropdowns and added a live preview so users could see the outcome before saving. I tested two prototypes, iterated on the feedback, and handed off revised specs to engineering.

*Result:* Task completion on that flow improved significantly after launch. Support tickets for that section dropped in the first month, and the improvement came up in a team retrospective as a notable win.

---

Q: Describe a time you pushed back on a stakeholder's design request.

*Situation:* A product manager requested a prominent 'quick-run' button on every test-case row in a list view. The request came late in the cycle, just before developer handoff.

*Task:* I needed to evaluate whether this addition helped users or added noise, and communicate my position without delaying the release.

*Action:* I pulled up session recordings that showed users rarely initiated quick-runs from the list view. I proposed a lighter solution: a hover-reveal action that kept the interface clean for most users while still serving the power-user need. I mocked both approaches side by side and wrote a short rationale document explaining the tradeoff.

*Result:* The team agreed to the hover-reveal pattern. It shipped on schedule and follow-up feedback from the QA team was positive. The product manager later said the data framing helped them see the decision from the user's perspective.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you had to adapt your design when a constraint appeared mid-sprint.

*Situation:* Midway through designing a new reporting dashboard, the engineering team found a data pipeline limitation that ruled out one of the core chart types I had built the layout around.

*Task:* I had a few days to redesign the affected screens without losing the core usefulness of the dashboard.

*Action:* I set up a short sync with the engineer to understand exactly what data was available. I sketched three alternative approaches, ran them by the product manager in a quick review, and chose the option that preserved the most value. I updated the Figma files, annotated the constraint for future reference, and handed off revised specs the next day.

*Result:* The dashboard shipped on schedule. Because I documented the constraint and the decision rationale, the team avoided designing around the same limitation in a later sprint.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use STAR for behavioural questions. Every 'tell me about a time' question works best with a clear Situation, Task, Action, and Result. Keep Situation and Task brief so you spend most of your time on what you actually did and what changed as a result.

Use a design process narrative for portfolio walkthroughs. Structure it as: problem discovery, user research or constraints, ideation, decisions made and why, outcome, and what you would do differently next time. Interviewers at product companies like Testsigma often interrupt with 'why did you choose that approach?' so be ready to defend each step.

Use a 'user first, then constraints' frame for hypothetical design questions. Start by asking a clarifying question about the user and their goal, then layer in technical or business constraints. This shows you do not jump straight to solutions. If asked to design an onboarding flow, open with something like: 'Before I sketch anything, who is the primary user and what is their biggest hurdle typically?'

Anchor pushback in evidence, not preference. When you disagreed with a stakeholder, explain what evidence you used: session recordings, usability test results, usage analytics. Testsigma is a data-oriented company by nature, and interviewers respond well to designers who speak in evidence rather than gut feel.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Comfort designing for technical users. Testsigma's product is built for QA engineers and developers. Interviewers want to see that you can meet a technical audience where they are, not just apply consumer-app patterns to a complex tool.

Systems thinking. Expect questions or exercises that probe whether you think beyond a single screen. Can you extend a pattern to cover ten similar states? How does your component behave at edge cases?

Close collaboration with engineering. Candidates who have worked directly with developers and understand constraints like component libraries, API limitations, and responsive behaviour tend to stand out. Show examples of design decisions you made because of an engineering reality, not despite it.

Clear articulation of decisions. Good work is not enough on its own. Interviewers want to hear the reasoning behind each choice. Practice narrating your portfolio with explicit 'I chose this because' statements for every significant design decision.

Ownership and iteration. Testsigma is a growth-stage company that moves fast. They value designers who take initiative, ship work, observe what happens, and come back with improvements rather than waiting for direction.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Use the product before your first round. Sign up for Testsigma's free tier or watch detailed demo walkthroughs. Note flows that feel smooth and any friction you notice. Specific, genuine product observations land far better than generic praise.

Step 2: Select three to four portfolio projects to prepare in depth. Prioritise work that shows complex workflow design, collaboration with engineers, and response to user feedback. B2B or developer-tool experience is particularly relevant here.

Step 3: Practice STAR narratives out loud. Write down the Situation, Task, Action, and Result for each project. Aim for about three minutes per answer. Rambling is one of the most common ways candidates lose points in behavioural rounds.

Step 4: Prepare for a design exercise. Candidates typically report a take-home brief or a live whiteboard session. Practise redesigning a screen from a QA or developer tool and be ready to walk through your choices step by step.

Step 5: Prepare two or three sharp questions. Ask about team structure, how designers collaborate with PMs and engineers, or what a typical sprint looks like for the design team. Avoid questions that are answered on the company website.

Step 6: Check Testsigma's product blog and LinkedIn. Note any recent launches or stated company priorities. Weaving in current context shows genuine interest rather than last-minute preparation.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Showing only visual polish, not process. A beautiful portfolio with no explanation of decisions will not land at a product-focused company. Prepare to narrate the thinking behind every key screen, including the options you rejected.

Treating QA users like general consumers. Simplifying without understanding context is a red flag here. Show that you know the difference between a QA engineer using a tool daily and a first-time user, and that you can design for depth without hiding power.

Skipping clarifying questions in design exercises. Jumping straight into wireframes without asking about users, constraints, or success metrics is a common and costly mistake. Interviewers are watching whether you ask before you sketch.

Being vague about impact. Saying 'the design improved the experience' means nothing on its own. Tie results to something concrete: fewer support tickets, faster task completion in a usability test, or specific team feedback.

Not being ready to defend your own decisions. If an interviewer asks 'why did you use a modal here instead of a drawer?', you need a real answer. Review every screen in your prepared projects and anticipate detailed follow-up questions.

Underselling cross-functional work. Designers who only talk about their individual contribution miss an opportunity. Explain how you worked with PMs, engineers, and researchers, and what you learned from those partnerships.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many interview rounds does Testsigma typically have for a Product Designer role?

Candidates publicly report a process that typically includes a portfolio review call, one or two design discussion rounds, and a practical design exercise. Some report an additional round with a senior leader. Round structures can change, so confirm the exact process with your recruiter at the start.

Is there a design assignment or take-home task in the process?

Most candidates report some form of design exercise, either a take-home brief or a live whiteboard session. Topics tend to involve redesigning a complex workflow or designing a new feature for a developer-facing tool. Practise end-to-end design walkthroughs on a real product you already use so you can show your process clearly under pressure.

What kind of portfolio projects are most relevant for Testsigma?

B2B, SaaS, or developer-tool projects carry the most weight. Work that shows you can simplify technically complex workflows, contribute to a design system, or respond to user feedback is especially relevant. If your portfolio is mostly consumer apps, frame the transferable skills explicitly and connect them to a B2B context.

How important is Figma proficiency for this role?

Figma is the standard tool at most SaaS product companies and is expected here. You should be comfortable with auto-layout, components, variants, and prototyping at a minimum. Familiarity with developer handoff workflows in Figma is a bonus, given how closely designers work with engineers at Testsigma.
