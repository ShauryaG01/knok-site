01 Overview

Overview

TripAdvisor is one of the world's largest travel platforms, and its design team shapes how hundreds of millions of travellers discover, plan, and book trips. As of July 2026, TripAdvisor had 99 open roles on knok's radar, reflecting active hiring across product, design, and engineering.

For a Product Designer role, you can typically expect 3 to 5 rounds covering portfolio review, design challenges, systems thinking, and cross-functional collaboration. Candidates report that interviewers pay close attention to how you frame user problems, justify design decisions, and measure outcomes. Travel is a high-intent, emotionally driven domain, so expect questions that probe your understanding of trust, discovery, and booking psychology.

Salary data from knok's jobradar shows Product Designer roles in India range from 6-12 LPA at entry level (0-2 years), 14-24 LPA for mid-level (3-5 years), 26-40 LPA for senior (6-9 years), and 36-55+ LPA for Lead or Principal designers.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

  1. Walk us through a project in your portfolio where you improved a core user flow. What was the problem, and how did you measure success?
  2. TripAdvisor surfaces reviews, photos, prices, and recommendations all on one page. How would you decide what to show above the fold on a hotel detail page?
  3. Describe a time you had to design for users with very different needs on the same surface, such as first-time travellers and frequent business travellers.
  4. How do you approach designing for trust? Travel involves high-value decisions, so how do you use design to reduce anxiety at the booking stage?
  5. Tell us about a design you shipped that underperformed. What did you learn, and what would you do differently?
  6. How would you redesign the TripAdvisor review submission flow to increase the number and quality of reviews submitted?
  7. You are working on a feature and the PM wants to launch in two weeks, but your research shows users are confused. How do you handle that conflict?
  8. How do you collaborate with engineers during handoff? Walk us through your process from final designs to launch.
  9. TripAdvisor operates across web, iOS, and Android. How do you ensure design consistency while respecting platform conventions?
  10. Describe a situation where you had limited data and had to make a design decision anyway. How did you validate your direction?
  11. How would you improve the search and filter experience on TripAdvisor to help users narrow down a long list of restaurants to the right one?
  12. What is your approach to accessibility in your designs? Give an example of where you proactively included accessibility thinking from the start.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through a project where you improved a core user flow.

*Situation:* I was working on a food-delivery app where users frequently abandoned the checkout screen. Our funnel data showed a commonly cited drop-off pattern between cart review and payment confirmation.

*Task:* My goal was to reduce checkout abandonment without changing pricing or adding discounts, using only UX improvements.

*Action:* I ran five moderated usability sessions and found users were unsure whether their delivery address had been saved correctly. I redesigned the address confirmation step to show a map thumbnail inline and added a single-tap edit option. I also collapsed the promo code field by default, since it created a pause where users left the screen to search for codes.

*Result:* After launch, product analytics showed a meaningful improvement in checkout completion. The team tracked this as a success metric in the next quarterly review, and the pattern was reused in two other flows.

---

Q: Describe a time you had to design for users with very different needs on the same surface.

*Situation:* At a travel startup, I had a single trip-planning screen used by solo backpackers and by corporate travel bookers. Their priorities were almost opposite.

*Task:* I needed to redesign the screen so both groups could complete their core task without feeling the product was built for someone else.

*Action:* I introduced a lightweight onboarding question ('Planning for work or leisure?') that set a persistent preference. Based on the answer, the information hierarchy shifted: corporate users saw price, cancellation policy, and invoice options first; leisure users saw photos, traveller ratings, and local tips first. The underlying data was identical.

*Result:* Qualitative feedback in follow-up interviews described the experience as feeling 'more relevant.' Engagement with the trip-save feature improved noticeably among both segments, according to the post-launch review.

---

Q: Tell us about a design you shipped that underperformed.

*Situation:* I redesigned a notification preferences screen for a fintech app. I was confident the cleaner layout would improve opt-in rates for push notifications.

*Task:* The goal was to increase the share of users who kept at least some notifications enabled, reducing churn from users who turned off all alerts.

*Action:* I simplified the toggle layout, reduced the number of categories, and added short descriptions of what each notification type would send. I tested it with colleagues but skipped external user research because of the timeline.

*Result:* The redesign shipped and opt-in rates did not improve. A post-launch survey revealed that users distrusted the new descriptions because they sounded promotional. I learned that copy and tone matter as much as layout, and that skipping even one quick external test session can cost more time in rework than it saves at launch.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For portfolio walkthroughs, use the Problem-Process-Outcome structure. Open with the user problem in one sentence, walk through your process (research, ideation, testing) concisely, and close with a measurable or observable outcome. Avoid spending more than a third of your time on visual decisions.

For product critique questions (like 'how would you redesign X'), use a four-step approach: identify the user goal, name the current friction, propose a focused change, and describe how you would validate it. Interviewers at product-led companies like TripAdvisor typically want to see disciplined scoping, not a full redesign pitch.

For conflict and collaboration questions, lead with the user data or insight that informed your position, describe how you communicated it to your stakeholder, and show that you stayed open to trade-offs. Avoid framing the story as you being right and the PM or engineer being wrong.

For trust and emotion questions, name specific design patterns: social proof placement, clear cancellation policies visible before commitment, progress indicators that reduce uncertainty, and error recovery flows that do not punish users. Tie each pattern to a travel-specific scenario to show domain awareness.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

TripAdvisor interviewers typically look for designers who can operate at the intersection of user empathy and business outcomes. Travel is a considered, sometimes stressful purchase, and the team wants evidence that you understand emotional states like travel anxiety, decision fatigue, and post-booking regret, not just task flows.

They also want to see that you can work across a complex ecosystem. TripAdvisor's product spans reviews, bookings, local experiences, and media. Candidates report that interviewers respond well to designers who ask 'what is the user's broader goal?' before jumping to UI solutions.

Strong candidates show they can hold the tension between moving fast and designing carefully. Bring examples where you made a defensible decision with limited information, then followed up with validation.

Finally, cross-functional fluency matters. Be ready to speak concretely about how you work with PMs, engineers, researchers, and data analysts. Vague answers like 'I collaborate closely with the team' do not land as well as specific process details.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know the product deeply. Use TripAdvisor as a traveller for at least three to four sessions. Take notes on what confuses you, what delights you, and where you notice inconsistency. Pick two or three surfaces (hotel detail page, search results, review submission) and be ready to discuss them in depth.

Week 2: Prepare your portfolio stories. Select three to four projects and reduce each to a tight Problem-Process-Outcome narrative. For each project, prepare answers to: what was the biggest constraint, what did you cut, and what would you do differently?

Week 3: Practice design critiques and whiteboard challenges. Ask a friend to name a TripAdvisor screen and give you fifteen minutes to propose an improvement. Focus on articulating your reasoning out loud, not on producing polished visuals.

Before each round: Review publicly available information about TripAdvisor's design team, including their blog, conference talks, and LinkedIn posts from designers on the team. Candidates report that showing genuine familiarity with the team's thinking leaves a strong impression.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping to UI before defining the problem. Interviewers typically stop candidates who immediately sketch screens. Start by restating the user problem and asking one or two clarifying questions.

Treating TripAdvisor as a generic e-commerce app. Travel has unique trust and discovery dynamics. Candidates who use retail or social media analogies without adapting them to travel context tend to score lower on domain fit.

Weak outcome statements. Saying 'the design was well received' or 'the team liked it' is not enough. Even if you do not have hard metrics, describe what changed in user behavior or what the team observed after launch.

Over-explaining visual decisions. Interviewers care more about why you made a choice than how it looks. Spend more time on the reasoning, the trade-offs, and the alternatives you considered.

Avoiding the failure question. Candidates who pivot away from genuine failures and describe a 'challenge that turned into a win' tend to come across as unself-aware. Pick a real failure and show clearly what you changed as a result.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the TripAdvisor Product Designer interview typically have?

Candidates report a process that typically includes a recruiter screen, a portfolio review with the hiring manager, a design challenge or take-home, and a final panel with cross-functional stakeholders. Some candidates also report a separate values or culture conversation. The exact structure can vary by team and seniority level.

Is there a take-home design assignment?

Many candidates report receiving a take-home prompt or an in-session design exercise. Prompts are typically travel-adjacent and focus on improving an existing flow rather than designing from scratch. Candidates suggest spending no more than three to four hours on take-home tasks and being explicit about trade-offs and what you would do with more time.

If your background is in fintech, e-commerce, or another high-intent vertical, draw explicit parallels to travel to show domain adaptability.
