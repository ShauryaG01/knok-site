01 Overview

Overview

LSEG (London Stock Exchange Group) is one of the world's leading financial markets infrastructure companies, building products that power trading, settlement, and market data for banks, brokers, and institutional investors worldwide. Their engineering centres in India handle significant product development, including real-time dashboards, analytics platforms, and data visualisation tools that financial professionals rely on every day.

With 129 open Frontend Engineer roles at LSEG on knok jobradar as of July 2026, the company is clearly scaling its engineering teams fast. Frontend work here is not typical web development: you are dealing with live price feeds, order books, risk visualisations, and high-frequency data updates where performance and accuracy matter as much as design.

Candidates report the process typically runs across three to four rounds: an initial HR or recruiter screen, one or two technical rounds covering coding and UI design, and a final round with a hiring manager or senior engineer. The exact format varies by team and seniority level. Expect questions that test both React and JavaScript depth, and your understanding of why performance matters in financial products.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How do you optimise a React application rendering a large, frequently-updating dataset?

*Situation:* At my previous company we had a portfolio dashboard that refreshed holdings data every few seconds for a list of hundreds of instruments. The page was noticeably janky on mid-range laptops.

*Task:* I was asked to investigate and fix the performance issues before the product launch.

*Action:* I started by profiling with React DevTools to find which components were re-rendering unnecessarily. I wrapped expensive child components in React.memo, moved derived calculations into useMemo, and replaced a full list re-render with a virtualised list using react-window. I also batched incoming WebSocket updates with a short debounce so the UI would not re-render on every individual tick.

*Result:* Frame rate improved noticeably and the jank was gone. The product shipped on time and internal beta testers gave positive feedback on smoothness.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you refactored a legacy component without breaking existing functionality.

*Situation:* Our team inherited a class-based React component written years earlier. It handled both data fetching and rendering for a complex form, had no tests, and was very difficult to read.

*Task:* My job was to rewrite it as a functional component with hooks while keeping the form behaviour identical for users.

*Action:* I first wrote a set of integration tests against the existing component to capture its actual behaviour, not what I assumed it should do. Then I rewrote it incrementally, running the tests after each step. I kept the old component in place behind a feature flag until the new one was validated in staging.

*Result:* The migration shipped without a single production bug. The new component was much easier to read and the test coverage gave the team confidence to iterate on it quickly afterward.

---

Q: Describe how you collaborate with backend engineers when an API contract changes mid-sprint.

*Situation:* During a sprint, the backend team told us they needed to change the shape of a market data response object because of a new data source they were integrating.

*Task:* I had to keep frontend progress moving without blocking on the backend, and ensure the final integration was smooth.

*Action:* I set up a mock API using MSW (Mock Service Worker) that mirrored the new agreed-upon contract. My team continued building against the mock while the backend made their changes. I also updated our shared API types in TypeScript and flagged every place in the frontend that would break, giving us a clear checklist for integration day.

*Result:* When the real backend was ready, integration took less than a day because we had already resolved all type mismatches and edge cases against the mock.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For technical 'how do you approach X' questions, use a three-part structure: state your default approach, explain the trade-offs you consider, then give a real example. LSEG interviewers are engineers themselves and appreciate that you understand why a solution works, not just that it works.

For performance questions specifically, frame your answer around: measure first (profiling, not guessing), fix the biggest bottleneck, then verify. Saying 'I start with the React profiler or browser performance tools' signals that you are systematic rather than reactive.

For behavioural questions, use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) but keep Situation and Task very brief. LSEG interviewers typically want to hear your specific Actions and the measurable or observable Result, not a long backstory. Give a sentence or two on Situation and Task combined, then use the rest of your answer on Action and Result.

For system and architecture design questions, start by clarifying the constraints: 'Are we designing for a single team or multiple teams consuming this? Is real-time data a requirement?' LSEG's products often involve both, so showing awareness of these constraints signals that you understand the domain.

For 'why LSEG' questions, connect your answer to the specific technical challenge of building for financial markets: complexity, performance requirements, regulated environment, and scale. Generic answers about 'a great company' will not stand out.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

LSEG frontend teams are building tools used by financial professionals, so interviewers look for a specific combination of skills and mindset.

Deep React and JavaScript fundamentals. Not just 'I know React', but a genuine understanding of the rendering model, reconciliation, hooks internals, and where the framework can become a bottleneck. Senior roles will probe this hard.

Performance instinct. Because market data moves fast, any engineer who cannot reason about render performance, network waterfalls, or bundle size is a risk. Candidates who can cite specific profiling techniques get noticed.

Comfort with complexity. Financial dashboards are not simple CRUD apps. Interviewers want to see that you are comfortable with multi-source data, complex state, and UIs that need to stay responsive under load.

Communication with cross-functional teams. LSEG engineering teams work closely with product managers, designers, and backend engineers across different locations. Candidates who describe collaboration in concrete terms (shared API contracts, design system contributions, sprint handoffs) do better than those who only talk about code.

Security and compliance awareness. Even at the frontend level, LSEG operates in regulated markets. Knowing about XSS prevention, Content Security Policy, secure cookie handling, and the basics of data protection shows you understand the environment.

Ownership mindset. Interviewers consistently look for people who take a problem to completion, flag risks early, and do not wait to be told what to do next.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Core technical revision

Revisit React fundamentals: the virtual DOM, reconciliation, the rules of hooks, and common performance patterns including memoisation, lazy loading, and code splitting. Practise explaining these out loud, not just implementing them. Work through several medium-difficulty JavaScript problems focussed on arrays, closures, and async patterns.

Week 2: LSEG domain prep

Read about what LSEG builds: trading platforms, market data feeds, and post-trade services. You do not need deep finance knowledge, but understanding terms like 'real-time price feed', 'order book', and 'risk dashboard' will help you ask smart questions and connect your experience to their context. Look up LSEG's publicly available developer documentation and any engineering blog posts they have shared.

Week 3: System design and architecture

Practise designing a component library from scratch, a micro-frontend shell, and a real-time data dashboard. For each, be ready to explain your decisions around state management, data flow, and performance trade-offs. Review common accessibility patterns (ARIA roles, keyboard navigation, focus management) since these come up for professional-grade tools.

Week 4: Behavioural prep and mock interviews

Write down a set of stories from your own work using the STAR format: one for performance improvement, one for refactoring, one for cross-team collaboration, and one for a mistake you made and what you learned. Do at least two mock interviews with a peer or using an interview prep tool, and review your stories for conciseness.

Ongoing: Check LSEG's open roles regularly. The job description often reveals which frameworks, tools, or domain areas the specific team cares about most.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Not connecting technical skills to the financial domain. Saying 'I am good at React' without any awareness of what makes LSEG's context different (real-time data, regulated environment, high-stakes UIs) misses an easy opportunity to stand out.

Guessing at performance fixes without profiling. If an interviewer asks how you would fix a slow component, jumping straight to 'I would add useMemo everywhere' is a red flag. Always start with 'I would measure first.'

Vague answers to behavioural questions. Saying 'I worked on a challenging project and we succeeded' is not useful. Be specific about what the problem was, what you personally did, and what the outcome was.

Ignoring accessibility and security. Many candidates skip these because they feel peripheral. At LSEG, they are not. Even a basic answer on XSS prevention or keyboard accessibility is better than silence.

Not asking good questions. Candidates who ask nothing, or ask only about salary and perks, leave a weaker impression. Ask about the team's tech stack, how they handle breaking changes across product teams, or what the biggest frontend challenge is right now.

Treating all rounds the same. A coding round needs you to think out loud as you go. A design round needs you to ask clarifying questions before diving in. A behavioural round needs structured stories. Prepare for the format, not just the content.

How many rounds does the LSEG Frontend Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically involves three to four rounds: a recruiter or HR screen, one or two technical rounds, and a final round with a hiring manager or senior engineer. The technical rounds typically cover React and JavaScript depth, a coding problem, and sometimes a UI or system design exercise. The exact structure varies by team and the seniority of the role you are applying for.

Does LSEG ask DSA (data structures and algorithms) questions for Frontend Engineer roles?

Candidates report that LSEG's frontend interviews focus more on practical UI and JavaScript problems than on classic competitive-programming DSA. You may encounter array manipulation, async patterns, or event-handling problems written in JavaScript. That said, a solid understanding of time complexity helps when explaining why you chose one approach over another, so a light review is worthwhile.

What frontend tech stack does LSEG use?

Publicly available job descriptions and engineering posts from LSEG commonly cite React and TypeScript, along with various state management and data visualisation libraries for charting financial data. Because LSEG has many product teams across different business lines, the exact stack can vary. It is worth asking in the interview which specific tools the team you would be joining uses day to day.

Is it worth applying to LSEG if I do not have a finance background?

Yes. LSEG hires engineers to build software, and deep finance knowledge is not a requirement for most frontend roles. What matters is that you understand the context well enough to ask sensible questions: why performance matters, what real-time data means for a UI, and why security and compliance are taken seriously.
