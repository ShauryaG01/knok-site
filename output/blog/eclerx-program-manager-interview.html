01 Overview

Overview

eClerx is a knowledge process outsourcing firm that delivers digital operations, analytics, and data-driven services to global clients in financial services, cable, and telecom. A Technical Program Manager here sits at the intersection of client delivery, engineering coordination, and process governance. You are not just managing sprints internally. You are the face of delivery to demanding client stakeholders who hold you to contractual SLAs.

Candidates report that the process typically runs 3-4 rounds: an HR screening, a technical or domain discussion, a behavioral panel or case round, and a final leadership conversation. Interviewers probe deeply on stakeholder management, cross-team coordination, risk thinking, and metrics-driven delivery.

With 152 open roles at eClerx as of mid-2026, the company is actively hiring across locations. Competition is real, but so is the opportunity. This guide covers the questions most commonly reported, frameworks that land well, and the mistakes that trip up otherwise strong candidates.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These are the questions candidates most commonly report encountering in eClerx TPM interviews. Prepare a concrete answer for each one before you walk in.

  1. How do you manage a program when client expectations and internal delivery timelines are in conflict?
  2. Walk us through a time you handled scope creep on a multi-team project. What did you do and what was the outcome?
  3. eClerx serves clients with strict SLA requirements. How do you track and report program health to both internal leads and the client?
  4. Describe your experience with offshore or distributed teams. How did you handle time zone gaps and communication breakdowns?
  5. How do you prioritize when multiple business units or clients are competing for the same delivery resources?
  6. Tell me about a time you spotted a program risk early. How did you mitigate it before it escalated?
  7. eClerx works with financial services clients who are compliance-driven. How do you factor regulatory or audit requirements into your program planning?
  8. How do you build a RACI or ownership model when roles are unclear across client-side and vendor-side teams?
  9. Describe a situation where a key technical dependency slipped. How did you keep the program on track?
  10. What metrics do you use to measure program success, and how do you present them to senior leadership?
  11. How do you handle it when your engineering lead disagrees with a timeline you have already committed to the client?
  12. Tell me about a time you drove process improvement across a delivery team. What changed and what was the measurable result?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How do you manage a program when client expectations and internal delivery timelines are in conflict?

*Situation:* At my previous company, we were running an analytics migration for a financial services client. The client wanted the first phase live in 8 weeks. The engineering team estimated 14 weeks at minimum.

*Task:* As the TPM, I needed to close that gap without damaging the client relationship or setting the team up to fail by committing to an unrealistic date.

*Action:* I ran a scope decomposition session with the engineering lead and identified a core feature set that could ship in 10 weeks with no quality shortcuts. I built a phased roadmap showing client value delivered at each checkpoint rather than an all-or-nothing delivery. I presented this to the client with transparent trade-off language: 'here is what you get at week 10, and here is what follows in phase 2.'

*Result:* The client accepted the phased plan. We delivered phase 1 on time with no critical defects, and the collaborative approach to planning led to an expanded scope agreement in the follow-on phase.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you spotted a program risk early and mitigated it before it escalated.

*Situation:* I was managing a data platform program for a telecom client. A third-party API integration sat on the critical path as a non-negotiable dependency.

*Task:* My job was to ensure that vendor dependency did not become a blocker to our go-live.

*Action:* In sprint 2, I noticed that the vendor had not yet confirmed sandbox credentials despite repeated email requests. I escalated through our account manager, set up a weekly call with the vendor's project lead, and worked with our engineering team to build a mock-data layer so development could continue without live API access.

*Result:* The credentials arrived 3 weeks late. Because of the mock-data fallback, our program timeline was unaffected. The client specifically called out risk management as a strength in the project retrospective.

---

Q: How do you build clear ownership when roles are ambiguous across client-side and vendor-side teams?

*Situation:* I was managing an operations automation program where both the client's internal IT team and our delivery team were contributing to the same workstreams. Nobody had agreed on who owned what, and early escalations were going in circles.

*Task:* I needed to establish clarity without creating friction between the two sides.

*Action:* In week 2, I facilitated a RACI-mapping workshop with representatives from both sides. We documented every deliverable, assigned Responsible and Accountable owners, and got written sign-off from both teams. I then embedded the RACI into the weekly status report so it stayed visible rather than sitting in a document nobody opened.

*Result:* Escalations dropped noticeably within the first month. Both sides knew what they owned, and the program moved to green status within 6 weeks of the RACI going live.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioral questions. Every 'tell me about a time' question needs a clear Situation, Task, Action, and Result. Keep Situation and Task brief. Spend most of your time on Action (what you personally did, step by step) and Result (a concrete outcome, not just 'it went well').

RAID thinking for risk and dependency questions. When asked about managing risks, show that you track Risks, Assumptions, Issues, and Dependencies in a structured log. Name the log, explain how often you review it, and say who receives updates. This signals operational maturity, not just familiarity with the concept.

Stakeholder mapping for escalation questions. When asked how you handle disagreements or pressure, show that you know who the decision-makers are, who the influencers are, and who gets impacted. Name your communication cadence: 'I send a weekly status to the client lead and hold a fortnightly review with the program sponsor.'

Metrics framing for program health questions. Lead with the metric, then the baseline, then the target, then the actual result. For example: 'We tracked on-time delivery rate against the SLA in the client contract. In the first quarter we were slightly below target. By quarter 3 we were consistently above it.' Tie metrics to outcomes the client cared about, not just process compliance.

MECE scope thinking for prioritization questions. When asked how you prioritize competing demands, show that you can break the problem into non-overlapping buckets (client impact, technical risk, regulatory urgency) and make a defensible call based on that structure rather than gut feel.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Client-first thinking. eClerx is a services company. Every program exists to deliver something to an external client. Interviewers want to see that you treat client satisfaction as a hard constraint, not a secondary consideration. Your stories should feature client interactions, not just internal coordination.

Structured delivery under pressure. SLAs at eClerx clients are often contractual. Interviewers look for candidates who run tight delivery cycles, catch slippage early, and communicate proactively rather than waiting for problems to surface in a weekly status call.

Cross-functional coordination. TPMs at eClerx typically bridge onshore client teams, offshore delivery teams, and internal technical leads. Show that you have managed multi-geography, multi-function programs and that you have a communication system that scales across time zones.

Process ownership. eClerx operates in compliance-heavy domains. Interviewers want to see that you understand process governance, can build and maintain delivery documentation, and know how to run a RACI or change-control process without over-engineering it.

Data-driven communication. Vague status updates do not work in client-facing delivery. Interviewers want to see that you report in concrete terms and that you can translate technical status into business language a client sponsor can act on.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Know the company first. Read eClerx's publicly available service line descriptions and investor communications. Understand the verticals they serve: financial services, cable, and telecom. Know what digital operations and analytics delivery means in a KPO context. This framing shapes every answer you give in the interview.

Build your story bank. Write out 6-8 STAR stories from your experience that cover: scope conflict, risk escalation, stakeholder disagreement, cross-team ownership, process improvement, and client communication under pressure. One story can serve multiple questions if you angle the Action section to match the theme being asked about.

Practice program management concepts. Revisit RACI, RAID logs, SLA tracking, change-control processes, and program status reporting formats. You do not need to recite theory. You need to speak about these tools as things you actually use, not things you have heard of.

Practice out loud. Record yourself answering the 12 questions in this guide. Watch for filler words, vague Actions ('I coordinated with the team'), and Results that trail off without a concrete outcome. Fix these before the real interview.

Review the JD before each round. Candidates report that eClerx interviewers often tie their questions directly to the listed responsibilities. Map your STAR stories to the specific duties in the JD and reference those duties by name when you answer.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating it like a product company interview. eClerx is a services business. Answers that focus only on internal roadmaps, product metrics, or user growth without any client-delivery angle miss the mark. Reframe your examples to include external client stakeholders wherever possible.

Being vague about what you personally did. In program management roles, it is easy to say 'we delivered' or 'the team achieved.' eClerx interviewers look for 'I' actions: what did you decide, what did you escalate, what process did you put in place. Passive answers suggest you were a coordinator, not a program owner.

Skipping the result. Many candidates give strong Situation and Action answers but trail off on the outcome. Even a partial result stated honestly, with a recovery story, is stronger than a vague 'it went well.' Quantify where you can, and if you cannot, describe the business impact in plain terms.

Not asking good questions. Candidates report that failing to ask about the client engagement model, team geography, or delivery methodology signals low interest in the role. Prepare 2-3 genuine questions about how the TPM interacts with clients and with offshore delivery teams.

Over-indexing on tools, under-indexing on judgment. Naming Jira or MS Project is not a differentiator. What sets strong candidates apart is showing that they knew when to escalate, when to push back, and when to absorb pressure and keep the team moving.
