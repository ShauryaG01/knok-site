03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk me through a system you designed or contributed to.

*Situation:* At my previous company, our notification service was sending duplicate messages to users during peak load, which hurt user trust.
*Task:* I was asked to identify the root cause and redesign the service to be reliable at scale.
*Action:* I analysed logs and found our retry logic lacked idempotency keys. I proposed a deduplication layer using Redis, wrote a design document, shared it with the team for review, and led the implementation across two sprints.
*Result:* Duplicate notifications dropped to near zero and the team adopted the idempotency pattern for two other services afterward.

---

Q: Describe a time you disagreed with a technical decision.

*Situation:* My team decided to adopt a monorepo structure mid-project. I felt it would slow our CI pipeline significantly given our codebase size.
*Task:* I needed to raise the concern constructively without blocking progress.
*Action:* I prepared a short comparison of build times from a similar migration, cited publicly reported benchmarks for teams of our size, and proposed a trial on one module before full adoption.
*Result:* The team agreed to the trial. We confirmed the build time concern was valid for our setup and adopted a hybrid approach that kept the benefits without the slowdown.

---

Q: Tell me about a project where you improved performance.

*Situation:* Our search API was timing out on large datasets and users were abandoning the feature.
*Task:* I owned the investigation and fix within a two-week sprint.
*Action:* I profiled the queries, found missing database indexes and an N+1 query pattern, added the indexes, and rewrote the fetch logic to use batch loading.
*Result:* Average query time dropped sharply, timeouts stopped, and the feature saw a clear uptick in usage according to our analytics dashboard.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the most reliable framework for behavioral questions. Keep your Situation brief (one or two sentences), spend the most time on Action describing what you specifically did, and make your Result concrete even if you cannot share exact figures.

For system design questions, structure your answer in order: clarify requirements first, then capacity assumptions if relevant, then component design, then trade-offs. Interviewers want to see you ask questions before jumping to solutions.

For debugging questions, a structured walk-through works well: reproduce the issue, isolate the component, form a hypothesis, test it, fix and verify. Opening with 'I would start by checking logs and metrics' signals good instincts immediately.

For 'tell me about yourself', a crisp three-part structure helps: your current role and stack, a key project or achievement, and why you are interested in this role at sarvm. Aim for around two minutes.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Candidates who have interviewed at companies like sarvm consistently report that interviewers prioritise a few things above raw algorithmic speed.

Clear thinking out loud. Interviewers want to follow your reasoning. Narrating your approach, even briefly, shows collaborative problem-solving and is valued over silent typing.

Ownership language. Use 'I' rather than 'we' when describing your contribution. It is fine to mention the team context, but be specific about what you personally did.

Trade-off awareness. For system design questions, no answer is perfect. Saying 'this approach works well for read-heavy loads but would need rethinking for write-heavy traffic' signals engineering maturity.

Genuine curiosity. Preparing a few thoughtful questions about the team's current challenges, tech stack choices, or onboarding process leaves a strong impression and shows real interest in the role.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Technical foundations.
Focus on data structures and algorithms using a practice platform of your choice. Work through problems on arrays, hashmaps, trees, and graphs. Revisit time and space complexity analysis so you can explain it clearly, not just calculate it.

Week 2: System design and behavioral prep.
Study common system design patterns: caching, message queues, database sharding, and API design. For behavioral questions, write out two or three STAR stories covering ownership, conflict resolution, and a key technical challenge. Practice saying them aloud rather than just writing them.

Final days: Company research and readiness.
Read recent news about sarvm, look at their product and engineering blog if available, and note anything you find genuinely interesting. Prepare a few thoughtful questions for your interviewer based on what you learn. Do a mock interview with a friend or record yourself to catch filler words and pacing issues.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping to code without clarifying requirements. Interviewers frequently report that candidates start typing the moment a problem is stated. Spend a moment asking about constraints, edge cases, and expected input size first.

Vague answers to behavioral questions. Saying 'I always communicate well with my team' is not a story. Use a specific situation with a concrete outcome.

Ignoring time and space complexity. Even if your solution works, not discussing complexity signals a gap in fundamentals. Always follow your solution with a complexity analysis.

Not asking questions. Ending every round with 'no, I think I am good' is a missed opportunity. Prepare a few genuine questions in advance.

Overlooking soft skills in a technical role. sarvm, like most product companies, cares about how you collaborate and communicate, not just whether you can solve problems in isolation.
