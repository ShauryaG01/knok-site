01 Overview

Overview

As a Data Analyst at WebMD, you would typically work on health content performance, user engagement metrics, ad revenue reporting, and sometimes clinical data analysis. 02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

  1. Walk me through how you would investigate a sudden drop in traffic to a key health article on WebMD.
  2. Write a SQL query to find the top articles by unique page views over the last month, broken down by health category.
  3. How would you define and measure the success of a new symptom checker feature?
  4. Describe a time when your data analysis directly changed a product or business decision.
  5. How do you handle missing, duplicate, or inconsistent records in a health-related dataset?
  6. What metrics would you use to evaluate the performance of a sponsored health content campaign?
  7. Two dashboards show different totals for the same KPI. How do you find the root cause and resolve it?
  8. How would you design an A/B test to compare two article recommendation algorithms?
  9. Tell me about a time you explained a complex data finding to a non-technical stakeholder.
  10. How do you prioritize competing data requests when multiple teams need analysis at the same time?
  11. What do you know about data privacy considerations for health platforms, and how would those shape your analysis?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time when your data analysis directly changed a business decision.

*Situation:* The marketing team was planning to significantly increase spend on a campaign targeting a popular symptom-search keyword, based on the assumption that it was driving high-value user sign-ups.

*Task:* I was asked to validate whether that keyword was actually converting users at the rate the team believed.

*Action:* I pulled session-level data from our analytics platform, joined it with sign-up and retention tables using SQL, and segmented users by their first-touch keyword. I built a funnel report comparing conversion and 30-day retention across keyword groups.

*Result:* The data showed that users from that keyword had much lower retention than users from our other high-spend keywords, even though initial conversion looked acceptable. The team shifted budget to a higher-retention keyword cluster. That analysis became a standard pre-campaign check on our team.

---

Q: How do you handle missing or inconsistent data in a health dataset?

*Situation:* I was building a monthly report on appointment completion rates for a healthcare analytics project. The source data had a commonly cited problem in health IT: record gaps that appeared when patients used different registration channels.

*Task:* I needed to deliver a reliable metric even with imperfect raw data.

*Action:* I first audited the extent of the problem: which fields were affected, whether the pattern was random or tied to a specific data source, and how much of the total volume was impacted. I flagged the findings to the data engineering team and documented them clearly. For the immediate report, I excluded records with missing key identifiers and added a plain-language data quality note to the report itself.

*Result:* Stakeholders received the report with full context about its limitations. That transparency led to a permanent fix in the data pipeline within the following quarter, and the data quality note became a template for similar reports.

---

Q: How would you design an A/B test for a new article recommendation algorithm?

*Situation:* A product team wanted to test a new recommendation engine showing related health articles at the end of each page, before committing to a full rollout.

*Task:* I needed to design a statistically sound test and define success criteria upfront.

*Action:* I defined the primary metric (click-through rate on recommended articles) and secondary metrics (session depth and return visits). I ran a sample size calculation based on baseline traffic to set a minimum run time. I proposed a random user-level split to avoid contamination and agreed on guardrail metrics with the team so we could stop early if key satisfaction signals dropped unexpectedly.

*Result:* The test ran cleanly with no significant carryover effects. The new algorithm showed a meaningfully higher click-through rate, and the team had the data they needed to make a confident rollout decision with clear stakeholder buy-in.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For SQL and technical questions: Think out loud before you write. Candidates report that WebMD interviewers value your reasoning process as much as the final query. State your assumptions (for example, whether a given ID is unique per session), write the query, then explain how you would validate the output before handing it to a stakeholder.

For product and metric questions (define success, measure a feature): Use a three-part structure. First, clarify the goal of the feature. Second, name your primary metric and one or two guardrail metrics. Third, explain how you would slice the data by user segment, device, or region to find what is actually driving the number.

For conflict or ambiguity questions (two dashboards disagree, data is messy): Show a structured debugging mindset. Start at the source: check metric definitions, filters, and time ranges used in each report. Work downstream from data source to transformation to visualization. Name the exact questions you would ask before escalating to engineering.

For behavioral questions: Use STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result), but keep Situation and Task brief. Spend most of your time on the Action (what you specifically did, which tools you used) and the Result (what changed because of your work, and what you learned from it).

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

WebMD interviewers, candidates report, are looking for analysts who combine technical ability with awareness of what makes health data uniquely demanding. A few qualities that come up repeatedly:

Comfort with ambiguous metrics. Health content performance is harder to define than e-commerce conversion. Interviewers want to see that you can propose a sensible metric when none is handed to you and defend your reasoning.

Data quality mindset. Health data often arrives incomplete or inconsistently formatted across sources. Showing that you audit before you analyse, and that you communicate gaps clearly to stakeholders, signals analytical maturity.

Stakeholder communication. WebMD teams span product, clinical, editorial, and ad sales. You will be expected to translate complex findings for non-technical audiences. Prepare a concrete example where you simplified a result without losing the key insight.

SQL fluency. SQL is central to this role. Be ready to write window functions, CTEs, and multi-table joins from scratch during the interview, not just describe them in the abstract.

Curiosity about healthcare. You do not need a clinical background, but showing genuine interest in how health data affects real users stands out. Candidates who have clearly spent time on WebMD's own product before the interview are noticed.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Two weeks before: SQL and technical foundations
Practice writing SQL with window functions, CTEs, and multi-table joins. Focus on aggregation and filtering patterns common in web analytics. Revisit session attribution, funnel analysis, and cohort retention queries, as these map directly to WebMD's analytics work.

One week before: Product and domain knowledge
Spend time on WebMD's own product: browse the symptom checker, article pages, and their ad-supported content. Think about what metrics you would track for each surface. Look for publicly reported case studies on health data analytics to build vocabulary around the domain without needing clinical expertise.

Three to five days before: Behavioral prep and mock practice
Write out 4-5 STAR stories from your own experience covering: a data quality problem you solved, a time you influenced a decision with data, and a time you worked with a non-technical stakeholder. Practice saying them out loud. Do at least two mock SQL rounds where you explain your logic as you type, not after.

Day before: Final check
Confirm the number of rounds and format with your recruiter. Prepare two or three specific questions about the team's current data stack and the biggest analytical problems they are working on. This signals genuine interest and gives you useful context going in.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping into SQL without stating assumptions. Many candidates write the query first and explain later. Starting by restating the problem and naming your assumptions prevents mistakes and signals structured thinking to the interviewer.

Defining vague success metrics. When asked how you would measure a feature, saying 'engagement' or 'user satisfaction' is too broad. Always name a specific, measurable metric and explain how you would collect it.

Skipping data quality checks. If a case question gives you a dataset, candidates who analyse it without questioning its completeness or source reliability miss an important signal. Audit first, analyse second.

Not connecting analysis to business impact. Interviewers want to know what changed because of your work, not just what you found. End every STAR story with a concrete outcome or a decision your analysis influenced.

Over-explaining the technical setup and rushing the result. In behavioral interviews, the result and the learning are what leave an impression. Do not spend most of your answer on tools and very little on what actually happened.

Forgetting data privacy in a health context. Health data carries privacy expectations that general web analytics does not. Failing to mention user data sensitivity at least once during the interview can read as a meaningful gap in domain awareness.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the WebMD Data Analyst interview typically have?

Candidates report a process of 2-3 rounds, typically starting with a recruiter screening call. This is usually followed by a technical round covering SQL and possibly a case study or take-home exercise. A final round with the hiring manager or a small panel is common. Confirm the exact format with your recruiter, as it can vary by team and location.

Is healthcare domain knowledge required for this role?

You do not need a clinical background, but candidates report that showing genuine curiosity about health data gives you a clear edge. WebMD's analytics work spans content engagement, ad performance, and user behavior, so strong web analytics skills transfer well. Spending time on WebMD's own product before the interview helps you speak concretely about the domain without needing medical expertise.

What SQL topics should I focus on to prepare?

Candidates report that SQL questions at WebMD typically cover joins, aggregations, and filtering. Window functions (especially RANK, ROW_NUMBER, and running totals) and CTEs come up frequently. Funnel queries and cohort analysis are especially relevant given the product's focus on user journeys. Practice writing queries from scratch and explaining your logic as you go, not just presenting the final answer.

What tools or technologies should I be comfortable with for this role?

SQL is the most critical skill, and you should be comfortable writing complex queries from scratch under interview conditions. Candidates report that experience with visualization tools such as Tableau or Power BI is valued for dashboard and reporting work. Familiarity with Python or R for analysis beyond SQL is a plus, and some exposure to web analytics platforms is useful given WebMD's digital and advertising business.
