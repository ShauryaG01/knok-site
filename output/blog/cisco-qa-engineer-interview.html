02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Cisco QA interviews typically cover three areas: core testing fundamentals, automation and tooling, and domain knowledge relevant to networking products. Here are the questions candidates most commonly report:

  1. Walk me through your experience with test automation frameworks. Which ones have you used and why did you choose them?
  2. How do you approach testing network protocols such as TCP/IP, SNMP, or routing protocols?
  3. Describe a time you found a critical bug late in the release cycle. What did you do?
  4. How do you prioritise test cases when you have limited time before a release deadline?
  5. What is the difference between regression testing and smoke testing? When do you use each?
  6. How would you design a test plan for a completely new networking feature?
  7. Have you worked with CI/CD pipelines? Describe how QA fits into a continuous delivery workflow.
  8. How do you handle flaky tests in an automation suite?
  9. Describe your experience with API testing. How do you test a REST API end-to-end?
  10. Cisco products operate in complex multi-device environments. How do you set up and manage test environments for such scenarios?
  11. Tell me about a time you disagreed with a developer about whether something was a defect. How did you resolve it?
  12. How do you measure the effectiveness of a QA process? What metrics do you track?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Three STAR-format sample answers for common Cisco QA interview questions:

Q: Describe a time you found a critical bug late in the release cycle.

*Situation:* During the final regression phase before a product release at my previous company, I was running end-to-end tests on a network configuration module.

*Task:* My responsibility was to verify all high-priority test cases before sign-off could be given.

*Action:* I noticed intermittent packet drops in one specific VLAN configuration scenario that had not surfaced earlier in the cycle. I documented the exact reproduction steps, captured logs and environment details, and immediately escalated to the dev lead with a severity classification. I then reproduced the issue across a couple of additional test environments to confirm it was not isolated to my setup.

*Result:* The team confirmed it as a defect and the release was held for a targeted fix. The issue would have caused outages for customers running multi-VLAN configurations, so catching it before release saved significant post-deployment effort.

---

Q: Walk me through how you built or improved a test automation framework.

*Situation:* At my previous role, the team was manually testing a REST API suite with a large number of endpoints, making the regression cycle very slow.

*Task:* I was asked to evaluate and implement an automation solution that the whole QA team could maintain going forward.

*Action:* I chose Pytest with the Requests library because the team was already comfortable with Python. I built a modular structure with reusable fixtures, parameterised test cases for edge scenarios, and integrated the suite with the existing Jenkins pipeline. I also documented the setup so new team members could onboard without needing extra help.

*Result:* The team moved from manual regression taking most of a sprint to automated runs completing in a fraction of that time. Integration issues started being caught much earlier in each sprint, reducing the number of defects that reached the final regression phase.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you disagreed with a developer about whether something was a bug.

*Situation:* I had logged a defect where the UI showed an incorrect device status when a network switch went offline, but the developer marked it 'works as designed.'

*Task:* I needed to either accept that resolution with justification or make a clear case that it was a genuine defect affecting end users.

*Action:* I pulled up the product specification, which described the expected behaviour explicitly, and gathered feedback from a few customers who had independently reported confusion about the same status message. I brought both pieces of evidence to a short call with the developer and the product manager.

*Result:* The product manager agreed it was a defect, the ticket was reopened, and it was fixed in the next sprint. The situation reinforced for me how important clear specs and customer evidence are when escalating quality issues.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For behavioral questions, use the STAR structure: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task brief (one to two sentences each) and spend most of your answer on Action and Result. Cisco interviewers typically probe with follow-up questions, so have concrete details ready rather than generalised statements.

For 'how would you test X' questions, a reliable structure is: define the scope and requirements, list the testing types you would apply (functional, regression, performance, negative scenarios), describe how you would prioritise given constraints, and mention the tools or automation you would use. This approach shows structured thinking and practical awareness.

For tool and framework questions, follow this pattern: name the tool, explain why you chose it over alternatives, describe a real project where you used it, and mention any limitations you encountered. Candidates who can speak critically about tools tend to stand out compared to those who simply list names on a resume.

For disagreement or conflict questions, Cisco interviewers typically look for evidence that you can advocate for quality without being combative. Lead with the data or specification that informed your position, show that you listened to the other side, and end with a collaborative resolution rather than a story where you simply 'won.'

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Cisco QA interviews typically evaluate three things in parallel: domain knowledge relevant to networking and enterprise products, hands-on technical skills in testing and automation, and soft skills around communication and ownership.

Domain knowledge means being comfortable with the basics of how networks work. You do not need to be a network engineer, but familiarity with OSI layers, TCP/IP, common protocols such as DNS and SNMP, and how enterprise network devices are configured will help you connect your QA experience to the products Cisco builds.

Technical skills Cisco typically looks for include experience with at least one test automation framework (Selenium, Pytest, Rest Assured, Cypress), familiarity with API testing, understanding of CI/CD integration, and the ability to set up and manage test environments independently.

Ownership and communication are often the deciding factor between two technically similar candidates. Cisco's QA teams work closely with development and product managers on products used in critical infrastructure. Interviewers look for candidates who flag risks proactively, communicate defects clearly, and take responsibility for quality outcomes rather than simply executing assigned test cases.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Testing fundamentals. Review core concepts including test design techniques (equivalence partitioning, boundary value analysis, exploratory testing), the defect lifecycle, and how QA fits into Agile sprints. Be ready to explain the difference between smoke, sanity, regression, and performance testing in your own words.

Week 2: Networking basics. For a Cisco QA role, expect questions on the OSI model, TCP/IP, common protocols, and how network devices behave under different conditions. Focus on vocabulary and concepts at a level where you can relate them to testing scenarios, even if you have not worked on networking products before.

Week 3: Hands-on automation practice. Pick the framework you know best and prepare two or three real examples. Be ready to discuss your test structure, how you handled flaky tests, how you integrated with a CI pipeline, and what you would do differently with more time.

Week 4: Mock interviews and STAR prep. Write out STAR answers for five or six common scenarios: a critical defect you found, a conflict with a developer, a process improvement you drove, and a high-pressure release you managed. Practise saying them out loud. Candidates report that Cisco interviewers notice when answers sound rehearsed versus genuinely drawn from experience.

On the day: Prepare two or three genuine questions for each interviewer about the team's testing stack, how QA and development collaborate, and what the biggest quality challenges are for the product you would work on. Thoughtful questions signal real interest in the role.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

1. Listing tools without depth. Mentioning Selenium or Jira without a real example is not enough. Cisco interviewers typically follow up on anything you list, so be ready to go deeper on every tool you claim experience with.

2. Vague defect examples. When describing bugs you have found, be specific: what was the severity, what were the reproduction steps, what was the business impact. Saying 'I found a bug and the developer fixed it' does not demonstrate QA thinking.

3. Underestimating networking knowledge. Candidates from non-networking backgrounds often skip this preparation area. Even a basic understanding of how packets move through a network can set you apart in a Cisco interview.

4. Missing measurable results in STAR answers. Where you can, tie your outcomes to something concrete: faster regression cycles, fewer defects reaching production, shorter feedback loops. Use only figures you can genuinely support and explain.

5. Treating behavioral rounds as less important. Cisco typically includes at least one round focused on teamwork, collaboration, and how you handle disagreement. Prepare for these as seriously as the technical rounds.

6. Not researching the specific product team. Cisco has QA roles across networking, security, collaboration, and cloud products. Find out which business unit you are interviewing for and connect your examples to that domain wherever possible.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does a Cisco QA Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report that the Cisco QA interview process typically involves three to five rounds. This usually includes an initial HR screening, one or two technical rounds covering testing fundamentals and automation, and a managerial or behavioral round. Some teams also include a practical coding or test-design exercise. The exact structure can vary by product team and level.

Do I need networking knowledge to pass a Cisco QA interview?

Having some networking knowledge is a clear advantage for most Cisco QA roles, since the products you would test are networking and enterprise infrastructure products. You do not need to be a network engineer, but being comfortable with concepts like OSI layers, TCP/IP, and common protocols will help you connect your QA skills to the domain. Candidates who can frame test scenarios in terms of network behaviour tend to stand out in the technical rounds.

What automation skills does Cisco look for in QA engineers?

Cisco QA roles typically expect experience with at least one automation framework such as Selenium, Pytest, Rest Assured, or Cypress. API testing skills are commonly required, as is familiarity with integrating test suites into CI/CD pipelines. Being able to write and maintain automation code independently, rather than just running scripts written by others, is especially important for mid and senior-level roles.

What salary can I expect as a QA Engineer at Cisco in India?

Based on knok job radar data for the broader Indian QA market, typical ranges are 4-9 LPA for entry level (0-2 years), 9-17 LPA for mid level (3-5 years), 17-30 LPA for senior roles (6-9 years), and 28-45+ LPA for lead positions. Cisco-specific compensation may differ based on the product team, location, and your skills profile. For current Cisco-specific figures, Glassdoor and levels.fyi are useful references.

How should I prepare for the behavioral round at Cisco?

Prepare five or six STAR-format stories covering key scenarios: a critical defect you found, a time you disagreed with a developer, a process improvement you drove, and a high-pressure release you managed. Cisco interviewers typically look for evidence that you take ownership of quality outcomes, communicate clearly under pressure, and collaborate well with development teams. Practise saying your answers out loud so they sound natural rather than rehearsed.
