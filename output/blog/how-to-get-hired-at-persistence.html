01 Hiring Overview

Hiring Overview

03 Interview Process

Interview Process

Here is a stage-by-stage look at how Persistence typically hires. Rounds can be added, merged, or skipped depending on the specific role and the hiring manager involved.

StageWhat HappensFormat
Resume ScreeningRecruiter or applicant tracking system checks your profile against the job descriptionAsync

Resume Screening: Recruiters at Persistence scan for keywords that match the job description closely. Tailor your resume to each application rather than sending one generic document across every role.

Online Assessment: Both freshers and some lateral hires face a timed test covering aptitude, reasoning, and technical basics. Practise on platforms that offer timed mock tests to build your speed and accuracy before the real thing.

Technical Round 1: For engineers, expect questions on data structures, algorithms, SQL, and your primary framework or language. For domain roles, scenario-based questions tied to the job function are common. Be ready to walk through past projects clearly and concisely.

Technical Round 2: Senior candidates are often asked to discuss architectural decisions, design trade-offs, or how they would approach a complex problem from scratch. Real examples from your work history land better than textbook answers.

Managerial Round: The focus here shifts to how you handle pressure, deadlines, and working across teams. Prepare a few structured examples covering situations where you took initiative or navigated a difficult delivery.

HR Round: Compensation expectations, notice period, and relocation (if applicable) are discussed here. Know your current CTC and have a clear target in mind before the call to avoid unnecessary back-and-forth.

04 What They Look For

What They Look For

Persistence hires people who can deliver in project-driven, client-facing environments where timelines are real and accountability is expected. Here is what tends to separate shortlisted candidates from the rest.

Technical depth in your core stack: Whether you are a Java developer, a data engineer, or a QA specialist, interviewers expect you to go beyond surface knowledge. They want to see that you can solve real problems, not just recite definitions.

Clear communication: Persistence works on client-facing projects regularly. The ability to explain technical decisions in plain language, write clear documentation, and present your thinking confidently is valued at every seniority level.

Structured problem-solving: Interviewers pay attention to how you approach a problem, not just whether you reach the right answer. Thinking out loud, asking clarifying questions, and organising your reasoning before diving in are all positives.

Ownership and follow-through: In IT services, delivery timelines are binding. Candidates who demonstrate that they take responsibility for their tasks, flag risks early, and see work through to completion consistently stand out.

Adaptability: Project requirements change, tech stacks evolve, and client priorities shift. A genuine willingness to learn new tools and adjust course when needed is something hiring managers at services firms actively look for.

Relevant certifications (for senior roles): Cloud credentials (AWS, Azure, GCP), PMP, or domain-specific certifications can strengthen your profile meaningfully, particularly for consulting, architecture, or delivery management roles.

05 How To Prepare

How To Prepare

Start with the job description. Read it carefully and map every requirement to something concrete in your resume or portfolio. If you notice a gap, prepare an honest, forward-looking answer for how you are addressing it.

Revise your fundamentals. For technical roles, brush up on data structures, algorithms, SQL queries, and the core concepts of your primary language or framework. Seniority does not excuse you from basics in a services interview.

Prepare your project stories. Pick two or three projects where you had real ownership. For each, be ready to explain what the problem was, what you built or fixed, what challenges came up, and what the measurable outcome was. Keep answers focused and specific.

Practise coding out loud. If your role involves a coding round, practise solving problems while narrating your thought process. Interviewers want to follow your reasoning, not just see a final answer appear.

Research Persistence as a company. Understand the industries they serve, the technologies they focus on, and any publicly reported projects or partnerships. This signals genuine interest and helps you ask smart questions at the end of the interview.

Prepare two or three questions to ask. Thoughtful questions about the team, the project you would be joining, or growth paths signal engagement and seriousness. Avoid asking things easily found on their careers page.

Sort your documents early. Have your updated resume, educational certificates, experience letters, and ID documents ready well before the offer stage. Delays in background verification can slow down your onboarding significantly.

Know your compensation numbers. Be clear about your current CTC, expected CTC, and notice period before the HR round. Vague or inconsistent answers create friction at a stage where you want everything to move smoothly.

06 Culture And Values

Culture And Values

Persistence operates in the IT services space, where the work culture is delivery-focused and client-oriented. Projects often run against fixed timelines, so teams are expected to be organised, proactive about flagging risks, and dependable under pressure.

Collaboration across functions: Most work happens in cross-functional teams that include developers, testers, business analysts, and project managers. The ability to coordinate across roles and communicate clearly with non-technical stakeholders is part of everyday work, not an optional skill.

Continuous learning: The technology landscape in IT services shifts fast, and Persistence encourages certifications and internal training. Professionals who build a habit of upskilling tend to progress faster here than those who rely only on on-the-job experience.

Career progression: Like other mid-to-large IT services firms, Persistence offers structured growth paths. Moving from individual contributor to lead or managerial roles typically requires a combination of strong delivery performance and the ability to mentor or manage others.

Global client exposure: Projects can involve working with international clients, which may mean adjusted hours for calls or reviews across time zones. This is worth thinking through if work-life predictability is a top priority for you.

Values in practice: Integrity, accountability, and client focus appear consistently in Persistence's published materials and come up during interviews as themes. Expect situational questions about how you have handled difficult client moments, tight deadlines, or cross-team disagreements. Concrete examples matter far more than general statements here.
