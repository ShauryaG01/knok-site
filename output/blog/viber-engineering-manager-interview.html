03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a time you turned around a struggling engineering team.

*Situation:* I inherited a team of eight engineers that had missed its last two quarterly milestones. Morale was low, and two senior members were considering leaving.

*Task:* My goal was to stabilise the team, restore delivery predictability within one quarter, and retain the senior engineers.

*Action:* I started with one-on-one conversations to understand each person's frustrations. The core issues were unclear priorities and a lack of technical ownership. I worked with product to cut the backlog down to a focused set of deliverables, assigned clear domain ownership to each engineer, and introduced fortnightly retrospectives so problems surfaced early.

*Result:* Within one quarter, the team shipped all planned deliverables on time. Both senior engineers chose to stay, and team satisfaction scores improved significantly in the next internal survey.

---

Q: How do you prioritise technical debt against new feature development?

*Situation:* At my previous company, our payment service had accumulated significant technical debt. Response times were creeping up, and engineers dreaded working on that codebase.

*Task:* I needed to reduce debt enough to unblock feature velocity, without halting product delivery entirely.

*Action:* I categorised debt items by business impact (outage risk, developer slowdown, customer-facing latency). I negotiated with product leadership to allocate a fixed percentage of each sprint to debt reduction, focusing on the highest-impact items first. I also set up automated alerts so we could measure improvement week over week.

*Result:* Over two quarters, average API response time dropped noticeably, and the number of production incidents from that service fell. Feature delivery speed actually increased because engineers spent less time working around legacy code.

---

Q: Describe a situation where you had to make a difficult decision with incomplete data.

*Situation:* During a product launch, we discovered a subtle data inconsistency bug. We had limited time before the launch window closed, and reproducing the bug reliably was proving difficult.

*Task:* I had to decide whether to delay the launch or ship with a mitigation in place.

*Action:* I gathered the team for a rapid risk assessment. We identified that the bug affected a small subset of edge-case users and built a temporary validation layer to catch and correct inconsistencies at read time. I documented the known risk, communicated it transparently to the VP of Engineering, and set up monitoring dashboards to flag any recurrence.

*Result:* We launched on schedule. The validation layer caught a handful of affected records in the first week, all of which were corrected automatically. We then fixed the root cause in the following sprint with no customer impact.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

The STAR Method (your primary tool)

Every behavioural answer should follow: Situation (set the scene briefly), Task (what was your specific responsibility), Action (what *you* did, not the team), Result (measurable outcome). Keep each section to two or three sentences.

For System Design Questions

Viber operates at massive scale with real-time requirements, so interviewers care about your design instincts. Use this structure:

  • Clarify requirements: Ask about expected users, latency targets, and consistency needs before drawing anything.
  • High-level architecture: Start with a simple diagram (load balancers, application servers, databases, message queues).
  • Deep dive: Pick one component the interviewer is most interested in. Discuss trade-offs (SQL vs. NoSQL, push vs. pull, eventual vs. strong consistency).
  • Scale and reliability: Address sharding, replication, failover, and monitoring.

For Prioritisation and Strategy Questions

Use a simple framework: State the goal, list the trade-offs, explain your decision criteria, describe the outcome. Interviewers want to see structured thinking, not a 'correct' answer.

Quantify Everything

Wherever possible, attach numbers to your results: 'reduced deploy time from X minutes to Y minutes', 'cut on-call pages by half'. Concrete results are more convincing than vague improvements.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Technical depth paired with leadership maturity. Viber's product handles real-time communication at global scale. Interviewers typically want to see that you understand distributed systems, not just manage people who build them. Be ready to go deep on system design while also demonstrating you can coach, hire, and unblock teams.

Ownership mentality. Candidates report that Viber values managers who take end-to-end ownership of outcomes, from architecture decisions to delivery timelines to team health. Avoid answers that position you as a 'process facilitator' only.

Clear communication under ambiguity. As a messaging platform company, Viber's teams often operate across time zones and cultures. Interviewers typically look for candidates who communicate decisions clearly, escalate early, and document well.

Bias for action with calculated risk. Your answers should show you can move fast without being reckless. Describe how you assess risk, make reversible decisions quickly, and set up guardrails (monitoring, rollback plans) rather than waiting for perfect information.

Culture fit around collaboration. Viber is not a massive corporation, so Engineering Managers are expected to work closely with product, design, and data teams. Show that you enjoy cross-functional work, not just managing within engineering.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Weeks one and two: Research and foundations

Study Viber's product suite (messaging, calls, communities, payments). Understand the technical challenges of real-time communication: WebSockets, message delivery guarantees, encryption, and presence systems. Read Viber's engineering blog or any publicly available tech talks.

Week two: System design practice

Practice designing systems relevant to Viber: a chat messaging service, a notification delivery pipeline, a media storage and retrieval system. Focus on trade-offs at scale. Use publicly available system design resources and practice explaining your design decisions out loud.

Week three: Behavioural stories

Prepare eight to ten STAR stories covering: team turnarounds, conflict resolution, hiring decisions, technical trade-offs, cross-functional collaboration, delivery under pressure, and coaching underperformers. Map each story to the questions listed above.

Week four: Mock interviews and refinement

Do at least two mock interviews, one for system design and one for behavioural/leadership. Ask your mock interviewer to push back on vague answers. Refine your stories to be concise (each STAR answer should be under two minutes when spoken).

Throughout: Stay current

Follow Viber's recent product announcements and any public engineering updates. Mentioning a recent feature or technical initiative in your interview shows genuine interest.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Going too abstract on technical questions. Saying 'I would use microservices' without explaining why, how you would handle service discovery, or what your data consistency strategy would be. Viber interviewers typically want specifics.

Talking about the team but not yourself. 'We built X' is fine for context, but interviewers want to know what *you* decided, influenced, or unblocked. Use 'I' more than 'we' in behavioural answers.

Ignoring the product context. Viber is a real-time communication platform. If your system design ignores latency requirements or your leadership examples are all about batch-processing teams, you are missing the mark. Tailor your examples.

Over-preparing scripted answers. Interviewers can tell when you are reciting a memorised response. Know your stories well enough to tell them conversationally and handle follow-up questions.

Not asking questions back. The interview is a two-way conversation. Prepare thoughtful questions about Viber's engineering challenges, team structure, and technical roadmap. This signals genuine interest and seniority.

Underselling your salary expectations. Engineering Manager salaries range from 35-60 LPA at the Manager level to 90-150+ LPA at the Director level based on knok jobradar data. Research your market value before the compensation discussion.
