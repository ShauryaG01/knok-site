02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly in Purplle.com Software Engineer interviews, based on candidate reports and the nature of their product:

  1. How would you design a product recommendation system for a beauty e-commerce platform? Tests system design thinking, awareness of ML-adjacent concepts, and e-commerce domain knowledge.
  1. Walk us through how you would build a real-time inventory management system at scale. Tests knowledge of distributed systems, consistency trade-offs, and event-driven architecture.
  1. How have you optimised a slow API or database query in a past project? Tests hands-on backend experience, profiling skills, and the ability to explain technical decisions clearly.
  1. How would you design the search and filter feature for a large product catalog? Tests knowledge of full-text search, indexing strategies, and user-experience thinking.
  1. How would you handle a sudden spike in traffic during a flash sale event? Tests knowledge of caching, load balancing, rate limiting, and horizontal scaling.
  1. What is your experience with microservices? How did you decide where to draw service boundaries? Tests architecture judgment and real-world trade-off reasoning.
  1. How would you approach A/B testing a new checkout or cart feature? Tests product thinking, experimentation design, and metrics awareness.
  1. Tell me about a production incident you handled. What went wrong and how did you resolve it? Tests debugging process, ownership, and communication under pressure.
  1. How do you maintain code quality when the team is moving fast? Tests engineering discipline, review practices, and pragmatism.
  1. How would you design a coupon, referral, or loyalty points system? Tests transactional system design and edge-case thinking.
  1. Describe a time you disagreed with a technical decision made by your team. What did you do? Tests communication, influence without authority, and intellectual honesty.
  1. How would you migrate a monolith feature to a separate service without downtime? Tests knowledge of incremental migration patterns, feature flags, and safe deployment practices.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Practice these three STAR-format answers before your Purplle interview.

---

Q: Tell me about a production incident you handled.

*Situation:* Our payment callback endpoint started timing out intermittently during high-traffic hours, causing orders to get stuck in a 'pending' state.

*Task:* I was the on-call engineer that weekend and needed to identify the root cause and restore normal order flow as quickly as possible.

*Action:* I pulled application logs and spotted that a third-party payment gateway was returning slower responses than usual. I added a circuit breaker around the gateway call and introduced a retry queue using Redis so stuck orders could be retried asynchronously. I also set up an alert on response time percentiles for that endpoint so we would catch the problem earlier in future.

*Result:* Order failures dropped to near zero within the hour. The retry queue cleared all pending orders automatically, and we added the pattern to our incident runbook so the team could apply it elsewhere.

---

Q: How have you optimised a slow API or database query?

*Situation:* The product listing page on our mobile app was slow because the backend fetched product details, reviews, and inventory status in separate sequential database calls.

*Task:* My goal was to bring the page response time down to a level that felt fast for users on typical Indian mobile networks.

*Action:* I profiled the endpoint and identified the three separate queries. I refactored them into a single joined query with selective column fetching, added a composite index on the most-filtered columns, and introduced a short-lived cache for inventory counts. Non-critical data like review summaries was moved to a lazy-loaded secondary call.

*Result:* Response time for the primary call dropped noticeably according to our internal monitoring. The product team reported a measurable improvement in add-to-cart rates in the following sprint, which they attributed partly to the faster page load.

---

Q: Describe a time you disagreed with a technical decision.

*Situation:* My team decided to store all user session data in our primary relational database, which I felt would become a bottleneck as traffic grew.

*Task:* I needed to raise my concern constructively without slowing the sprint or coming across as obstructionist.

*Action:* I prepared a short written comparison of the current approach versus a Redis-based session store, including read and write latency figures from a small prototype I built over two evenings. I shared it during our weekly design review and invited the team to challenge my assumptions.

*Result:* The team agreed to pilot the Redis approach for new sessions. Within a month we had fully migrated, the primary database load dropped noticeably, and my manager later cited this as an example of raising technical concerns constructively.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For system design questions: Start by clarifying scope, covering read-versus-write patterns, scale expectations, and consistency requirements. Sketch a high-level architecture before diving into components. Call out trade-offs explicitly: 'I am choosing eventual consistency here because...'. Purplle interviewers, candidates report, appreciate when you tie design choices back to e-commerce realities like seasonal traffic spikes, catalog size, or seller onboarding complexity.

For coding questions: Think out loud before writing any code. Confirm input and output format, and ask about edge cases upfront. Write a brute-force solution first, explain its complexity, then optimise. Use clean variable names throughout. Ask the interviewer at the start of the round if they have a language preference.

For behavioural questions: Use STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for every story. Keep the Situation and Task brief, and spend most of your time on the Action you personally took. Always end with a concrete Result, even if you describe it in relative terms rather than exact numbers.

For 'how would you improve X' questions: Acknowledge what is already working before proposing changes. Prioritise your suggestions and explain the constraints you are working within: engineering bandwidth, business priorities, and existing technical debt. This shows product maturity, which Purplle values especially in mid-to-senior candidates.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Ownership mindset. Purplle moves fast and expects engineers to treat problems as their own, whether or not they were specifically asked to fix them. Stories where you spotted an issue proactively and resolved it without waiting for direction tend to land well.

E-commerce domain awareness. You do not need prior e-commerce experience, but you should understand concepts like catalog management, cart and checkout flows, payment reconciliation, and seller integrations at a surface level. Spend time using the Purplle app before your interview and think about the engineering behind what you see.

Communication clarity. Candidates report that interviewers pay close attention to how you explain your reasoning, not just whether your final answer is correct. Practice saying your thought process out loud, especially during system design and debugging questions.

Pragmatism over perfectionism. Purplle moves at startup speed. Interviewers, candidates report, value engineers who know when to build the elegant solution and when to ship the good-enough one. Demonstrating that judgment is a strong positive signal.

Collaboration signals. How you respond to hints, pushback, or alternative suggestions from the interviewer matters. Treat the interviewer as a colleague working through a problem with you, not as an examiner looking for a gotcha.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Core data structures and algorithms

Revisit arrays, linked lists, trees, graphs, hash maps, and sorting algorithms. Solve at least one problem per day on a coding practice platform of your choice, focusing on medium-difficulty problems. Review Big O notation so you can explain time and space complexity clearly and confidently during a live interview.

Week 2: System design

Study three to five system design topics relevant to e-commerce: recommendation engines, search and autocomplete, inventory management, payment processing, and notification systems. For each topic, practice sketching the architecture and explaining your trade-offs out loud in under half an hour without notes.

Week 3: Company and role context

Spend time on the Purplle app and website. Note features that interest you from an engineering perspective, such as product search and filters, reviews, or the seller portal. Look for any public engineering blog posts or talks from the Purplle tech team. Prepare two or three STAR stories from your own experience that map to the ownership, communication, and pragmatism signals Purplle looks for.

Week 4: Mock practice and logistics

Complete at least two full mock interviews, one coding and one system design, ideally with a peer who can give honest feedback. Review your STAR answers for behavioural questions. Prepare a few thoughtful questions to ask the interviewer about engineering culture, on-call practices, or team structure, as these show genuine interest and help you evaluate whether the role is the right fit.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping to code without clarifying the problem. Many candidates start typing the moment a question is stated. Spend a minute clarifying the problem, confirming constraints, and outlining your approach. Interviewers, candidates report, weigh the problem-solving process heavily.

Generic system design answers. A design that could apply to any company misses the mark at Purplle. Ground your answers in beauty e-commerce realities: traffic spikes around festivals, a large and growing product catalog, varying seller quality, and mobile-first users on diverse network speeds.

Vague STAR answers. 'I improved performance significantly' is weak. Even without exact numbers, describe the improvement in relative terms: 'the page was slow enough that users noticed, and after the fix the product team saw a measurable lift in engagement.'

Skipping the 'questions for us' slot. Not asking questions signals low interest. Prepare genuine questions about team structure, engineering challenges, how success is measured in the role, or what the biggest technical problem the team is currently solving.

Over-engineering design answers. Proposing a complex multi-service architecture for a feature that could use a simple job queue signals poor judgment. Start simple, then layer in complexity only when you can justify it with specific scale or reliability requirements.

How many rounds does the Purplle.com Software Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically runs through several rounds. It usually starts with a recruiter or HR screening call, followed by one or two technical coding rounds, a system design discussion for mid and senior candidates, and a final conversation with a hiring manager or engineering lead. The exact number can vary by team and seniority level, so it is worth asking your recruiter at the start of the process what stages to expect.

What programming language should I use in the Purplle coding round?

Candidates report that Purplle engineering teams commonly work with Python, Java, and Node.js, among other languages. Most interviewers are flexible about the language used in coding rounds as long as your code is clean and readable. Ask your interviewer at the start of the round if they have a preference, and default to whichever language you know best if they say it does not matter.

Does Purplle ask system design questions for entry-level Software Engineer roles?

Candidates report that dedicated system design rounds are more common for mid-level and senior candidates. Entry-level candidates are more likely to face data structures, algorithms, and low-level design problems. That said, reading up on basic system design is worthwhile even as a fresher, since 'how would you design X' questions can come up at any seniority level in a fast-moving startup environment.

How should I prepare for the Purplle behavioural round?

Spend time using the Purplle app before your interview and think about the engineering challenges behind the features you see. Prepare two or three work stories in STAR format that demonstrate ownership, technical problem-solving, and cross-team collaboration. Purplle interviewers, candidates report, want to see that you can thrive in a fast-moving, product-focused team. Researching any public content from their engineering team also helps you ask informed questions at the end of the round.
