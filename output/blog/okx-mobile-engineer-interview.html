02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Based on what candidates report and the nature of OKX's product, here are the questions you are most likely to face:

  1. Walk me through how you would architect a real-time crypto price feed in a mobile app.
  2. How do you approach state management in a large, fast-moving mobile codebase?
  3. Describe how you have optimised app performance, especially around list rendering or memory usage.
  4. How do you securely store sensitive user data such as wallet keys or authentication tokens on a mobile device?
  5. Explain your approach to offline-first design. How would you handle a user who loses connectivity mid-transaction?
  6. How do you handle deep linking and universal links, and how have you tested them in production?
  7. Describe a production crash that was difficult to reproduce locally. How did you investigate and fix it?
  8. How would you design a push notification system that is both timely and respectful of battery life and user preferences?
  9. What is your approach to writing testable mobile code? What mix of unit, integration, and UI tests do you write?
  10. Tell me about a time you worked closely with designers and backend engineers to ship a feature with tight dependencies.
  11. OKX operates in a regulated, security-critical environment. How do you think about mobile security from first principles?
  12. How do you stay current with platform changes (iOS/Android release cycles, deprecation notices) and apply them proactively?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe your experience optimising app performance.

*Situation:* At my previous company, we had a portfolio screen that displayed live asset prices. As users added more assets, the scroll became visibly sluggish over several releases.

*Task:* I was asked to diagnose the issue and restore smooth performance before a major product launch.

*Action:* I used the platform profiler to identify that the entire list was re-rendering on each incoming price tick. I introduced virtualised lists, memoised the price update logic so only changed rows re-rendered, and deferred non-critical data fetches to after the initial paint.

*Result:* Scrolling became noticeably smoother, beta users gave positive feedback, and the improvement shipped before the launch deadline. This kind of render-aware optimisation is exactly what OKX interviewers probe for in mobile candidates.

---

Q: Describe a time you debugged a production crash that was hard to reproduce locally.

*Situation:* Our crash monitoring tool flagged a recurring crash on older Android devices. The stack trace pointed to our wallet balance fetch callback.

*Task:* I owned the investigation because the suspected code was in an area I had built.

*Action:* I added granular logging to the suspected path, created a test build targeting older device tiers, and worked with QA to simulate poor network conditions. The crash turned out to be a missing null check that fired after the fragment had detached from the activity.

*Result:* A targeted null guard fixed the issue. No further crashes were reported on that path after the release. I documented the root cause as a team reference on lifecycle-aware async calls.

---

Q: How have you worked with cross-functional teams to ship a feature under pressure?

*Situation:* I was the mobile lead on a KYC (Know Your Customer) verification flow that required backend engineers, a compliance team, and product designers to work in parallel.

*Task:* I needed to keep mobile development unblocked while API contracts and compliance requirements were still being finalised.

*Action:* I built the UI against a local mock layer so the team could iterate on design and edge cases without waiting on the API. I held short daily syncs to surface contract changes early and flagged blockers to the product manager before they became delays. When the real API was ready, swapping out the mock was straightforward.

*Result:* We shipped the KYC flow on schedule. The mock-first approach was adopted as a standard practice for future features with uncertain backend timelines.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For behavioral questions, use STAR: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep the Situation and Task brief. Put most of your energy into the Action (what you specifically did, not just what 'we' did) and make the Result concrete. If you do not have an exact figure, describe the outcome in terms of user impact, team adoption, or business result.

For system design questions: Start by clarifying scope. Ask about scale, latency requirements, and which platforms need to be supported. Then walk through components: data layer, networking strategy, state management, and UI rendering. Finish by discussing trade-offs (native vs cross-platform, polling vs WebSocket, local cache vs always-fetch). OKX will likely care about real-time data handling and security, so have clear opinions on both.

For coding questions: Think aloud. State your approach before you write code. Handle edge cases explicitly (null inputs, empty states, network failure). For mobile-specific coding questions, mention the threading model (main thread vs background thread) wherever it is relevant.

For security questions: Avoid generic answers. Talk about specific mechanisms: Keychain (iOS) or Keystore (Android), certificate pinning, biometric authentication, token refresh strategies, and what happens if a device is flagged as compromised.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

OKX interviewers for mobile roles are looking for a specific combination, based on what candidates report and the demands of their product.

Platform depth. You should be able to go deep on your primary platform, whether that is Swift/iOS, Kotlin/Android, or React Native/Flutter. Surface-level answers do not pass the bar at a company like OKX.

Security thinking. OKX handles user funds and identity data. Interviewers want to see that you treat security as a first-class concern, not an afterthought. Know your platform's secure storage APIs, how to handle token lifecycle, and what your app should do when a device is compromised.

Real-time data fluency. Crypto products are driven by live data. You should be comfortable discussing WebSockets, event-driven state updates, and how to prevent UI jank when data arrives faster than frames can render.

Product thinking. OKX values engineers who consider the user experience alongside the technical implementation. Be ready to explain why you made a design decision, not just how you executed it.

Clear communication. Candidates who explain complex mobile concepts clearly and surface trade-offs confidently tend to score well, especially in system design rounds.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

First, cover core mobile fundamentals. Go deep on your primary platform. Review the activity and view lifecycle (Android) or UIViewController lifecycle (iOS), the threading model, memory management, and the rendering pipeline. Practise explaining these concepts out loud as if teaching someone new to the topic.

Second, practise mobile system design. Prepare to design at least three systems: a real-time price feed, an offline-first sync flow, and a push notification architecture. For each, practise scoping the problem, listing constraints, sketching components, and discussing trade-offs.

Third, sharpen your security knowledge. Study Keychain/Keystore APIs, certificate pinning, biometric authentication, and secure token storage. Read publicly available resources on mobile security best practices for fintech applications.

Fourth, build your behavioral stories. Identify your strongest project experiences. For each, prepare a clear STAR narrative focused on what you personally did and what the outcome was. Practise telling each story concisely so you can deliver it clearly without rambling.

Fifth, learn the product context. Spend time using the OKX app. Read their public engineering blog or product updates if available. Understanding their product (wallets, trading, Web3 features) helps you give contextually sharp answers in both design and behavioral rounds.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Going shallow on security. OKX is a fintech and crypto company. Saying you 'use HTTPS' is not enough. Know your platform's secure storage APIs, token management patterns, and how you handle a device that may be rooted or jailbroken.

Treating every question as purely technical. OKX wants engineers who think about the product experience. A performance fix is not just a technical improvement. It is about whether a user trusts the app enough to keep using it.

No real-time data experience. If WebSockets or event-driven updates are unfamiliar territory, study them before the interview. Crypto apps are built around live data, and this topic will come up.

Vague STAR answers. Saying 'we improved performance' is not a STAR answer. Name what you changed, explain why you chose that approach, and describe the outcome in terms your interviewer can evaluate.

Jumping into system design without scoping. Drawing an architecture before you understand the requirements is a common red flag. Always ask about scale, latency, and platform requirements before you begin.

Not preparing questions to ask. Candidates who ask nothing at the end of an interview can seem disengaged. Prepare a few genuine questions about the team, the tech stack, or how the mobile product fits into OKX's broader roadmap.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

What tech stack does OKX typically use for mobile?

Candidates report OKX uses a combination of native iOS (Swift) and Android (Kotlin) alongside React Native for some cross-platform surfaces. In your interview, be clear about your strongest platform and be ready to discuss the trade-offs between native and cross-platform development. Demonstrating that you can reason across platforms, even if you specialise in one, is valued at a company with global reach.

How many rounds does the OKX mobile interview typically have?

Candidates typically report a process spanning several rounds, including a technical screen, one or more coding or system design rounds, and a final round that often includes a values or culture discussion. Round structure can vary by team and hiring manager, so treat each message from the recruiter as your most current source of truth on what to expect.

Does OKX ask DSA (data structures and algorithms) questions?

Yes, candidates report DSA is part of the process, typically in a dedicated coding round. The problems tend to be practical rather than highly academic, but you should be comfortable with arrays, trees, and graph traversal. Pair your DSA prep with mobile-specific coding practice such as handling async operations and threading correctly, since both may come up.

Is prior crypto or Web3 experience required?

Prior crypto experience is not strictly required, but candidates who understand the product context (wallets, real-time prices, transaction flows, security requirements) tend to answer design and behavioral questions more confidently. Spending time using the OKX app and reading their publicly available product updates before your interview can give you a real advantage.

How long does the full OKX hiring process typically take?

Candidates report the process from application to offer can span a few weeks to over a month, depending on team availability and scheduling. Following up politely after each round is common practice and generally well-received. Avoid letting long gaps pass without checking in with your recruiter contact, as roles can be filled or re-scoped during a slow process.

Should I prepare for both iOS and Android questions, or just one?

Focus on your strongest platform and be honest about your experience with others. Interviewers generally prefer depth on one platform over shallow knowledge of both. If the OKX job description mentions React Native or cross-platform development, make sure you can speak to the trade-offs and describe any relevant experience you have with that approach.
