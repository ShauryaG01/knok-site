01 Overview

Overview

Braze is a customer engagement platform trusted by consumer brands worldwide to run personalised push notifications, email campaigns, in-app messages, and more. Their infrastructure handles very high event volumes, which means the DevOps and platform engineering teams are mission-critical. With 252 open roles at Braze as of the latest knok data, the company is in active growth mode, and DevOps profiles are among the most sought after.

Candidates report a process that typically runs across four stages: a recruiter screen, a technical phone round covering fundamentals, a hands-on round (live coding or take-home), and a final panel with senior engineers and possibly an engineering manager. Braze follows an end-to-end ownership culture, so interviewers assess whether you can drive reliability on your own initiative, not just execute assigned tickets.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions are drawn from publicly shared candidate experiences and the kind of technical depth Braze is known to probe. Expect a mix of system design, hands-on technical, and behavioural questions.

  1. Walk us through a Kubernetes cluster you managed: how did you handle upgrades, node pool changes, and workload disruptions without impacting production?
  2. Describe a CI/CD pipeline you built or significantly improved. What were the bottlenecks and what specifically did you change?
  3. Braze processes very high event volumes. How would you design infrastructure to absorb sudden traffic spikes without taking the platform down?
  4. Walk through a production incident you owned from the first alert to the post-mortem. What did you learn and what changed afterwards?
  5. What is your approach to infrastructure-as-code? How do you manage Terraform state, prevent drift, and handle team collaboration on shared IaC repos?
  6. How do you build observability into a distributed system? Describe your approach to metrics, logs, and traces.
  7. How do you manage secrets and credentials securely in a cloud-native environment?
  8. What does zero-downtime deployment mean to you in practice, and how have you actually achieved it?
  9. How do you shift reliability left and work with application developers so that reliability is built in before code reaches production?
  10. Describe your approach to cloud cost optimisation. What levers do you use and how do you measure the impact?
  11. How do you design for capacity at a SaaS product where usage patterns are unpredictable?
  12. Tell me about a time you automated a painful manual process. What drove you to do it and what was the outcome?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk through a production incident you owned from the first alert to the post-mortem.

*Situation:* A critical payment processing service at my previous employer started throwing elevated error rates on a Friday evening, and alerts fired across multiple dashboards simultaneously.

*Task:* As the on-call engineer, I was responsible for triaging the issue, coordinating the response, and driving the post-mortem.

*Action:* I pulled the error logs, narrowed the root cause to a misconfigured load balancer rule introduced in the latest deployment, and rolled back that release. I posted regular status updates to the stakeholders channel while the rollback was in progress. Once the service was stable, I opened a post-mortem document, ran a blameless review with the team, and identified two systemic gaps: no automated rollback trigger on error-rate spike and no pre-deployment validation for load balancer rule changes.

*Result:* Both fixes shipped in the following sprint. The same class of failure has not recurred since. The incident also became a case study for on-call onboarding across the broader engineering team.

---

Q: Describe a CI/CD pipeline you built or significantly improved.

*Situation:* At a previous role, the deployment pipeline ran all test suites sequentially with no caching, making every push slow to reach staging and developers reluctant to ship frequently.

*Task:* I was asked to make the pipeline faster and more reliable so the team could ship with confidence throughout the day.

*Action:* I redesigned the pipeline in GitHub Actions, parallelised test suites across matrix jobs, introduced Docker layer caching, and added a fast pre-merge stage that ran unit tests first to give early feedback. I also wrote a script to detect unchanged services in a monorepo and skip their builds entirely.

*Result:* Build times dropped substantially and release frequency increased. Developers moved from batching changes for end-of-day releases to shipping smaller changes throughout the day, which reduced the blast radius of any single deployment and made rollbacks far simpler.

---

Q: How do you shift reliability left and work with application developers?

*Situation:* At a company where I worked as the sole platform engineer, developers had little visibility into how their services performed in production and rarely thought about reliability before a feature merged.

*Task:* I needed to create a culture where developers considered reliability before opening a pull request, not after an incident.

*Action:* I built internal runbook templates, added SLO dashboards accessible to every developer, and ran a monthly 'reliability office hours' session open to the whole engineering org. I also added a reliability checklist to the PR template covering health checks, graceful shutdown, timeout configuration, and structured logging.

*Result:* Incidents caused by missing health checks dropped to zero in the months following. Developers started proactively asking platform questions before opening PRs, which I took as the clearest sign that the culture had genuinely shifted.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For behavioural questions (incidents, collaboration, automation): Use the STAR structure. Keep Situation and Task brief, two to three sentences each. Spend most of your answer on Action, because interviewers want to see how you think and what you personally drove. End with a concrete Result and, where possible, what you learned from the experience.

For system design questions (scale, traffic spikes, capacity planning): Start by clarifying the constraints: event volume, latency requirements, failure tolerance. Then walk through your design top-down, covering the entry point, compute layer, data layer, and observability. Braze is event-driven and latency-sensitive, so call out where you would add queuing, caching, or async processing. Always address failure modes proactively before the interviewer raises them.

For technical deep-dive questions (Kubernetes, IaC, CI/CD): Ground your answer in a real system you have operated. Avoid speaking in abstractions. Name the specific tools you used, explain the tradeoffs you made, and mention what you would do differently with hindsight. Interviewers at product companies want engineers who have felt the consequences of their choices, not engineers who have only read documentation.

For ownership and culture questions: Braze values end-to-end ownership. When answering questions about cross-team work or collaboration, show that you followed through on outcomes rather than handing off a task and moving on. Measure your results where you can, and be honest about what you could not fully control.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Deep hands-on experience, not theory. Braze runs large-scale infrastructure, and interviewers can tell quickly whether you have actually operated a system at scale or have mainly studied for the interview. Come prepared with specific examples from real production environments, including what broke and why.

Ownership mindset. Braze engineers are expected to own their services fully. Interviewers listen for signs that you treat reliability as your problem, not someone else's. Answers that trail off with 'I raised a ticket' or 'I escalated it' without follow-through are a red flag.

Communication under pressure. Incident response questions are partly a communication test. Can you stay calm, keep stakeholders informed, and make decisions with incomplete information? Show this clearly in your incident stories.

Cost and efficiency awareness. Braze is a SaaS company with real infrastructure costs. Candidates who can talk about cloud cost optimisation, right-sizing, and efficiency tradeoffs stand out from those who focus only on reliability and speed.

Collaboration with developers. DevOps at Braze is not a gatekeeper function. Interviewers want to see that you build self-service tooling, work closely with application developers, and help teams ship faster without sacrificing reliability.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Core technical revision
Revise Kubernetes internals including scheduling, autoscaling, pod disruption budgets, and cluster upgrade strategies. Review Terraform best practices: state management, remote backends, and drift detection. Brush up on the observability tools you have used most, whether Prometheus and Grafana, Datadog, or a similar stack.

Week 2: System design and scale
Practice designing event-driven, high-throughput systems. Think through how you would build a pipeline that ingests very large event volumes reliably and recovers gracefully from failures. Read about queue-based architectures, Kafka, and rate limiting patterns. Braze's engineering blog is publicly available and gives real context on the problems their platform team has tackled, which is worth reading before your interview.

Week 3: Behavioural prep and storytelling
Write down five to six stories from your career using STAR format. Cover: a hard incident, a pipeline improvement, a cross-team collaboration, a time you automated something significant, and a time you disagreed with a technical decision and how it resolved. Practice telling each story in under three minutes so your answers stay focused.

Week 4: Mock interviews and loose ends
Do at least two mock technical interviews and ask for honest feedback. Revisit any tool you have not used recently. Prepare three to four questions for your interviewer about Braze's reliability culture, on-call practices, and how the platform team partners with product engineering.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Giving textbook answers without real examples. Saying 'I would use Kubernetes HPA to handle traffic spikes' is not enough. Interviewers want to know whether you have actually done it, what went wrong, and what you learned from the experience.

Softening incident stories. Candidates sometimes downplay their incident answers to avoid looking bad. Interviewers are not judging you for the incident itself. They are judging how you handled it. Be specific and honest about the failure, your response, and the systemic fix you put in place.

Ignoring cost and efficiency. DevOps interviews at product companies often include cost questions. Candidates who talk only about reliability and speed, without mentioning cost awareness, miss an important dimension that Braze interviewers are known to probe.

Treating DevOps as purely infrastructure. Braze values engineers who bridge platform and product. If your answers focus only on infrastructure and never mention developer experience or self-service tooling, it signals a scope that is too narrow for the role.

Not preparing questions for the interviewer. Arriving without questions reads as low interest. Prepare thoughtful questions about the team's on-call culture, how they measure reliability, and what the biggest infrastructure challenges are heading into 2026.

Over-engineering system design answers. Adding complexity for its own sake is a red flag. Start with a simple, working design, explain your tradeoffs clearly, and layer in complexity only when you have a clear reason for it.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many interview rounds does Braze typically have for DevOps roles?

Candidates report a process that typically includes a recruiter screen, one or two technical rounds covering Kubernetes, CI/CD, and system design, and a final panel with senior engineers or an engineering manager. The exact number of rounds can vary by team and level, so it is worth confirming the current format with your recruiter when you are first scheduled.

Does Braze give a take-home assignment for DevOps interviews?

Some candidates report a take-home exercise, often involving writing a Terraform configuration, designing a CI/CD pipeline, or debugging a broken Kubernetes setup. Others report a live coding or whiteboard session instead. Where a take-home is given, candidates typically say it is scoped to a few hours of work rather than a multi-day project.

What cloud platform does Braze primarily use?

Braze is publicly reported to run primarily on AWS. Expect questions about AWS services relevant to DevOps work: EKS, EC2, RDS, S3, IAM, and CloudWatch. Familiarity with multi-region deployments and AWS security best practices will help you across both the technical rounds and the system design discussion.

What is the typical DevOps salary range at Braze in India?

Braze India-specific salary data has a small public sample size, so treat specific figures with caution. Based on the broader DevOps market in India, mid-level roles (3-5 years) sit in the 15-28 LPA range and senior roles (6-9 years) in the 30-50 LPA range per knok's current job market data. For Braze-specific compensation numbers, Glassdoor and levels.fyi carry candidate-reported figures that are worth checking before you negotiate.

Is prior experience with Braze's product SDK required for a DevOps role?

No, SDK knowledge is not typically required for DevOps roles at Braze. The focus is on infrastructure, reliability, and developer tooling rather than the product SDK itself. That said, understanding at a conceptual level how a high-volume customer engagement platform works will help you answer system design questions more credibly.

How important is on-call experience for a Braze DevOps interview?

On-call experience is highly valued. Braze's infrastructure must stay up during customer campaigns, and the company places a strong emphasis on reliability and end-to-end ownership. Come prepared with at least one detailed incident story that covers your triage process, how you communicated during the incident, and what systemic changes you put in place afterwards to prevent recurrence.
