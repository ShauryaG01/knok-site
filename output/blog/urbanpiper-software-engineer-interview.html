02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates report these topics coming up frequently in UrbanPiper Software Engineer interviews. Work through each one before your first call.

  1. Walk me through how you would design a system to sync menu updates from a restaurant to multiple food delivery platforms in real time.
  2. How would you handle order deduplication when the same order arrives from two different sources at the same moment?
  3. Explain the difference between SQL and NoSQL databases. When would you choose one over the other for a restaurant order-management scenario?
  4. How do you approach rate limiting in a REST API that receives bursts of traffic during peak meal hours?
  5. Describe a time you improved the performance of a slow database query. What did you identify and how did you fix it?
  6. How would you design a webhook system that reliably delivers order status updates to restaurant POS systems, even when the POS is temporarily offline?
  7. What is eventual consistency, and how would you explain a delayed menu update to a non-technical restaurant owner?
  8. How do you ensure your code handles failures gracefully when a third-party aggregator API goes down mid-order?
  9. Tell me about a project where you balanced speed of delivery with code quality. What trade-offs did you make?
  10. How would you debug a production issue where orders are intermittently failing to reach a kitchen display system?
  11. Describe your experience with message queues or event-driven architectures. How have you used them to decouple services in a real project?
  12. How do you think about backward compatibility when updating a shared API used by multiple restaurant clients?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use these as a starting point, then swap in your own real experience.

Q: Describe a time you improved the performance of a slow database query.

*Situation:* At my previous company, the order history page for restaurant accounts with large transaction volumes was loading very slowly, generating complaints from the support team.

*Task:* I was asked to investigate and reduce the load time without restructuring the existing data model.

*Action:* I ran EXPLAIN ANALYZE on the query and found it was doing a full table scan because the WHERE clause filtered on a column with no index. I added a composite index on (restaurant_id, created_at) and rewrote one subquery as a JOIN. I tested the change on a staging environment with production-scale data to confirm no regressions before merging.

*Result:* Page load time dropped noticeably, support tickets for that feature stopped, and the fix was deployed within the same sprint.

---

Q: Tell me about a project where you balanced speed of delivery with code quality.

*Situation:* During a product sprint, we had a hard deadline to launch a new aggregator integration before a major promotional campaign.

*Task:* I needed to ship the integration on time while keeping it maintainable, because the team would need to extend it later.

*Action:* I separated the 'must-have' and 'nice-to-have' pieces early. For the critical order-routing logic I wrote unit tests and kept the code modular. For lower-risk configuration parsing I shipped a simpler version with a comment marking it for a follow-up refactor. I flagged the trade-off explicitly in the PR description so reviewers could make an informed call.

*Result:* We launched on time, the critical path had solid test coverage, and the follow-up refactor was completed in the next sprint without surprises.

---

Q: How have you handled a production failure in a system you owned?

*Situation:* A background job that pushed order confirmations to restaurant printers started failing silently after a dependency update.

*Task:* I was the on-call engineer and needed to restore service quickly to minimise disruption to restaurant operations.

*Action:* I confirmed the scope of the failure from logs and identified the first failed job. I rolled back the dependency update to restore service immediately, then reproduced the failure locally to find the root cause. The new library version had changed an exception type we were catching. I updated the error-handling code, added a targeted test for that specific case, and re-deployed.

*Result:* Restaurants were back to receiving confirmations quickly. We added a check in CI to catch similar dependency-related breakages going forward.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions. Situation and Task together should take no more than a third of your answer. Spend the bulk of your time on Action and Result. If the project was cancelled or the result was mixed, be honest about it, then say what you learned.

Clarify, design, trade-off for system design. Start by asking a few scoping questions (read vs write volume, consistency requirements, latency targets). Sketch a high-level design before diving into individual components. Name trade-offs explicitly. For example: 'I am choosing eventual consistency here because the menu sync delay is acceptable, but if strong consistency were required I would use a distributed transaction.' This shows you understand the cost of every decision, not just how to build the happy path.

Think aloud in coding rounds. UrbanPiper's stack handles real-time order processing, so interviewers often probe your reasoning about edge cases: duplicate events, retry logic, and partial failures. Walk through your assumptions before writing any code.

Layered approach for debugging questions. Start at the symptom (what the user sees), move to the system layer (logs, metrics), narrow to the specific component, then propose a fix. Structured reasoning matters more than reaching the answer instantly.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

UrbanPiper's engineering challenges come from real restaurant operations: menus change mid-service, orders spike around meal times, and third-party aggregators have varying reliability. Interviewers typically look for a few things beyond raw coding ability.

Ownership mindset. Candidates who treat production systems as their own responsibility, not just the ops team's problem, stand out. Show that you monitor what you ship and act when something breaks.

Comfort with distributed systems basics. You do not need large-scale production experience, but you should be able to explain concepts like idempotency, retry with backoff, and message queues in plain terms and connect them to practical problems.

Clear communication. UrbanPiper serves restaurant operators who are not always technical. Candidates who can explain a trade-off in simple language are valued. Practice stating your reasoning out loud, not just in your head.

Practical over theoretical. Candidates report that interviewers respond better to answers grounded in real experience. A story about a flaky third-party API, a slow query, or a race condition you fixed in production is more compelling than a textbook definition.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Two to three weeks out: Revise data structures and algorithms with a focus on problems relevant to order processing: queues, heaps, hash maps, and graph traversal. Review SQL query optimisation basics, indexing strategies, and transaction isolation levels. If you are not familiar with event-driven architecture patterns, spend time on those too.

One week out: Practice system design by sketching architectures for problems similar to what UrbanPiper solves. A useful prompt to work through: 'Design a system that receives orders from multiple aggregators and routes them to the correct restaurant reliably.' Think through failure modes at each step. Prepare three to five STAR stories from your own experience covering performance improvement, production incidents, and cross-team collaboration.

Two to three days out: Research UrbanPiper's product. Understand how their platform centralises orders from multiple aggregators, how menu management works across delivery apps, and how their integrations with Zomato and Swiggy function at a high level. Being able to reference their actual product in your answers shows genuine interest. Review the job description carefully and match your stories to the skills listed.

Day before: Do a short mock interview. Record yourself answering one behavioural and one system design question and listen back critically. Candidates report UrbanPiper interviews are typically conducted over video call, so check your audio and camera setup in advance.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

  1. Jumping into code without clarifying requirements. Candidates who ask no questions and dive straight in often miss edge cases that matter to the interviewer. Take a moment to confirm scope and constraints before writing anything.
  1. Treating distributed systems as a senior-only topic. Even for entry and mid-level roles, UrbanPiper's platform involves multiple integrated services. Knowing the basics of retries, idempotency, and async processing is expected earlier than at many other companies.
  1. Vague STAR answers. Saying 'I improved performance' without explaining what you identified and how you changed it does not convince. Bring specific technical detail, even if you cannot share exact business numbers.
  1. Not asking questions at the end. Interviewers see this as a signal of low engagement. Prepare two or three genuine questions about the team's engineering practices, on-call culture, or current technical challenges.
  1. Ignoring failure scenarios in system design. A design that only works when everything goes right is incomplete. Always address what happens when an aggregator API times out or a message fails to deliver.
  1. Underestimating the culture-fit round. Candidates report that UrbanPiper values how you collaborate and handle disagreement. Prepare a story about a time you pushed back on a technical decision respectfully and what came of it.
