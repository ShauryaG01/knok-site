01 Overview

Overview

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

  1. Walk me through a product you have shipped end-to-end. What was your role and what would you do differently today?
  2. Autodesk serves both individual professionals and large enterprise customers. How do you balance the needs of these two segments when prioritising a roadmap?
  3. How would you prioritise a backlog for one of Autodesk's core design tools, given competing requests from power users versus new learners?
  4. Tell me about a time you had to kill or deprioritise a feature your team was excited about. How did you handle it?
  5. How do you measure the success of a B2B SaaS product? What metrics would you track for an Autodesk product used in construction workflows?
  6. Autodesk has been shifting from perpetual licences to subscription. How would you manage customer retention during such a business model transition?
  7. Describe a situation where data and user feedback pointed in opposite directions. How did you decide what to do?
  8. How would you improve onboarding for a complex professional software product where users have steep learning curves?
  9. Tell me about a cross-functional conflict you resolved. What was your approach and what did you learn?
  10. How would you approach building a roadmap for an Autodesk product in a new geography like India where construction and manufacturing practices differ from the US market?
  11. A key enterprise customer is threatening to churn because a competitor released a feature you do not have. How do you respond?
  12. How do you stay close to users when your product serves technical professionals in niche industries like architecture or civil engineering?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk me through a product you shipped end-to-end.

*Situation:* At my previous company, our mobile app had a drop-off problem at checkout. Data showed that a large portion of users who added items to cart did not complete the purchase, but we had no qualitative data on why.

*Task:* I was asked to own the checkout experience redesign. The goal was to reduce drop-off without compromising security or compliance requirements.

*Action:* I ran five user interviews in two weeks, which surfaced that users were confused by a mandatory account-creation step. I worked with design to prototype a guest-checkout flow, negotiated with the security team to allow a one-time verification code instead of full registration, and partnered with engineering to ship an A/B test within six weeks.

*Result:* The guest-checkout variant showed a clear improvement in completion rate in our experiment cohort. We rolled it out fully, and the team used this discovery to revisit three other forced-registration flows across the app.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you had to deprioritise a feature your team was excited about.

*Situation:* My team had spent two sprints scoping an advanced export feature that engineers were genuinely excited to build. A new piece of customer research landed the same week showing our core sync reliability was causing churn among paying customers.

*Task:* I needed to redirect the team without losing morale, and communicate the change to a product head who had already seen the export feature in a roadmap review.

*Action:* I documented the reliability findings in a one-pager, quantified the risk using support ticket data, and scheduled a short roadmap sync. I was transparent with the team about why the tradeoff mattered, and offered to keep the export spec in a 'ready to build' state so it would not be lost.

*Result:* The team pivoted within a week. Reliability fixes shipped in the next sprint. The export feature shipped the following quarter, with the team more invested because they had seen how prioritisation decisions were made transparently.

---

Q: How do you balance enterprise and individual user needs on a shared product?

*Situation:* At a previous role, our analytics tool had a shared codebase serving both freelancers and mid-size business teams. Feature requests from both groups were pulling the roadmap in different directions.

*Task:* I was asked to define a segmentation strategy so we could make deliberate tradeoffs rather than trying to please everyone at once.

*Action:* I ran a revenue-weighted analysis of our user base, interviewed six enterprise admins and four individual power users, and mapped their jobs-to-be-done side by side. I proposed tiered feature access, where advanced team-management features were gated to the business plan while core functionality stayed shared.

*Result:* The framework got sign-off from leadership and gave the team a clear decision rule for future requests. Enterprise upsell improved in the following two quarters, per publicly reported figures from our annual review.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

CIRCLES (for product design questions)
A structured way to answer 'design a product' or 'improve this product' questions: Comprehend the situation, Identify the user, Report user needs, Cut through prioritisation, List solutions, Evaluate tradeoffs, Summarise. Autodesk interviewers typically want to see that you start with the user and industry context before jumping to features.

STAR (for behavioral questions)
Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task brief, two or three sentences each, and spend most of your answer on Action and Result. Autodesk behavioral questions often probe cross-functional influence and data-driven decisions, so your Result should include both a measurable outcome and a learning.

The 'Why Now' Roadmap Framework
When asked about prioritisation, structure your answer around: user pain, business impact, strategic fit, and effort. For Autodesk, strategic fit matters a lot because many products are part of a broader platform ecosystem. A feature that helps one product but creates integration debt for the platform is a bad tradeoff.

Metric Trees for SaaS
For any question about measuring success, build a simple metric tree: start with the north-star metric, then break it into leading indicators (activation, engagement, retention) and lagging indicators (revenue, NPS). Autodesk's subscription model means retention metrics carry extra weight compared to a company still in pure growth mode.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Candidates report that Autodesk PM interviewers are looking for a few qualities above most others.

Industry curiosity. Autodesk's products serve architects, engineers, manufacturers, and media professionals. You do not need to be a civil engineer, but interviewers want to see that you find these industries genuinely interesting and have done the work to understand how professionals in these fields actually think and work day to day.

Systems thinking. Autodesk builds platform products where decisions ripple across multiple tools and customer segments. Interviewers push back on answers that optimise one metric at the cost of the broader system. Show that you think in ecosystems, not just individual features.

Clear communication under ambiguity. Autodesk PMs work with globally distributed teams. Interviewers test whether you can structure a problem clearly, communicate tradeoffs simply, and make a recommendation without being asked twice.

Ownership without authority. Many candidates report being asked about situations where they had to influence without formal authority. Engineering, design, legal, and go-to-market teams all have a say in Autodesk product decisions. Your stories should show you can build alignment, not just escalate.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week one: Know the company and products
Use Autodesk's own product pages and the Autodesk community forums to understand the core product lines: AutoCAD, Revit, Maya, and the construction and manufacturing platform products. Read recent earnings call summaries (publicly available) to understand how leadership talks about the subscription transition and platform strategy.

Week two: Practice product questions
Pick two or three Autodesk products and practice full CIRCLES walkthroughs out loud. Record yourself. Focus on: who is the user, what is the job-to-be-done, how does this product fit the Autodesk platform. Time yourself to stay under five minutes per answer.

Week three: Behavioral stories
Write out five to seven STAR stories covering: shipping a product, killing a feature, cross-functional conflict, data versus intuition, and customer feedback that changed your view. Tailor at least two stories to contexts that map to Autodesk's industries (construction, manufacturing, media, or engineering software).

Week four: Mock interviews and logistics
Do at least two full mock interviews with someone who will give you real feedback. Review Glassdoor and Blind for recent Autodesk PM interview experiences (note that these reflect individual experiences and sample sizes are small). Confirm your interview format with the recruiter: whether there is a take-home case, a design exercise, or a final presentation round.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating Autodesk like a consumer company. Autodesk is a B2B and B2B2C company. Answers built around growth hacking, virality, or consumer engagement loops will miss the mark. Frame your thinking around professional workflows, long sales cycles, and enterprise retention.

Skipping the 'why Autodesk' question mentally. Even if the interviewer does not ask it directly, your answers should reflect genuine familiarity with Autodesk's mission and products. Candidates who could be talking about any SaaS company stand out for the wrong reasons.

Generic metrics. Saying 'I would track DAU and revenue' is not enough. Interviewers want to see that you can tie metrics to the specific business model. For a subscription product serving professional users, metrics like licence utilisation, seat expansion, and renewal rate matter more than raw acquisition numbers.

Over-indexing on features. Autodesk interviewers often push back with 'why not just ship X?' The candidates who do well explain the tradeoffs clearly and defend their prioritisation logic rather than adding more features to the list.

Not asking good questions. Autodesk PM roles span very different products and teams. Use your questions to understand the team's current biggest challenge, the metrics they care about most, and how product decisions get made. Candidates who ask sharp, specific questions consistently stand out.

Rushing past the problem statement. In case or design questions, jumping to solutions too quickly is a common failure mode. Autodesk interviewers typically want to see you spend real time scoping the problem and the user before proposing anything.

Frequently asked

How many PM roles does Autodesk have open in India right now?

What salary can I expect as a PM at Autodesk India?

Based on knok jobradar data, PM salaries in India range from 12-20 LPA at the Associate level up to 55-90+ LPA at the Group or Principal PM level. Mid-level PMs with 3-6 years of experience typically fall in the 24-40 LPA band. Actual Autodesk-specific numbers vary by team, level, and stock component, and Glassdoor has individual data points you can cross-reference for a more complete picture.

How many interview rounds does Autodesk typically have for PM roles?

Candidates report that Autodesk's PM interview process typically involves four to six rounds, though this varies by level and team. You can generally expect a recruiter screen, a hiring manager conversation, and rounds covering product design, behavioral questions, and analytical or strategic thinking. Some roles include a take-home case or a final presentation, so confirm the format with your recruiter early.

Do I need domain knowledge in architecture or engineering to get a PM role at Autodesk?

Domain expertise is a plus but not typically a hard requirement. Candidates report that interviewers value genuine curiosity about Autodesk's industries more than technical credentials. Spending time to understand how architects, engineers, or manufacturers use design software in their daily work, and being able to speak to it clearly, goes a long way in interviews.

What is the difference between a PM and a Senior PM role at Autodesk?

At most companies including Autodesk, a PM role typically involves owning a defined feature area or product surface, while a Senior PM is expected to drive larger roadmap initiatives, influence across teams, and mentor junior PMs. Candidates report that Senior PM interviews go deeper on strategic thinking, stakeholder alignment, and cross-functional leadership. The salary bands from knok jobradar data show a meaningful step up from the PM level (24-40 LPA) to the Senior PM level (40-60 LPA).

Is Autodesk a good company for PMs to grow their career?

Autodesk offers exposure to complex B2B products, a large platform ecosystem, and a customer base of professional users across multiple industries, which is genuinely differentiating experience for a PM's career. Growth opportunities depend heavily on your team and manager. Industry surveys suggest that PMs who spend time at large enterprise software companies develop strong skills in stakeholder management, data-driven prioritisation, and working with technical customer segments.
