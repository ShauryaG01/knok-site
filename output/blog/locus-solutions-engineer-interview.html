01 Overview

Overview

Locus is a supply chain intelligence platform that helps enterprises optimise dispatch and last-mile delivery. A Solutions Engineer at Locus bridges the technical and commercial sides of a deal: you understand a prospect's existing logistics setup, demonstrate how the Locus platform solves their specific pain points, and support the sales team through technical evaluations and proof-of-concept builds.

As of mid-2026, Locus has 18 open Solutions Engineer roles, and the broader market shows strong demand with 1,270 Solutions Engineer openings across India. Bangalore leads with 55 of those positions, followed by Mumbai (23) and Delhi (20).

The interview process at Locus typically runs over several rounds: a recruiter screen, a technical product knowledge discussion, a live demo or case presentation, and a final round with a senior stakeholder. Candidates report that Locus values people who can speak to both the engineering side of logistics tech and the business outcomes a client cares about. If you have exposure to supply chain, SaaS pre-sales, or API-driven integrations, that experience will come up early.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These are questions candidates commonly report hearing across Locus Solutions Engineer interviews. Prepare concrete examples for each.

  1. Walk us through how you would run a technical discovery call with a new logistics prospect.
  2. How do you handle a situation where the prospect's requirement is outside Locus's current product capabilities?
  3. Explain the Locus platform as if you are speaking to a VP of Operations at a large FMCG company who has never heard of it.
  4. Describe a time you turned a technical objection into a reason to move forward with a deal.
  5. How would you set up a proof-of-concept for a client who wants to compare Locus against a competing routing solution?
  6. What metrics would you present to a CFO to prove the ROI of a last-mile optimisation platform?
  7. A client's integration team says the Locus API does not support their ERP. How do you respond?
  8. How do you decide which prospects get a full technical demo versus a shorter discovery call?
  9. Tell us about a time you had to learn a new technical domain quickly to support a sales cycle.
  10. How would you handle a prospect who is content with their current manual dispatch process and sees no urgency to switch?
  11. Describe how you co-ordinate with the product team when a prospect requests a feature that does not yet exist.
  12. What does a successful pre-sales to customer success handoff look like, and how do you make sure it happens cleanly?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time you turned a technical objection into a reason to move forward.

*Situation:* A retail client in a late-stage evaluation raised concerns that our platform's API response times could slow down their warehouse management system during peak dispatch windows.

*Task:* I needed to address the concern with real evidence rather than reassurance, and keep the deal from stalling.

*Action:* I asked for a brief technical call with their integration lead to understand their exact throughput requirements. I pulled benchmark data from a comparable client deployment and put together a short side-by-side comparison showing how our async processing handles volume spikes. I also invited a product engineer to join part of the call to speak directly to the architecture.

*Result:* The integration lead was satisfied after the call. The objection was removed from their risk log and the deal moved to contract stage within the same quarter.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you had to learn a new technical domain quickly.

*Situation:* I joined a pursuit where a prospect was already deep into evaluating our platform for cold-chain logistics. I had not worked in cold-chain before, and the client was asking detailed questions about temperature-controlled routing constraints.

*Task:* I needed to get credible on the domain in time to lead the next demo session without losing the client's confidence.

*Action:* I spent several days reading industry documentation and spoke with a couple of our existing cold-chain customers to understand their real pain points. I then worked through our product documentation with the implementation team and built a demo script that mapped cold-chain-specific terminology directly to the features we were showcasing.

*Result:* The demo received strong positive feedback from the client's operations head, who noted that our team understood their world better than a competitor they had also evaluated. We moved to a paid pilot shortly after.

---

Q: How do you handle it when a prospect's requirement is outside your product's current capabilities?

*Situation:* During a late-stage evaluation, a large e-commerce client asked whether Locus could natively integrate with a homegrown TMS they had built in-house, which was not on our standard connector list.

*Task:* I needed to be honest about the gap without losing the deal or the relationship.

*Action:* I acknowledged the gap clearly on the same call rather than deferring it. I walked through two realistic options: a custom integration via our API with their own engineering team, and a phased go-live on standard connectors first with the custom build scoped for a later phase. I then looped in our product team to confirm feasibility and a rough timeline for the custom path.

*Result:* The client appreciated the transparency. They chose the phased approach, signed the contract for phase one, and the custom integration was added to the renewal roadmap.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For technical objections: Use a three-part structure. First, validate the concern (show you have heard it properly). Second, ask a clarifying question to understand the real technical constraint, not just the surface complaint. Third, respond with data or a concrete next step, not a promise. Candidates who try to talk past objections lose trust quickly at Locus.

For 'explain our product' questions: Use the problem-solution-outcome sequence. Start with the pain the prospect has (fragmented dispatch, high delivery costs, poor visibility). Then show how the platform addresses it. Then land on a business outcome such as faster deliveries or lower cost per shipment. Avoid feature-listing without connecting to a business result.

For discovery call questions: Structure your answer around three phases: understanding the current state (what tools they use, what breaks down), defining the ideal state (what good looks like for them), and agreeing on a next step. Show that you listen before you pitch.

For cross-functional co-ordination questions: Be specific about who you loop in and when. Locus interviewers want to see that you know the boundary between what you can commit to and what needs product or engineering to weigh in. Saying 'I would loop in the product team before making any commitment on timelines' signals maturity.

For ROI and metrics questions: Think in terms of what a CFO actually cares about: cost per delivery, fleet utilisation, failed delivery rates. Avoid vague statements like 'efficiency gains.' Anchor your answer in a metric you have actually tracked, even if from a different industry context.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Locus Solutions Engineer interviewers are typically looking for a specific combination of skills. Understanding what they value helps you frame every answer correctly.

Domain credibility in logistics or supply chain. You do not need to be a logistics expert on day one, but you should be able to speak the language: dispatch, last-mile, routing, SLA, delivery density. Candidates who cannot engage with logistics terminology struggle to build rapport with the operations stakeholders they will be selling to.

Technical fluency without engineering depth. You should be comfortable discussing APIs, integrations, and data flows at a conceptual level. You do not need to write code, but you should be able to have a credible conversation with a client's tech team. Understanding REST APIs and webhook-based architectures is a common expectation.

Commercial awareness. This is a pre-sales role. Interviewers want to see that you understand deal stages, what makes a prospect move forward or stall, and how your work connects to revenue. Purely technical candidates who cannot speak to timelines, stakeholders, or urgency tend not to advance.

Communication clarity. Locus works with large enterprise clients whose decision-makers range from CTOs to warehouse managers. Your ability to adjust your communication style for each audience is something interviewers test directly, often through 'explain this to a non-technical person' style questions.

Collaborative instinct. Solutions Engineers at Locus work closely with sales, product, and customer success. Interviewers look for candidates who flag gaps to the right team rather than working around them solo.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Understand Locus and the role first. Read everything publicly available about Locus: their website, recent news, case studies, and any content from their team on LinkedIn. Understand their core product (dispatch optimisation, route planning, delivery intelligence). Map out which industries they serve most heavily. Review the job description line by line and note every skill mentioned.

Build your logistics vocabulary. If you are not from a logistics background, spend focused time learning the basics: how last-mile delivery works, what a TMS is, what SLAs mean in a delivery context, and how dispatch optimisation creates business value. Locus publishes case studies that are a useful starting point.

Practise your answers out loud. Take the questions in this guide and prepare answers using the STAR structure. Focus on making the outcomes specific to your own experience. Practise explaining the Locus platform out loud as if to a VP of Operations who has never heard of it. Recording yourself helps catch filler words and unclear explanations.

Prepare smart questions to ask. Good ones include asking how the Solutions Engineering team works with the product team on feature requests, and what a typical handoff to customer success looks like. These show you understand the role beyond demo delivery.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating it as a pure technical interview. Candidates sometimes over-prepare on product knowledge and under-prepare on commercial thinking. Locus interviewers care as much about how you handle a stalling deal as they do about whether you can describe the API architecture.

Vague answers without outcomes. Saying 'I helped a client with an integration issue' is not enough. You need the result: did the deal close, did the objection get resolved, did the client move to the next stage? If you cannot measure it, describe the qualitative outcome clearly.

Over-promising in hypothetical scenarios. A common trap is the 'what if the prospect wants something we do not have' question. Candidates who say they would 'find a way to make it work' without escalating to product raise red flags. Honesty and correct escalation matter more than optimism.

Not knowing the Locus product. This seems obvious but candidates report being caught off-guard when asked to describe a Locus feature in detail. Spend time with their public demo content, blog posts, and case studies before the interview.

Ignoring the logistics context. Generic SaaS pre-sales answers that could apply to any product do not land well. Try to frame every example in terms of delivery, dispatch, routing, or supply chain wherever possible.

Skipping discovery before the pitch. In role-play or case questions, many candidates jump straight into demoing features. Locus interviewers typically want to see that you ask clarifying questions and understand the prospect's situation before you start pitching.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Locus Solutions Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically involves three to four rounds. These usually include a recruiter screen, a technical or product discussion, a demo or case presentation, and a final round with a senior leader. The exact structure can vary by team and hiring manager, so it is worth asking your recruiter what to expect after the first call.

Do I need a background in logistics to get this role at Locus?

A logistics background helps but is not always required. Candidates with strong SaaS pre-sales experience in adjacent domains like supply chain software, ERP, or fleet management have cleared the process by picking up the logistics vocabulary quickly. What matters more is your ability to absorb domain knowledge fast and apply it in client conversations. Locus does expect you to engage credibly with operations stakeholders, so some preparation on last-mile and dispatch concepts is advisable before your first round.

What salary can I expect for a Solutions Engineer role at Locus?

Locus does not publish fixed salary bands publicly. Glassdoor and levels.fyi have community-reported figures for Solutions Engineer roles at Indian SaaS companies, which you can use as a reference point for negotiation. Your offer will typically depend on your years of experience, your prior pre-sales track record, and the seniority of the specific role. It is reasonable to ask the recruiter for a band early in the process so you are not surprised at the offer stage.

How technical does the Locus Solutions Engineer interview get?

Candidates report the technical questions stay at a conceptual level rather than a coding level. You should be comfortable discussing REST APIs, webhook integrations, and how enterprise systems like ERP and TMS connect to a SaaS platform. You will not typically be asked to write code, but you should be able to have a credible conversation with a client's tech team about how an integration would work and where the risks lie.

Is there a case study or presentation round?

Many candidates report a round where they are asked to present a mock demo or respond to a hypothetical client scenario. This is where Locus tests your ability to communicate clearly, handle objections, and balance technical detail with business outcomes. Practising a short Locus product pitch out loud before this round is strongly recommended, ideally with someone playing the role of a sceptical client so you get used to handling pushback in real time.
