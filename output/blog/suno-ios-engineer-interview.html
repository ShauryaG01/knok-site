01 Overview

Overview

Suno is an AI-powered music generation platform where users type a text prompt and get back a full original song, complete with vocals, instruments, and lyrics. The iOS app is the primary way users interact with Suno on the go, making the iOS Engineering team a core product team rather than a maintenance function.

Suno had 62 open roles across all teams as of July 2026. The iOS Engineer position typically sits within the core mobile product team. Candidates report that the hiring process typically includes a recruiter call, a technical phone screen, a coding assessment (take-home or live), and a final virtual loop with engineers and a product manager. Suno moves quickly, so expect the process to be lean and direct.

Among iOS Engineer openings tracked across India as of July 2026, Delhi leads with 21 roles, followed by Bangalore (6), Mumbai (3), and Chennai (2).

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions reflect what candidates report encountering most often in Suno iOS Engineer interviews, shaped by the nature of the product:

  1. Walk us through how you would build a real-time audio waveform visualiser inside a SwiftUI view.
  2. How would you design a caching layer for AI-generated audio so users can replay tracks without hitting the API again?
  3. Explain how you would use async/await or Combine to stream audio data progressively while the AI is still generating the track.
  4. How do you ensure background audio playback works reliably and responds correctly to Control Centre commands and audio interruptions?
  5. Suno lets users remix songs. How would you design local state so a remix session supports full undo and redo?
  6. Describe a time you diagnosed and fixed a significant performance problem in an iOS app. What tools did you use and what was the outcome?
  7. How would you implement a custom gesture recogniser for a music timeline scrubber that feels precise and natural?
  8. What is your strategy for unit-testing code that is tightly coupled to AVFoundation?
  9. A song-generation API call is still running after the user has navigated away from the screen. How do you handle the result when it eventually arrives?
  10. How do you maintain backward compatibility in local data models when the server schema changes between app versions?
  11. The product is music, but some users are deaf or hard of hearing. How would you approach accessibility for them?
  12. Tell us about a technical or product decision you pushed back on and how you handled that conversation.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How would you design a caching layer for AI-generated audio?

*Situation:* At my previous company, we built a podcast app where audio files were large and users frequently replayed episodes. Server costs for repeated streaming were rising steadily.

*Task:* I was asked to design a local caching solution that would serve replays from device storage without breaking the streaming experience for first-time plays.

*Action:* I built a two-tier cache using NSCache for in-memory buffering of recent tracks and a disk cache with an LRU eviction policy. Each cached file was keyed by a hash of the track URL plus a version timestamp. I used URLSession background download tasks so caching continued even when the user left the screen. I also set a storage cap so the app would not consume more than a reasonable portion of device space.

*Result:* Replay latency dropped to near zero for cached tracks. App store reviews mentioning 'buffering' fell noticeably over the following release cycle, and the team reported reduced bandwidth costs.

---

Q: Describe a time you fixed a significant performance bottleneck in an iOS app.

*Situation:* Our app had a social feed showing user-generated content, including short video clips. On older devices, scrolling was visibly janky and frame drops made the app feel low quality.

*Task:* I was responsible for getting the feed to scroll smoothly on devices going back a couple of model generations.

*Action:* I profiled the app using Instruments and found that cell configuration was decoding images on the main thread. I moved decoding to a background queue and added ahead-of-scroll prefetching via UICollectionViewDataSourcePrefetching. I also replaced auto-layout-heavy cells with manual frame calculation on the critical rendering path and corrected missing cell reuse.

*Result:* Frame drops in the feed fell significantly after the fix. The scroll experience felt smooth even on older test devices, and the change shipped without regressions in the next sprint.

---

Q: Tell us about a product decision you pushed back on.

*Situation:* The product team wanted to auto-play the next AI-generated song immediately after the current one finished, with no visible opt-out, to boost session time.

*Task:* I needed to raise a concern without coming across as obstructionist or dismissing a legitimate product goal.

*Action:* I ran a quick informal test with five colleagues and observed that three of them reached to stop playback when the next song started unexpectedly. I brought these observations to the next planning sync, framed the concern as a trust issue rather than a personal preference, and proposed a middle ground: auto-play on by default but with a visible 'next' and 'stop' control surfaced during a short countdown.

*Result:* The team adopted the compromise. The opt-out rate after launch was low, meaning most users were happy with auto-play once they understood they could stop it, and the feature shipped without negative reviews about forced playback.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use STAR for behavioural questions. Structure your answer as Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task brief (two to three sentences each) and spend most of your time on Action. Interviewers at product-led companies like Suno want to understand how you think and what you personally did, not just what the outcome was.

Use a design walk-through for architecture questions. Start with requirements and constraints ('What does the user actually need? What are the device or network limits?'). Propose a high-level design first, then drill into the component the interviewer asks about. Invite clarification early: 'Does this constraint match what you see in production?' This signals collaborative instinct.

Use a debugging story for performance questions. Lead with the symptom, name the diagnostic tool you used (Instruments, os_signpost, the memory graph debugger), explain the root cause, describe the fix, and state the measured result. If you do not remember an exact figure, estimate and say so. An honest estimate with context is more credible than silence.

For product or accessibility questions, lead with the user need before describing any technical implementation. Interviewers at a consumer AI startup want to see that you build for outcomes, not just for engineering elegance.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Suno builds at the intersection of AI and consumer audio, and interviewers are looking for a specific combination of qualities.

Swift fluency. Candidates are typically expected to be comfortable with Swift concurrency (async/await, actors, structured task cancellation), memory management (ARC, weak and unowned references), and both UIKit and SwiftUI. Gaps in any of these tend to surface quickly in a phone screen.

AVFoundation depth. Because the core product is audio, hands-on experience with AVAudioSession, AVPlayer, background audio modes, and interruption handling is a real differentiator. If you have built a custom audio player or worked with audio processing, bring it up.

Product instinct. Suno is a startup that ships frequently. Interviewers want to see that you think about user experience alongside code correctness. Be ready to discuss decisions where you traded engineering elegance for shipping speed, and ones where you held the line on quality.

Comfort with ambiguity. Candidates report that interviewers use open-ended design questions to observe how you structure thinking under uncertainty. There is often no single right answer. Interviewers watch for whether you ask clarifying questions before diving in.

Ownership mentality. Expect probes into whether you see your job as writing code or shipping outcomes. Stories where you caught a bug before it reached users, or flagged a UX problem outside your direct scope, tend to resonate well.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1, core Swift and concurrency. Re-read Swift's concurrency documentation, focusing on structured concurrency, task cancellation, and actor isolation. Write small programs from scratch that stream data using async/await and cancel midway. Review ARC and identify a past experience with a retain cycle or memory leak that you can walk through clearly in an interview.

Week 1, AVFoundation practice. Build or revisit a basic audio player using AVPlayer. Wire it up to Control Centre via MPNowPlayingInfoCenter and test interruption handling for phone calls and Siri. If you have not worked with audio APIs before, this practice is non-negotiable given the nature of the product.

Week 2, product immersion. Use the Suno iOS app daily. Note what delights you and what frustrates you. Prepare two or three specific observations you could share if asked what you would improve. Always frame observations with the user problem first, then your proposed solution.

Week 2, behavioural preparation. Write out five STAR stories covering: a performance win, a hard technical decision, a time you disagreed with the team, a failure and what you learned, and a project you are most proud of. Practise each one out loud in under three minutes.

The day before. Review your stories once. Confirm your coding environment works if the interview includes a live coding session. Get proper rest and do not try to cram new Swift APIs the night before.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping straight into code. For architecture or design questions, candidates who start writing pseudocode before clarifying requirements often end up solving the wrong problem. Spend a moment asking one or two constraint questions before you begin.

Treating audio as a black box. Many iOS engineers have limited AVFoundation exposure. When the product is built around audio and you cannot speak to AVAudioSession categories, interruption handling, or background modes, that gap shows immediately.

Vague STAR answers. Answers like 'the team worked together and we shipped it' tell an interviewer nothing about you specifically. Be precise about what you personally did, what decision was yours, and what the measurable outcome was. If you do not remember exact figures, estimate and say so.

Not using the product before the interview. Candidates who have never opened the Suno app miss easy opportunities to show genuine product empathy. Download it, use it, and form a real opinion before you show up.

Underestimating accessibility questions. Suno's product is audio, which makes accessibility questions both likely and strategic. Answers that treat accessibility as a compliance checkbox miss what the interviewer is actually probing: whether you design for all users, not just the typical case.

Over-engineering take-home submissions. Candidates sometimes submit elaborate abstractions that are hard to read and show low signal-to-noise. Clean, readable code that directly solves the stated problem tends to land better than impressive-looking architecture for a small assignment.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

What is the typical interview process for an iOS Engineer at Suno?

Candidates report a process that typically starts with a recruiter call to discuss background and interest, followed by a technical phone screen covering Swift fundamentals and iOS system design. There is usually a coding assessment, either as a take-home or in a live session, and the process typically ends with a virtual loop involving engineers and a product manager. Exact rounds and timelines can vary, so ask your recruiter for the current format at the outset.

Do I need prior experience in audio or music apps to get this role?

Not necessarily, but strong AVFoundation knowledge is clearly relevant given what Suno builds. Candidates report that audio session management, background playback, and interruption handling are areas that come up in technical screens. If you lack direct audio experience, building a basic AVPlayer-based app in the week before your interview can close that gap meaningfully and give you something concrete to discuss.

Is SwiftUI or UIKit more relevant for this role?

Suno has not published detailed information about their internal stack, so it is best to be comfortable in both and to ask your recruiter or interviewer which the team leans on. Many modern iOS apps at startups mix both frameworks, and demonstrating fluency in SwiftUI alongside a clear understanding of when UIKit is the right tool tends to read well across teams.

What salary can I expect for an iOS Engineer role at Suno?

Suno has not published official salary bands for their iOS roles. Glassdoor and industry surveys can give a rough sense of iOS Engineer compensation at funded AI startups, though figures vary widely by experience level and negotiation. It is worth asking your recruiter for the band early in the process so you are not caught off guard at the offer stage.

How active is the iOS Engineer job market right now?

Job data from July 2026 shows 70 iOS Engineer openings tracked across major Indian cities, with Delhi accounting for 21 of those roles and Bangalore, Mumbai, and Chennai also showing demand. Suno had 62 open roles across all functions at the same point, suggesting active hiring across the company. Publicly reported hiring trends indicate that senior iOS engineers with AI or audio product experience are in a particularly strong position.

Should I prepare for a system design round?

Candidates report that architecture and design questions appear in both the technical phone screen and the final loop, typically focused on mobile-specific challenges such as offline caching, background task handling, or real-time data streaming. Practise designing a feature end-to-end out loud, starting from user requirements and working down to implementation choices, rather than relying on backend system design patterns that do not map cleanly to mobile contexts.
