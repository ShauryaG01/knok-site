Most Asked Questions

The following question themes are commonly reported by candidates who have interviewed for BA roles at fintech companies like abra. Candidates report these areas appearing frequently across rounds.

  1. Walk me through how you would define the key success metrics for a new crypto investment feature.
  2. Abra's user retention drops after the first transaction. How would you investigate the root cause?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a time you had to present a data-driven recommendation to a non-technical stakeholder.

*Situation:* I was a BA at a payments startup where the marketing team wanted to launch a broad re-engagement campaign to all churned users.

*Task:* My job was to identify which segment of churned users was actually worth targeting, rather than sending the campaign to the entire list.

*Action:* I built a cohort analysis comparing users who churned after their first transaction against those who had made several transactions before going inactive. I used a multi-week retention window to define 'active', then visualised the difference in reactivation likelihood for each group. I kept the presentation to two slides: one showing the data clearly, one with the recommendation in plain language.

*Result:* The marketing team targeted only the multi-transaction cohort. Reactivation rates for that segment were notably higher than the previous blanket campaign, and cost per reactivation dropped significantly. Stakeholders said the clear framing was why they trusted the recommendation quickly.

---

Q: A product manager says 'engagement is down'. How do you break that statement into something measurable and actionable?

*Situation:* At a previous role, the PM flagged that 'users seem less engaged' after a product update, without pointing to specific data.

*Task:* I needed to translate a vague concern into a structured investigation so we could identify where to act.

*Action:* I first asked the PM which engagement metrics they were watching and over what time window. Then I broke 'engagement' into components: session frequency, feature usage depth, and transaction volume per user. I queried each metric separately, segmented by user cohort and acquisition channel, and built a simple dashboard so the PM could explore the data themselves going forward.

*Result:* We discovered that engagement had increased for newer users but dropped for users acquired many months earlier. This pointed to a retention issue for older cohorts, not a general product problem. The PM redirected effort to a targeted retention campaign rather than reverting the product update.

---

Q: How would you prioritise three product improvements when you have limited engineering bandwidth?

*Situation:* Our product team had three feature requests queued: an improved search filter, a portfolio summary dashboard, and an in-app notification system. Only one engineer was available for the quarter.

*Task:* I had to help the PM decide which to build first.

*Action:* I set up a simple scoring matrix using three criteria: estimated user impact (based on support ticket frequency and survey data), alignment with the company's current growth goal, and implementation complexity as estimated by the engineer. I assigned a score of 1-3 for each criterion and presented the matrix to the PM and engineering lead together so everyone could debate the scores openly.

*Result:* The portfolio summary dashboard scored highest because it addressed the top support complaint and required the least engineering effort. We shipped it first. The PM later said the structured matrix made the decision easy to defend to leadership.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

The STAR framework (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the most reliable structure for behavioural questions. Keep the Situation and Task brief, spend most of your time on the Action, and always close with a concrete Result.

For metric questions, use a decomposition approach. Break the top-level metric (like 'engagement' or 'revenue') into its component parts before jumping to conclusions. State the components out loud so the interviewer can follow your reasoning.

For SQL or analytical problems, verbalise your approach before writing any code. A short statement like 'I will filter for active users, then join the transactions table, then aggregate by user' shows structured thinking even if your syntax is not perfect.

For prioritisation questions, use a simple scoring matrix or a value-vs-effort grid. Bring up concrete criteria such as user impact, business alignment, and technical complexity, rather than relying on gut feel. Interviewers at data-driven companies like abra typically want to see a repeatable framework, not just an answer.

For ambiguous open-ended questions, clarify before you solve. Ask one focused clarifying question, state your assumption if the interviewer says 'go ahead', and then proceed. This signals analytical maturity.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Abra interviewers, like most fintech BA panels, typically look for a few core qualities beyond technical skill.

Comfort with financial data. Because abra works with crypto and digital assets, expect questions that involve transaction data, portfolio metrics, or user financial behaviour. You do not need to be a crypto expert, but being able to reason about financial flows and user risk profiles helps.

SQL fluency under pressure. Candidates report that live SQL problems are common. You should be comfortable writing queries that involve joins, aggregations, and window functions. Practise thinking out loud while writing.

Structured communication. BAs at abra work across product, engineering, and business teams. Interviewers want to see that you can translate data findings into clear, jargon-free language. Avoid over-complicating your explanations.

Ownership mindset. Stories where you proactively identified a problem, rather than waiting to be assigned one, tend to land well. Interviewers look for candidates who treat ambiguity as an opportunity to dig in, not a blocker.

Business context awareness. Understand what abra does, who its users are, and what metrics likely matter most to a digital assets platform (acquisition, retention, transaction volume, trust). Showing this context in your answers signals genuine interest.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week one: sharpen your SQL. Practise queries on transaction-style datasets. Focus on window functions (RANK, ROW_NUMBER, LAG/LEAD), CTEs for multi-step problems, and aggregation with GROUP BY. The LeetCode SQL section and Mode Analytics practice problems are solid starting points.

Week one (continued): review metrics fundamentals. Revisit how to define and decompose product metrics: activation rate, retention, churn, lifetime value. Be able to draw a simple funnel from memory and explain where a drop-off investigation would start.

Week two: prepare five to six STAR stories. Map them to the question themes listed above. Cover at least one story about stakeholder communication, one about conflicting priorities, and one where you found a surprising insight in data.

Week two (continued): research abra. Read their product pages and any recent coverage of the platform. With 128 open roles, the company is clearly in a growth phase. Glassdoor reviews can give you a feel for culture and interview style. Connect your preparation to a scaling context.

Day before the interview: Review your STAR stories out loud. Practise metric decomposition on a couple of hypothetical scenarios. Get comfortable with your SQL approach for window functions in particular.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping to conclusions without decomposing. Many candidates hear 'engagement is down' and immediately suggest a fix. Always break the metric into parts first. Interviewers notice when you skip the diagnostic step.

Vague STAR results. Saying 'the project went well' is not a result. Where possible, describe the outcome in concrete terms even without a specific number. 'The reactivation rate for our target segment was notably higher than the previous campaign' is far stronger than 'it was successful'.

Writing SQL without explaining it. In a live coding question, silence is risky. Talk through what each clause is doing. If you make a mistake, catching it out loud is far better than submitting wrong code quietly.

Ignoring the business context. BAs who answer purely in technical terms often miss the mark. Always connect your analysis back to what the business cares about.

Not asking clarifying questions. Open-ended questions are designed to be ambiguous. Candidates who dive in without clarifying assumptions often solve the wrong problem. One focused question at the start shows good analytical instinct.

Underestimating the communication round. Candidates report that abra, like most fintech firms, places heavy weight on how clearly you explain your thinking. Even a technically correct answer can fall flat if the explanation is hard to follow.
