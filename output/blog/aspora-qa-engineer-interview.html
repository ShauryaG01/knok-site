03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time you caught a critical bug just before a release.

*Situation:* I was on the QA team for a payments feature at my previous company, two days before a production release.

*Task:* My job was to complete the final regression pass on the core transaction flow and give sign-off.

*Action:* During exploratory testing I noticed that when a user entered an amount with a decimal and then changed currency, the conversion logic was silently dropping the decimal portion. I logged the bug with full reproduction steps, screenshots, and API response logs, then flagged it directly to the tech lead and product manager rather than just raising a ticket and waiting.

*Result:* The release was held for three days while the calculation logic was patched. The tech lead told me the defect would have affected a large number of transactions. It reinforced my view that thorough exploratory testing on edge cases is worth the extra time, especially in financial products.

---

Q: How do you handle a situation where a developer insists a bug is 'by design'?

*Situation:* At a previous role, a developer marked a bug I raised as 'not a bug, working as intended.' The issue was a form that allowed submission with a blank mandatory field under a specific browser condition.

*Task:* I needed to either accept the call or make a case for fixing it, without damaging the working relationship.

*Action:* I went back to the original requirements document and the acceptance criteria. The criteria clearly stated the field was mandatory. I prepared a short document tying the user scenario to the acceptance criteria and the potential customer impact, then asked for a brief call with the developer and product manager together.

*Result:* Once the product manager saw the acceptance criteria alongside the impact, the bug was reopened and fixed before the sprint ended. I learned to anchor QA disagreements in written requirements rather than personal judgement.

---

Q: How would you approach testing an API endpoint end to end?

*Situation:* At my last job, I was asked to own QA for a new notification API that multiple mobile and web clients would consume.

*Task:* I needed to build a thorough test plan covering both happy paths and failure scenarios before any client teams integrated.

*Action:* I started by reviewing the API contract in the OpenAPI docs, then wrote test cases covering valid inputs, boundary values, missing required fields, invalid data types, authentication failures, and rate limit behaviour. I used Postman for exploratory testing and then automated the regression suite so it could run in CI. I also coordinated with the frontend team to run integration testing as soon as a staging environment was ready.

*Result:* We caught three integration issues that would not have surfaced in unit tests alone, and the API shipped with zero P1 defects on launch day.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions (Situation, Task, Action, Result): use this for any question starting with 'Tell me about a time' or 'Describe a situation.' Keep the Situation and Task brief (a sentence or two each), spend most of your time on the Action, and always close with a concrete Result. If you do not have a perfect match, say 'The closest situation I have is...' rather than fabricating details.

Risk-based framing for prioritisation questions: When asked how you prioritise bugs or testing effort, show that you think in terms of customer impact and likelihood. A practical frame: how many users are affected, how severely, and how often does this path get used? This reads as more mature than simply reciting severity labels.

Requirements anchoring for disagreement questions: Always bring disputes back to written requirements or acceptance criteria. This removes the 'my opinion versus your opinion' dynamic and shows you operate on evidence, not instinct.

Shift-left framing for process questions: Aspora likely values engineers who get involved early. When describing your process, mention points where you engaged with requirements or designs before development started. This signals that you think of QA as a practice, not just a phase at the end of the sprint.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates typically report from fintech and payments product companies like aspora, interviewers look for several things beyond basic testing knowledge.

Ownership mindset: They want to see that you treat quality as your responsibility across the full delivery cycle, not just a gate at sprint end. Candidates who mention raising quality concerns during planning sessions, not only during testing, tend to stand out.

Precision in communication: In a product where financial accuracy matters, how precisely you describe bugs, reproduction steps, and outcomes signals whether you can be trusted. Vague descriptions land badly.

Automation pragmatism: You do not need to be a full-stack developer, but you should have a clear view on what to automate and why. Candidates who explain their automation strategy, not just list tool names, get much further.

Customer empathy: Because aspora serves diaspora customers sending money across borders, interviewers respond well to candidates who test from the customer's perspective, not just against the spec.

Collaboration under pressure: QA often means delivering unwelcome news close to a deadline. Show that you can do this calmly and constructively, and that you bring solutions alongside the problems.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Phase 1: Research and mapping (first few days of your prep window)
Read aspora's product pages, LinkedIn, and any available engineering content. Map every skill in the job description to a concrete example from your past work. Note gaps and decide what to focus on.

Phase 2: Technical refresh
Revise core QA fundamentals: test case design techniques (equivalence partitioning, boundary value analysis, decision tables), the defect lifecycle, and test planning. If the job description mentions automation tools such as Selenium, Cypress, Playwright, or Postman, write and run actual scripts so you can speak to them with hands-on examples rather than theory.

Phase 3: Scenario practice
Write out STAR answers for at least 5 behavioural questions from the list in this guide. Practice them out loud, not just in your head. Aim for 2-3 minutes per answer and cut anything that does not add to the Action or Result.

Phase 4: Mock interview and final review (last 2-3 days before the interview)
Do at least one full mock interview with a peer or mentor, or record yourself and play it back. Prepare specific questions to ask aspora's panel, such as how they measure test coverage, what the release cadence looks like, and how QA is structured in relation to product and engineering.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Over-explaining the Situation: Candidates often spend half their time on background and rush the Action. Interviewers want to hear what you did. Get to it quickly.

Listing tools instead of demonstrating judgement: Saying 'I know Selenium, Cypress, Postman, and JIRA' is table stakes. What interviewers want to hear is when you used each tool, why you chose it, and what resulted.

Vague bug descriptions: If asked to walk through a bug you raised, candidates often describe it loosely. Practice describing bugs the way you would write a ticket: steps to reproduce, expected versus actual behaviour, environment, and severity.

Not asking questions at the end: Candidates who say 'No, I think you covered everything' leave an opportunity on the table. Prepare specific questions about aspora's QA practices before you walk in.

Ignoring the payments and fintech context: Generic QA answers that could apply to any software company miss the mark here. Tie your examples to data accuracy, transaction reliability, or customer trust wherever you can.

Underplaying cross-functional work: QA roles at product companies require a lot of coordination with developers, PMs, and support teams. If you only talk about tools and test cases, you leave a gap in how you come across.
