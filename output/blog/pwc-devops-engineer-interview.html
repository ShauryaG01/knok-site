02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up consistently in PwC DevOps interviews, based on what candidates report:

  1. Walk me through a CI/CD pipeline you built end-to-end. What tools did you choose and why?
  2. PwC works with large enterprise clients. How do you handle infrastructure-as-code at scale, and how do you manage drift?
  3. Describe a production incident you owned. How did you detect it, respond, and prevent recurrence?
  4. How do you embed compliance and security checks into a delivery pipeline for a regulated industry client? What gates or policies do you enforce?
  5. We often work in multi-cloud environments. How do you approach deploying the same application across two or more cloud providers?
  6. Explain how you would design a secrets management strategy for a large organisation with many development teams.
  7. How do you balance developer velocity with security and governance requirements?
  8. A client wants to reduce deployment lead time from once a week to multiple times per day. What is your approach?
  9. Describe your experience with container orchestration. How have you handled pod scheduling, resource limits, or cluster upgrades in production?
  10. How do you monitor a distributed microservices architecture? Walk us through your observability stack.
  11. PwC values knowledge transfer and documentation. How do you ensure your infrastructure work is maintainable by a team after you leave?
  12. Tell me about a time you had to convince a risk-averse client or stakeholder to adopt an automated approach.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk me through a CI/CD pipeline you built end-to-end.

*Situation:* My previous employer was deploying a monolithic Java application manually every two weeks, leading to frequent rollback incidents.

*Task:* I was asked to design and implement a fully automated pipeline within a tight deadline.

*Action:* I set up a Git-based trunk-development workflow, added static analysis and unit test gates as the first stage, then containerised the application using Docker. I wrote Terraform modules for the staging and production environments on AWS and used a blue-green deployment strategy so rollbacks could happen in under two minutes. I also added automated smoke tests that ran post-deploy before traffic was shifted.

*Result:* Deployment frequency moved from fortnightly to daily. Rollback time dropped significantly. The pattern I documented was later adopted by two other teams in the organisation.

---

Q: Describe a production incident you owned.

*Situation:* A database connection pool on a client-facing service exhausted itself during a flash sale, taking the checkout flow offline.

*Task:* As the on-call engineer, I needed to restore service quickly and then find the root cause.

*Action:* I immediately scaled out the application pods to reduce per-instance connections, then worked with the database team to identify a slow query holding connections open. I added a connection timeout and a circuit breaker in the code. After the incident I wrote a blameless post-mortem, added an alert for connection pool utilisation above a safe threshold, and ran a load test to validate the fix.

*Result:* The service recovered within minutes. The post-mortem led to a shared runbook that the whole SRE team now uses for similar incidents.

---

Q: How do you balance developer velocity with security and governance requirements?

*Situation:* A product team I worked with was bypassing the security scanning gate because it added too much time to their pipeline.

*Task:* I needed to make security fast enough that developers would not want to bypass it.

*Action:* I analysed where the scanning time was going and found the tool was re-scanning unchanged dependencies on every run. I switched to an incremental scanning mode and added caching for the dependency graph. I also moved critical security gates earlier (shift-left) so developers got feedback in their local environment before pushing, reducing pipeline-level failures.

*Result:* Scanning time dropped to under two minutes. Bypass requests stopped. The team later volunteered to extend the same approach to a second project.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use STAR for experience questions. Every 'tell me about a time' question at PwC expects a tight Situation, Task, Action, Result structure. Keep Situation and Task brief (two to three sentences combined) so most of your time goes on Action and Result.

Use a decision-log format for tool-choice questions. When asked 'why did you choose X', walk through: the constraints you faced, the options you considered, the criteria you used to decide, and what you would change today. This shows senior-level thinking rather than just name-dropping tools.

Use a layered approach for architecture questions. Start at the highest level (what problem are we solving for the business), then move down through the system design, then into specific implementation details. PwC interviewers are often assessing whether you can communicate with a client who is not an engineer, so starting at the business level matters.

Quantify results wherever possible. PwC consulting culture values measurable outcomes. Before the interview, prepare three to five metrics from your past work: deployment frequency, lead time, mean time to recover, or cost reductions. Numbers make stories credible and memorable.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

PwC DevOps interviewers are typically looking for four things:

Client-facing communication. Unlike a pure product company, PwC engineers often sit in front of clients. Interviewers want to see that you can explain infrastructure decisions without jargon and that you remain calm under pushback.

Breadth with genuine depth. You are expected to know the DevOps toolchain broadly (CI/CD, IaC, containers, cloud, observability) but also to go deep on at least one or two areas. Saying 'I have used Terraform' is not enough. Expect follow-up questions on state management, modules, and handling real-world drift.

Security and compliance awareness. PwC works heavily in regulated industries such as banking and insurance. You should be comfortable talking about embedding security gates in pipelines, managing secrets, and enforcing policy-as-code. Understand the principles behind compliance requirements rather than just citing standard names as checkboxes.

Ownership mindset. Stories that end with 'I raised the issue to my manager' tend to score lower than stories where you drove resolution. PwC looks for engineers who take end-to-end accountability, including writing post-mortems and following through on fixes.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Fundamentals and gaps. Review your weakest area among CI/CD pipelines, Kubernetes, Terraform and IaC, cloud networking, and observability. Aim to explain each from first principles, not just usage.

Week 2: PwC context. Read about PwC Technology Consulting practice areas and understand which industries they serve in India (BFSI, healthcare, retail). Map your past experience to client problems in those sectors. This makes your answers feel relevant rather than generic.

Week 3: Story preparation. Write out eight to ten STAR stories covering production incidents, pipeline builds, security improvements, cross-team collaboration, and a time you convinced a sceptical stakeholder. Practice each story out loud until you can deliver it in under two minutes.

Week 4: Mock rounds. Do at least two full mock interviews covering both technical and behavioural questions. Ask someone to challenge your answers with follow-up questions. Focus on not getting defensive and on quantifying your results.

Ongoing. Track new DevOps openings at PwC and note any shifts in job description language, as this often signals what the team is currently prioritising.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Listing tools without context. Saying 'I know Jenkins, GitHub Actions, ArgoCD, Terraform, Ansible' in one breath is a red flag. Interviewers want to know when and why you chose each tool, not just that you have used it.

Vague incident stories. Answers like 'we had an outage and I helped fix it' are too weak. PwC expects you to own a specific part of the story and explain your personal contribution clearly.

Ignoring the client layer. Candidates who talk only about technical architecture and never mention how they communicated with stakeholders or managed expectations tend to score lower at a consulting firm.

Skipping the 'why' on architecture choices. Interviewers will probe deeply. If you chose a particular deployment strategy or monitoring tool, know the trade-offs you evaluated and be ready to defend your choice.

Not asking questions. Candidates who ask nothing at the end of a round are often seen as low-curiosity. Prepare two or three specific questions about the team's current challenges, the client mix, or how DevOps practices are maturing within the firm.

Overstating certifications. A certification shows you studied for an exam. If your practical experience does not match the certification level, the technical round will surface that gap quickly.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the PwC DevOps interview typically have?

Candidates report two to four rounds, typically starting with a technical screening, followed by one or two deeper technical interviews, and a final round with a manager or director that focuses on behaviour and fit. The exact structure varies by team and level. Treat every round as potentially the last, and bring the same preparation to each one.

Is there a coding or DSA round at PwC for DevOps roles?

Candidates generally report that pure data-structures-and-algorithms questions are rare for DevOps Engineer roles at PwC. You are more likely to be asked to write a shell script, a Terraform snippet, or a Dockerfile on the spot, and to explain your reasoning. Some candidates report a light coding question to test logical thinking, so basic scripting in Python or Bash is worth practising before your interview.

What cloud platform does PwC focus on for DevOps roles in India?

PwC India works across AWS, Azure, and GCP depending on the client. Job descriptions candidates report seeing mention all three, with Azure appearing frequently in banking and enterprise client projects. Be prepared to discuss your primary cloud platform in depth and show awareness of the others. Multi-cloud deployment patterns are a common interview topic.

What is the salary range for a PwC DevOps Engineer in India?

Based on knok jobradar data, DevOps Engineers in India typically earn 6-12 LPA at entry level (0-2 years), 15-28 LPA at mid level (3-5 years), and 30-50 LPA at senior level (6-9 years). PwC-specific figures are not publicly reported in a verifiable way, so treat these market bands as a starting reference. Check Glassdoor and levels.fyi for additional data points before you negotiate.

How long does the PwC hiring process take from application to offer?

Candidates report the full process taking two to six weeks from first contact to offer, though timelines vary with team urgency and notice periods. Consulting firms sometimes move faster when a client project is about to start. Following up politely after each round is reasonable if you have not heard back within a week.

Should I apply directly on the PwC website or through a job portal?

Applying through the PwC careers page gives you a direct record in their applicant tracking system, which is generally the safest route. Job portals such as LinkedIn and Naukri are also commonly used and can surface roles you might miss.
