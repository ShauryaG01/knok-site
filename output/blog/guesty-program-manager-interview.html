01 Overview

Overview

Guesty is a property management platform built for short-term rental operators, OTAs, and hospitality businesses. As of July 2026, Guesty had 19 open roles across functions, with Technical Program Manager positions typically sitting at the intersection of engineering, product, and operations.

Candidates report a multi-stage process that typically includes a recruiter screen, a hiring manager conversation, and one or more panel rounds with engineering leads and cross-functional partners. The interview focuses heavily on how you coordinate complex programs, manage dependencies across teams, and communicate status and risk to leadership.

Knowing Guesty's domain matters. The platform connects property managers, distribution channels (like Airbnb and Booking.com), and guests, so interviewers want to see that you can think in terms of multi-sided systems and fast-moving release cycles.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates report these questions coming up frequently in Guesty TPM interviews. Questions are typically behavioral or situational, with a few role-play or case-style prompts in later rounds.

  1. Walk me through a program you owned end-to-end: scope, stakeholders, delivery, and what you would do differently.
  2. How do you track dependencies across multiple engineering teams working in parallel?
  3. Tell me about a time you had to push back on a product or engineering request. What was at stake, and how did you handle it?
  4. Describe how you managed a program that slipped its deadline. What caused the slip and what did you do?
  5. Guesty's product spans property managers, channel integrations, and guest experiences. How would you design a program cadence for a feature touching all three groups?
  6. How do you build a launch readiness checklist, and how do you decide when a release is actually ready to ship?
  7. Tell me about a time you had to align stakeholders with competing priorities. How did you get to a shared plan?
  8. How do you measure program health while a project is in flight, not just at the end?
  9. Describe your approach to identifying and communicating risk early, before it becomes a crisis.
  10. How do you ramp up in a new organisation and quickly understand the existing program landscape and key dependencies?
  11. Tell me about a production incident you helped coordinate from a program management standpoint.
  12. What does strong engineering-product alignment look like, and how have you built it in past roles?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a program you owned end-to-end.

*Situation:* My company was migrating a legacy booking API used by many downstream teams, with no hard cutover date agreed.

*Task:* I was responsible for coordinating the migration, keeping all consuming teams unblocked, and getting the old API decommissioned without a service disruption.

*Action:* I ran a kickoff with all stakeholders to map every dependency, then created a shared tracker visible to all teams. I set up a weekly sync and an async status channel so teams could flag blockers without waiting for the meeting. I worked with the API owner to set a clear deprecation date and communicated it to leadership with a risk log.

*Result:* We completed the migration on schedule with zero high-severity incidents during cutover. Several teams flagged integration issues early enough to fix them before the deadline, which would not have happened without the shared tracker.

---

Q: How have you managed a program that slipped its deadline?

*Situation:* A mobile feature release fell behind schedule because a third-party data provider changed their API contract mid-sprint.

*Task:* I needed to inform leadership, adjust the plan, and keep the team's morale intact during a stressful replanning cycle.

*Action:* I immediately flagged the slip to leadership with a clear root cause and two recovery options: ship a reduced scope on the original date, or ship full scope with a delay. I documented trade-offs for each and let the business make the call. Once the decision was made, I updated every stakeholder's expectations in writing and adjusted the sprint plan with the engineering lead.

*Result:* Leadership chose the reduced-scope option. The release shipped shortly after the original date with core functionality intact, and the remaining features followed in the next sprint. The team appreciated being kept informed rather than surprised.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you had to align stakeholders with competing priorities.

*Situation:* Product wanted to prioritise a new channel integration while engineering wanted to focus on infrastructure stability work. Both had legitimate business cases.

*Task:* My job was to surface the conflict clearly and help both sides reach a shared plan without letting it escalate to a leadership standoff.

*Action:* I set up a joint session, prepared a simple one-pager showing the capacity available, the cost of each path, and the risk of the alternative. I kept the conversation focused on outcomes rather than team preferences. I also proposed a sequencing option that let infrastructure work proceed in parallel with early discovery for the channel integration.

*Result:* Both leads agreed to the sequenced plan. The infrastructure work completed first, which actually reduced risk for the channel integration that followed. Neither team felt they had 'lost' the conversation.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the standard for behavioral questions at Guesty and most tech companies. Keep Situation and Task brief, spend most of your time on Action (what you specifically did, not what the team did), and end with a concrete Result.

For risk and dependency questions, use a simple structure: identify the risk early, quantify the impact if it hits, describe the mitigation, and explain how you communicated it. Interviewers want to see that you treat risk as a program artifact, not an afterthought.

For 'how would you' or hypothetical questions (like the Guesty multi-stakeholder cadence question), walk through your thinking step by step: who are the stakeholders, what decisions need to be made at what frequency, what does good visibility look like, and how do you keep the cadence from becoming noise. Thinking out loud is fine; interviewers are evaluating your process.

For questions about alignment and pushback, lead with the business context before the interpersonal dynamic. Explain why the disagreement mattered to the product or the customer, not just to the teams involved. This shows business judgment alongside communication skills.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Guesty TPM interviewers typically look for a specific combination of skills and traits, based on what candidates report from recent rounds.

Cross-functional coordination at scale. Guesty's platform touches property managers, distribution channels, and payment providers simultaneously. Interviewers want to see that you have managed programs with many external and internal dependencies, not just single-team sprints.

Structured communication under ambiguity. TPMs at Guesty are expected to synthesise information from multiple teams and present it clearly to leadership. Show that you write status updates, risk logs, and escalation memos in plain language, not project management jargon.

Domain curiosity. You do not need hospitality industry experience, but interviewers want to see that you have researched Guesty's product and can reason about its specific complexity: multi-channel inventory, real-time pricing, and integration with platforms like Airbnb and Booking.com.

Bias toward action. Candidates report that Guesty values TPMs who move programs forward without waiting for perfect information. Phrases like 'I called a meeting to decide' or 'I made the call and documented it' land better than 'I escalated and waited for guidance.'

Ownership without authority. Like most TPM roles, this one requires influencing without a direct reporting line. Show specific examples of how you got engineers, product managers, or external partners to move in the same direction.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know the product and the role.
Spend time on Guesty's public website and any available product demos. Understand what property management software does and how Guesty differentiates. Read the job description carefully and map each requirement to a story from your own experience.

Week 1-2: Build your story bank.
Prepare at least one strong STAR story for each of these themes: program delivery end-to-end, dependency management, stakeholder conflict, timeline slip recovery, launch readiness, and technical risk communication. Write them out, not just in your head.

Week 2: Practice out loud.
Record yourself answering questions or practice with a peer. TPM interviews reward clear, structured verbal communication. If your answers are crisp on paper but rambling out loud, you need more spoken practice.

Before each round:
Review Guesty's recent product announcements or press releases. Prepare two or three questions for each interviewer that show you have thought about the role's specific challenges, not generic questions about company culture.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Talking about the team instead of yourself. In TPM interviews, every answer that says 'we did' without explaining what you personally did costs you points. Interviewers are evaluating your judgment and your actions, not your team's.

Vague risk management answers. Saying 'I flagged the risk to the team' is not enough. Interviewers want to know: what was the risk, how did you quantify it, who did you tell, and what was the mitigation plan? Prepare specific examples.

Ignoring Guesty's product. Candidates who walk in without knowing what property management software does, or who have never heard of OTA integrations, typically struggle with the situational questions. Some product research before each round makes a visible difference.

Over-engineering the 'how would you' answers. When asked how you would approach something hypothetical, a clear 3-4 step process beats a very long monologue. Keep it structured and invite the interviewer to go deeper on any part.

Underselling results. TPMs often downplay outcomes because the work is collaborative. Practice stating results in concrete terms: what shipped, what was avoided, what changed for the customer or the business. If you do not have a number, describe the impact in qualitative terms that are still specific.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Guesty TPM interview typically have?

Candidates report a process that typically includes a recruiter screen, a hiring manager conversation, and one or more panel rounds. The number of panel rounds can vary by team and seniority level. Prepare for at least three to four conversations in total, with the later rounds involving cross-functional interviewers from engineering or product.

Does Guesty ask technical questions in the TPM interview?

Guesty TPM interviews are typically not coding-heavy, but candidates report questions that test your ability to reason about technical systems. Expect questions about API integrations, release dependencies, and how you communicate technical risk to non-technical stakeholders. You do not need to write code, but you should be comfortable discussing system design trade-offs at a high level.

What domain knowledge do I need about the hospitality industry?

You do not need deep hospitality industry experience to interview for a TPM role at Guesty. What interviewers look for is enough product curiosity to understand what Guesty does and why its integrations are complex. Spend time understanding how property management software connects to booking channels like Airbnb, how real-time inventory works, and what property managers care about day to day.

How should I answer questions about managing programs without direct authority?

Frame your answers around specific influence tactics you have used: building alignment through shared goals, making trade-offs visible to stakeholders, and creating transparency that makes it hard for teams to stay unaligned. Avoid answers that sound like you relied on charm or seniority alone. Interviewers want to see a repeatable approach, not a one-time win.

Is there a take-home assignment or case study in the Guesty TPM process?

Some candidates report a case study or take-home exercise, though this is not confirmed as a universal step. The exercise, when present, typically asks you to design a program plan or communication structure for a realistic scenario. Treat it as an opportunity to show your written communication style, your risk thinking, and your ability to structure a plan without being told the right answer.

How competitive is the Guesty TPM role compared to other TPM openings in India?

As of July 2026, knok's data showed 313 TPM openings across India, with 41 in Bangalore alone. Guesty had 19 open roles total across all functions at the same point in time. TPM roles at product-led SaaS companies like Guesty tend to attract experienced candidates, so your story bank and product preparation matter more than they would for a generalist program role.
