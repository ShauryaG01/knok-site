01 Overview

Overview

Simplotel is a Bangalore-based hotel technology company that builds SaaS products for hotels, including booking engines, website management tools, and channel managers. Their core mission is helping hotels compete with OTAs (online travel agencies) by growing direct bookings and owning the guest relationship. As of July 2026, knok jobradar shows 8 open roles at Simplotel.

The PM role here sits at the intersection of hospitality domain knowledge and classic SaaS product thinking. You will own features used daily by hotel front-desk staff, revenue managers, and property owners. Interviewers care as much about empathy for these users as they do about metrics and prioritisation frameworks.

Candidates report a process that typically runs 3-4 rounds covering product sense, a metrics or analytical discussion, and at least one behavioural conversation. Some candidates also mention a take-home case study. The process is fairly conversational but expect sharp follow-up questions on your reasoning.

Salary bands for PM roles in India are publicly reported at 24-40 LPA for mid-level positions (3-6 years of experience) and 40-60 LPA for senior roles. Simplotel's specific offers vary by level and negotiation.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Simplotel interviewers tend to probe three areas: product sense for the hotel industry, data and metrics thinking, and how you handle real-world stakeholder complexity. Here are questions candidates commonly report:

  1. Walk us through a product you shipped from idea to launch. What tradeoffs did you make along the way?
  2. Simplotel serves hotels of different sizes, from large chains to small boutique properties. How would you prioritise features differently for each segment?
  3. How would you improve Simplotel's booking engine to help hotels increase direct bookings over OTA bookings?
  4. A hotel client reports a sharp drop in online reservations after your team's most recent release. How do you investigate and respond?
  5. How do you decide which metrics matter most for a channel manager product? Walk us through your thinking.
  6. Describe a time you had to say no to a customer request that seemed reasonable but conflicted with your roadmap priorities.
  7. How would you approach reducing churn among Simplotel's existing hotel clients?
  8. Design a mobile check-in feature for hotels. Who are the users, what is the core problem, and what does the MVP look like?
  9. OTAs like Booking.com and MakeMyTrip already have deep hotel relationships. How does Simplotel differentiate, and how does that shape your product decisions?
  10. How do you gather requirements from hotel front-desk staff versus the hotel general manager? How do you reconcile conflicting needs?
  11. Describe a situation where you had to push back on an engineering timeline. How did you handle it, and what was the outcome?
  12. If Simplotel wanted to expand into a new hospitality segment such as hostels or serviced apartments, how would you evaluate that opportunity?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through a product you shipped end to end.

*Situation:* At my previous company, we built a B2B SaaS platform for restaurant chains. Restaurant managers complained that monthly sales reports arrived too late to act on and were difficult to read.

*Task:* I was responsible for scoping and delivering an improved reporting module within one quarter, without pulling engineering away from higher-priority roadmap work.

*Action:* I ran five user interviews with restaurant managers to understand what decisions they actually made using these reports. I found they needed three numbers daily, not fifty numbers monthly. I worked with design on a simplified dashboard and negotiated with engineering to ship a lightweight version in six weeks by deferring custom date ranges to a phase two. I also set up a direct feedback channel with our top five clients during the beta so we could iterate quickly.

*Result:* The dashboard launched on time. Support tickets about 'missing data' dropped in the following month, and one client renewed early citing this feature specifically.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you said no to a customer request.

*Situation:* A large hotel chain using our channel manager asked for a custom export format that only their team needed. It would have taken two sprints to build.

*Task:* I needed to decline without damaging the relationship, since this was one of our highest-value accounts.

*Action:* I scheduled a call with their revenue manager to understand the underlying need rather than the surface request. It turned out they needed a specific column order that our CSV export did not support. I checked with engineering: a configurable column selector would take three days and would also benefit other clients. I got internal approval for the generalised version and came back to the client with a solution that worked for everyone.

*Result:* The client got what they needed. The feature launched in two weeks instead of six, and three other clients used the column selector within the first month after launch.

---

Q: Describe a time you used data to change a product decision.

*Situation:* Our team assumed that hotel website visitors who saw the booking widget on the homepage would convert at a reasonable rate. Our designer wanted to move the widget above the fold to improve visibility.

*Task:* Before committing engineering time to the redesign, I wanted to confirm whether widget placement was actually the conversion bottleneck.

*Action:* I pulled session recordings and funnel data. The analysis showed that most drop-offs happened after users clicked the widget and reached the rate calendar, not before. I ran a short exit survey and found that mobile users found the calendar confusing. I redirected the sprint to fix the mobile calendar UX rather than moving the widget.

*Result:* Mobile booking completion improved noticeably after the calendar fix, according to our funnel data. The homepage widget change was deprioritised, saving two weeks of design and development effort.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Having a few structured frameworks ready helps you think clearly under interview pressure. You do not need to announce the framework name. Just use the logic naturally.

RICE for prioritisation. When asked 'how would you prioritise X versus Y,' walk through Reach (how many hotel clients benefit), Impact (how much does it move your key metric), Confidence (how sure are you of the estimate), and Effort (engineering time required). This is far more credible than saying 'I would prioritise whatever has the most business value.'

STAR for behavioural questions. Every 'tell me about a time' question deserves a clear Situation, Task, Action, and Result. Keep Situation and Task short (two to three sentences each). Spend most of your time on Action and always close with a concrete Result, even if the number is approximate.

Jobs-to-be-Done for product sense. When asked to improve a Simplotel product, start by asking: what job is the hotel actually trying to get done? A hotel using a booking engine is not just 'taking reservations.' They are trying to reduce OTA commission costs and own the guest relationship. Framing your answer around that job immediately sets you apart from candidates who jump straight to features.

Structured teardown for improvement questions. A simple sequence: identify the user segments, pick one to focus on, map their journey, find the biggest pain point, propose one solution, and state how you would measure success. Taking sixty seconds to lay out this structure before diving in signals that you think systematically rather than reactively.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Simplotel interviewers are typically looking for a few specific signals.

Domain curiosity about hospitality. You do not need prior hotel experience, but you should understand the business model: room nights, RevPAR, OTA commission pressure, and seasonality. Candidates who can speak the language of hotel revenue managers consistently stand out.

Metrics instinct. For any product decision, be ready to name the metric you would move and explain how you would measure it. 'I would track user satisfaction' is too vague. 'I would track booking conversion rate on the widget and watch for changes in average booking lead time' is the level of specificity they want.

Customer empathy across multiple stakeholders. Hotel software has layered users: the owner cares about revenue, the front-desk staff cares about ease of use, and the IT team cares about reliability. Showing that you can hold all three perspectives at once is a strong signal of product maturity.

Decisiveness with humility. Interviewers want PMs who can make a call with incomplete information but who also know when to say 'I need to validate this assumption first.' Avoid being vague, but do not pretend to certainty you do not have.

Communication clarity. Because Simplotel works closely with hotel clients, PMs sometimes join client conversations. Interviewers notice whether you can explain a complex product decision in plain language without jargon.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Spread your preparation over one to two weeks using this structure.

Days 1-2: Know the product. Sign up for a demo or watch any publicly available walkthroughs of Simplotel's booking engine and channel manager. Write down three things you would improve and why. Be ready to defend your reasoning with specifics.

Days 3-4: Learn the hospitality basics. Look up how OTA commissions work, what RevPAR means, and why direct bookings matter so much to hotels. A few hours of reading will let you speak naturally about the domain without sounding like you memorised a glossary.

Days 5-6: Prepare your stories. Write out five to seven STAR stories from your own experience. Cover: a product you shipped, a customer conflict you resolved, a data-driven decision you made, a time you prioritised ruthlessly, and a time something did not go as planned. Practise each story out loud in under three minutes.

Days 7-8: Mock product sense questions. Pick two questions from the list above and practice your full structured answer, timed. Ask a peer to give feedback on whether your reasoning is clear and your structure holds.

Day 9: Review your resume. Every bullet point is fair game. For each one, prepare the STAR story behind it so you are never caught off guard.

Day 10: Prepare your questions for them. Good questions to ask: how does the PM team decide what goes on the roadmap, how closely do PMs work with hotel clients, and what does success look like in the first six months.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Giving generic PM answers without hospitality context. Saying 'I would do user research and prioritise the highest-impact features' could work in any PM interview. At Simplotel, say 'I would talk to front-desk staff and revenue managers separately because their pain points are very different.' Specificity wins every time.

Not researching Simplotel's product before the interview. Candidates who arrive without having explored the booking engine or website builder are easy to spot. Spend at least an hour with the product and come in with genuine observations.

Jumping to solutions before understanding the problem. When asked to improve a feature, many candidates immediately list ideas. Pause first: who is the user, what is their biggest frustration, and why has it not been solved already? Interviewers notice this discipline and reward it.

Treating all hotel clients as the same. A five-star business hotel has very different needs from a budget guesthouse. Candidates who segment customers thoughtfully demonstrate real product maturity.

Weak or missing metrics in answers. Every solution you propose should come with a success metric. If you cannot name one, it signals you might ship features without knowing whether they worked.

Rambling in behavioural answers. Keep your STAR stories tight. If your Situation setup takes more than ninety seconds, you are losing the interviewer. Practise out loud before the interview, not just in your head.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Simplotel PM interview typically have?

Candidates report a process that typically runs 3-4 rounds. These commonly include a product sense discussion, a metrics or analytical conversation, and a behavioural round. Some candidates also mention a take-home case study before or after the live rounds. Confirm the exact structure with your recruiter since it can vary by role and team.

Do I need prior experience in hotel or hospitality tech to apply?

You do not need to have worked in hospitality, but you should arrive understanding the basics of the hotel business model. Concepts like OTA commissions, direct booking economics, and RevPAR are easy to learn in a day or two and make a real difference to how you come across. Candidates who show genuine curiosity about the domain tend to do better than those who rely only on their general PM credentials.

What salary can I expect for a PM role at Simplotel?

Simplotel-specific salary data is not widely published, so treat broader market data as a reference point only. Industry surveys and Glassdoor data show mid-level PM roles (3-6 years) in India at 24-40 LPA and senior PM roles at 40-60 LPA. Your actual offer will depend on your level, the specific role, and your negotiation. Levels.fyi and Glassdoor sometimes have more granular company data worth checking before your final round.

What kind of case study does Simplotel give in interviews?

Candidates report case studies that are typically framed around Simplotel's actual products, such as improving booking engine conversion or designing a feature for a new hotel segment. The focus is on structured thinking: who is the user, what is the problem, what would you build, and how would you measure success. Practising the frameworks listed in this guide will help you structure a confident answer under time pressure.

How important is technical knowledge for a PM role at Simplotel?

Simplotel builds SaaS products, so a basic understanding of APIs, integrations, and web performance is useful context to have. You are not expected to write code, but being able to have an informed conversation with engineers about technical constraints helps in both the interview and the day-to-day role. Candidates report that interviewers do not ask coding questions but do appreciate PMs who understand how systems connect.

How should I research Simplotel before the interview?

Start by signing up for a demo or watching any publicly available product walkthroughs of their booking engine and channel manager. Read their website carefully and look at what hotel clients say in publicly available reviews. Understanding their core products and being able to name one concrete improvement will immediately signal that you are a serious candidate. Also search for any recent news about the company to understand where they are investing and growing.
