01 Overview

Overview

Lendingkart is a Bangalore-based fintech focused on unsecured working-capital loans for small and medium businesses across India. Product designers there typically work on borrower-facing flows such as loan application, KYC, and repayment, as well as internal tools for credit and operations teams, and growth surfaces like referral and re-engagement screens.

The process candidates report typically runs 3-4 stages: a recruiter screen, a portfolio walkthrough with the hiring manager, a design challenge (take-home or live exercise), and a final discussion with a senior stakeholder or cross-functional panel. Round names are not always standard, so confirm the structure with your recruiter early.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly in Lendingkart product design interviews, based on publicly shared candidate experiences and the company's core focus on SME lending.

  1. Walk us through a project where you made a complicated financial product easy for users who are not very tech-savvy.
  2. How would you design the end-to-end loan application flow for a first-time small business owner applying on a mobile app?
  3. Lendingkart serves borrowers in Tier 2 and Tier 3 cities. How does that shape your decisions around language, literacy, and connectivity?
  4. Describe a time you worked with a compliance or legal team to include regulatory requirements in a user flow without hurting the experience.
  5. How do you balance conversion rate goals (a business priority) with building user trust in a lending product?
  6. Your target users, such as small shop owners and traders, are hard to recruit for research. How do you still get reliable user insights?
  7. How would you design a loan rejection screen that keeps the user's trust and encourages them to reapply later?
  8. Tell us about a time you made a significant design decision with little or no data. What did you do?
  9. Walk us through how you would redesign Lendingkart's borrower repayment dashboard. What would you change and why?
  10. Describe a time when product goals and design goals were in conflict. How did you handle it?
  11. How do you approach designing for users who have low digital literacy or who mix English with their regional language mid-session?
  12. Tell us about a project where you worked closely with engineers under a tight deadline. What tradeoffs did you make?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format for all behavioral questions. Three worked examples are below.

Q: Walk us through a project where you simplified a complex financial product for non-tech users.

*Situation:* I was at a fintech startup where we offered a credit-linked insurance product bundled with personal loans. Drop-off in the mid-funnel was high because borrowers could not understand what the insurance covered or why it cost extra.

*Task:* I was asked to redesign the product explanation screen and the consent flow to reduce drop-off without removing the insurance upsell.

*Action:* I ran in-person interviews with users from Tier 2 towns, conducted in Hindi. I found that users trusted visuals over text and could not parse legal copy. I replaced the text wall with a three-panel illustrated explainer in plain Hindi, added a 'What does this cost me per month?' calculator, and moved legal disclosures to an expandable section rather than the main view. I then tested two prototypes with users via remote moderated sessions.

*Result:* The redesigned flow reduced mid-funnel drop-off by a meaningful margin, which the PM confirmed post-launch. The business also reported improved uptake of the insurance add-on.

Q: Describe a time when business goals and user experience goals conflicted.

*Situation:* At my previous company, the growth team wanted to add a mandatory referral step inside the loan disbursal confirmation screen, arguing it would drive new user acquisition.

*Task:* My role was to design the screen, but I believed forcing referral at that moment would feel exploitative and could harm user trust right after they had taken a loan.

*Action:* I presented two options: a mandatory referral gate (what the team wanted) and a soft, dismissible referral nudge placed after the disbursal confirmation. I backed the second option with a short usability test showing users felt pressured by the mandatory version. I also referenced publicly reported patterns in fintech apps where forced referral steps led to negative reviews.

*Result:* The team agreed to the soft nudge. The referral rate was lower than the growth team's initial hope, but the satisfaction score for the disbursal flow stayed healthy. We later ran a timed post-disbursal notification for referral that outperformed the in-flow gate.

Q: Tell us about a time you made a design decision with limited data.

*Situation:* I was designing an onboarding flow for a new borrower segment (first-time users from manufacturing micro-enterprises) with no existing research on that group.

*Task:* I needed to ship an onboarding redesign in three weeks with no budget or time for formal user research.

*Action:* I spoke with customer support agents who handled inbound calls from this segment daily. They gave me a clear picture of the top confusion points. I also reviewed call transcripts (with permission) to identify recurring questions. Based on that, I made three targeted changes: simplified the document upload screen, added a progress indicator so users knew how many steps remained, and placed a 'call us' button at the highest drop-off point.

*Result:* We shipped on time. Post-launch, support tickets related to document upload dropped noticeably, which the support team confirmed. I flagged this was proxy data and recommended a proper research round once we had budget, which the team approved the following quarter.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For behavioral questions: STAR is the standard. Keep Situation and Task brief. Spend most of your time on Action (what you specifically did, not what the team did) and Result (a metric if possible, or a clear qualitative outcome if numbers are unavailable).

For design challenge questions: Start by clarifying who the user is, what they need, and what the business context is. Define success metrics before jumping to solutions. Sketch multiple directions briefly before committing to one. End with what you would test and how.

For product critique questions (for example, 'how would you improve our repayment dashboard'): Ground your observations in user needs, not personal preference. Frame issues as 'users may struggle with X because Y.' Prioritise one or two changes rather than a long wish list. Show awareness of technical and business constraints.

For constraint-heavy fintech questions: Acknowledge the regulatory environment upfront. Show you understand that compliance, KYC norms, and RBI guidelines are non-negotiable in lending. Propose solutions that work within those constraints and flag where you would involve legal or compliance stakeholders.

For vague or open-ended prompts: Ask a clarifying question before diving in. Interviewers at fintech companies often leave prompts open deliberately to see if you define scope or just guess. Narrowing the problem first shows product maturity.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Empathy for underserved users. Lendingkart's core borrower is a small business owner in a non-metro city, often with limited digital fluency. Interviewers want to see you have genuinely thought about this user, not a generic urban smartphone user.

Comfort with regulated environments. Lending products operate under RBI and data-privacy rules. Candidates who treat compliance as an afterthought stand out negatively. Show that you know how to design within constraints without making the experience feel like a legal document.

Portfolio with outcomes. Case studies should show what changed as a result of your work. 'I redesigned the screen' is weak. 'The redesign reduced support calls related to document upload' is strong, even if the metric is directional rather than exact.

Cross-functional collaboration skills. Product designers at Lendingkart work closely with product managers, engineers, data analysts, and credit or risk teams. Stories that show you communicated trade-offs clearly across those functions are valued.

Ownership and initiative. Lendingkart expects designers who identify problems independently, propose solutions, and follow through without heavy handholding. Stories where you spotted a UX problem no one had formally flagged and then drove a fix land especially well.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

One week before the interview:

Download the Lendingkart app and go through the loan application flow as a first-time borrower would. Note every screen where you feel confused, uncertain, or where the language is unclear. This gives you real, grounded material for 'how would you improve our product' questions.

Read one or two publicly available reports on SME lending in India (from sources like RBI or industry bodies) to understand the borrower's financial context. You do not need deep credit knowledge; knowing the user's world is enough.

Portfolio preparation:

Select three or four case studies that together show: a complex problem you made simpler, a data-informed design decision, and one instance of cross-functional collaboration. Lead with fintech or regulated-industry work if you have it. If not, draw explicit parallels between your past work and the fintech context.

Prepare a one-sentence outcome for each case study: the metric or result that came directly from your work.

Practice:

Do at least one timed live design exercise. Pick a fintech prompt such as 'design a loan status tracker for a small business owner' and work through it out loud, the way you would in a live interview. Practice your STAR stories for the top behavioral questions above, aiming for a crisp two-minute answer per question.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Designing for the wrong user. Many candidates default to a young, urban, English-fluent smartphone user. Lendingkart's actual borrowers are often older, more comfortable in Hindi or a regional language, and using a mid-range Android phone with patchy connectivity. Not acknowledging this gap signals a mismatch with the role.

Ignoring compliance in design proposals. Candidates sometimes suggest removing KYC steps or simplifying disclosures without checking whether those elements are legally required. Proposing a change that would violate RBI norms shows you have not thought through the operating context.

Weak metrics in the portfolio. Showing wireframes and final screens without explaining what changed in user behavior, support tickets, or conversion is a common miss. Even rough directional data is better than no outcome at all.

Talking only about visuals. Lendingkart interviews look for product thinking, not just visual polish. Candidates who focus only on colors, typography, and layout without addressing the user problem and business goal are consistently rated lower.

Not researching the actual product. Candidates who have clearly never opened the Lendingkart app make a poor impression. Specific, grounded observations (for example, 'I noticed the document upload screen has no progress indicator') are far more compelling than generic feedback.

Overcomplicating the design challenge. In live exercises, candidates often try to design a complete product end to end. Interviewers typically prefer a well-reasoned, focused solution for one user flow over a half-finished end-to-end system.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many interview rounds does Lendingkart typically have for Product Designer roles?

Candidates report that the process typically runs 3-4 rounds: a recruiter screen, a portfolio presentation with the hiring manager, a design challenge (take-home or live), and a final discussion with a senior stakeholder or cross-functional panel. The exact number varies by level and team, so confirm the structure with your recruiter after the first call.

Is there a take-home design assignment?

Many candidates report receiving a take-home design task or a live design exercise as part of the process. It typically involves designing a feature or flow relevant to lending, such as a loan application screen or a borrower dashboard. Show your thinking process clearly, not just the final screens. Submitting polished mockups without explaining your decisions is a common reason for rejection.

Do I need previous fintech experience to get hired?

Fintech experience is a plus but is not always required, particularly for mid-level roles. What matters more is showing you have thought through the challenges of designing for underserved users, regulated environments, and low-digital-literacy audiences. Candidates from e-commerce, edtech, or healthtech who can draw clear parallels to the fintech context are regularly hired.

What design tools does Lendingkart expect candidates to know?

Figma is the industry standard for product design in Indian tech companies, and most Lendingkart roles expect proficiency in it. Familiarity with prototyping within Figma and clean handoff to engineers are both useful. The interview itself is unlikely to test tool knowledge directly, but your portfolio should be presented clearly, ideally via a Figma file or a well-structured PDF.

What salary can I expect as a Product Designer at Lendingkart?

Based on market data for Product Designers in India (2026), typical ranges are: 6-12 LPA for entry-level (0-2 years), 14-24 LPA for mid-level (3-5 years), 26-40 LPA for senior (6-9 years), and 36-55+ LPA for lead or principal roles. Actual offers depend on the specific role, your portfolio strength, competing offers, and negotiation. Always benchmark against Glassdoor and levels.fyi for the most current data points.

How long does the Lendingkart hiring process take from first contact to offer?

Candidates report that the timeline typically spans a few weeks from the first recruiter call to an offer, though this varies based on how quickly rounds are scheduled and whether a take-home assignment is included. Following up politely after each stage is normal practice. If you have competing offers, let the recruiter know early so they can try to move things faster.
