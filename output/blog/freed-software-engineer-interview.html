01 Overview

Overview

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a time you worked on a feature under tight deadline pressure. How did you decide what to cut?

*Situation:* At my previous company, we had a client demo coming up in a few days and the reporting dashboard feature was only half-built.

*Task:* I needed to ship something that showed real value without cutting corners that would cause problems later.

*Action:* I listed every remaining task, split them into 'must-have for demo' and 'nice-to-have,' and dropped all the nice-to-haves. I flagged to my manager upfront exactly what would not be in the build so there were no surprises. I focused on the data display and export flow since that was what the client cared about most.

*Result:* The demo went well, we closed the deal, and the remaining features shipped in the next sprint. My manager later said that flagging scope clearly was as valuable as the code itself.

---

Q: Describe your experience working with LLMs or NLP models in a production environment.

*Situation:* At a previous role, we integrated an LLM to auto-tag customer support tickets.

*Task:* My job was to get the model running reliably in production, not just in a notebook.

*Action:* I wrapped the model calls in a retry and fallback layer, set up monitoring for latency and error rates, and wrote prompt versioning so we could A/B test prompts without a full deployment. I also added a human-in-the-loop review step for low-confidence outputs.

*Result:* The system handled production ticket volumes without downtime. Tagging accuracy improved steadily over several months as we iterated on prompts, freeing up the support team for complex cases.

---

Q: How have you built features with privacy and security in mind?

*Situation:* I worked on a feature that stored user health questionnaire responses.

*Task:* We needed to ensure no plain-text sensitive data ever hit our logs or was accessible beyond the owning user.

*Action:* I implemented field-level encryption for sensitive columns, scrubbed PII from all log outputs, added role-based access controls, and ran the schema through a security review before launch. I also documented which fields were sensitive so future engineers would know.

*Result:* The feature passed an internal security audit on the first review. No data incidents were reported after launch, and the pattern became a template that two other teams adopted.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For coding rounds: Think out loud from the start. freed engineers care about how you reason, not just whether you reach the right answer. Clarify inputs and edge cases before writing any code. When you hit a wall, say what you are considering next rather than going silent.

For system design: Start with requirements, then move to high-level components, then dig into the parts the interviewer seems most interested in. For freed specifically, design questions often involve real-time audio or text processing, so be ready to discuss queues, streaming pipelines, and latency trade-offs.

For behavioural rounds: Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) but keep your Situation brief. Interviewers want to hear the Action most. Lead with what you did, not what 'we' did as a team.

For culture questions: freed is mission-driven around helping doctors. Candidates who connect their answers to genuine care for end users tend to leave stronger impressions than those who focus only on technical craft.

General tip: After each answer, pause and ask if the interviewer wants you to go deeper on any part. This shows self-awareness and gives them control of the conversation.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on publicly reported freed interview feedback, interviewers consistently look for a few things:

Mission alignment. freed builds tools for doctors. Engineers who show they understand the stakes of healthcare software, such as patient data sensitivity, reliability, and user trust, stand out. You do not need a medical background, but you do need to care about the user.

Speed paired with judgement. freed ships fast. They want engineers who move quickly but know when to slow down for a security review or a design conversation. Expect questions that probe how you make trade-off decisions under pressure.

Hands-on AI familiarity. The core product is AI-driven. Even if you are not an ML engineer, showing you can integrate models, handle non-deterministic outputs, and monitor AI systems in production is a clear advantage.

Clear communication. freed is a small team. Direct communication matters as much as technical skill. Interviewers notice candidates who explain their thinking well and ask good clarifying questions.

Ownership mentality. Candidates report that interviewers respond well to stories where you noticed a problem, took initiative to fix it, and followed through without being asked.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: product research and foundations

Read everything publicly available about freed, their product, and any engineering content they have shared. Install and use the freed app if a trial is available. This gives you concrete things to reference in interviews. Revise core data structures, algorithms, and complexity analysis in parallel.

Week 2: system design focus

Practise designing real-time audio and text processing systems. Think through message queues, streaming pipelines, storage for large audio files, and latency reduction. Sketch designs on paper and practise talking through them clearly within a set time limit.

Week 3: behavioural and AI-specific prep

Write out several STAR stories from your past work covering: handling pressure, shipping with quality, debugging hard problems, working through conflict, and building with AI or NLP. Practise saying each story out loud, not just writing it down.

Week 4: mock interviews and review

Do at least a couple of full mock interviews covering coding and system design with a peer or via a practice platform. Review weak spots. Prepare thoughtful questions to ask the interviewer about freed's engineering culture, their biggest technical challenges, and how they measure success for the role.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping the 'why freed' question. Many candidates prepare technically but have no clear answer for why they want to work at a health-tech AI startup. Interviewers notice a generic answer immediately. Prepare something specific to freed's product and mission.

Treating healthcare as just another domain. freed's product touches patient data and affects doctors' daily workflows. Treating it like a generic SaaS product in your answers signals shallow thinking. Mention reliability, privacy, and user trust naturally in your responses.

Not discussing trade-offs in system design. Candidates who jump straight to a solution without exploring alternatives leave interviewers uncertain about their depth. Always name at least one trade-off in your design before committing to an approach.

Talking in 'we' throughout behavioural answers. Interviewers are assessing you, not your team. Use 'I' when describing your specific contribution, and acknowledge the team only where credit is genuinely due.

Going silent during coding rounds. freed interviews are collaborative. Silence for several minutes makes interviewers uncomfortable and misses the chance to show your reasoning process. Think aloud even when you are unsure of the next step.

Underestimating the recruiter screen. Candidates report that the initial recruiter call often surfaces useful information about round structure and current team priorities. Treat it as an intelligence-gathering session, not just a formality.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the freed Software Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically involves several rounds, usually covering a recruiter screen, a technical coding assessment, a system design discussion, and a culture or values interview. The exact number and order can vary by role and team. Always confirm the structure with your recruiter at the start of the process so you can prepare the right way.

Does freed ask LeetCode-style coding questions?

Based on publicly reported experiences, freed's coding rounds tend to involve practical problem-solving rather than purely algorithmic puzzles. Expect questions related to real-world tasks such as data transformation, API design, or text parsing. That said, being solid on core data structures and algorithm basics remains useful preparation.

What salary can I expect for a Software Engineer role at freed?

freed is a US-based company and compensation may be structured differently depending on whether the role is India-based or remote for a US entity. For context, knok jobradar data shows Indian Software Engineer salaries broadly ranging from 6-12 LPA at entry level to 28-45 LPA at senior level. Confirm the specific offer structure and range directly with freed's recruiter.

Do I need a healthcare or medical background to interview at freed?

No medical background is required. freed hires generalist Software Engineers who are curious about the healthcare domain. What matters more is showing you understand the importance of reliability, data privacy, and building tools for a high-stakes user group like doctors. Demonstrating that you have thought about healthcare software constraints will set you apart from candidates who have not.

How important is AI or ML experience for a freed Software Engineer role?

The core product is AI-powered, so familiarity with working alongside ML models is a real advantage. You do not need to be an ML researcher, but understanding how to integrate LLM or NLP APIs, handle non-deterministic outputs, and monitor AI systems in production strengthens your profile considerably. Candidates without direct ML experience can compensate by showing strong system design skills and genuine curiosity about AI.

How should I prepare if I have limited time before the interview?

Prioritise in this order: use or research the freed product so you can speak to it specifically, prepare a few strong STAR behavioural stories from your past work, and practise one system design question involving real-time text or audio processing. Even focused product research done the evening before will differentiate you from candidates who treat freed as just another coding interview.
