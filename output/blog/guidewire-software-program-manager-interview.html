01 Overview

Overview

Guidewire Software (Nasdaq: GWRE) builds core technology for property and casualty (P&C) insurance carriers worldwide. Their flagship products, PolicyCenter, BillingCenter, and ClaimCenter, run the operations of major insurers globally, and the company has been steadily moving customers to Guidewire Cloud in recent years.

A Technical Program Manager at Guidewire typically sits at the intersection of engineering, product, and client delivery. You will coordinate across multiple teams, manage program-level risks, and keep complex, multi-year implementation programs on track. The role demands both technical credibility and strong stakeholder communication, because your internal teams and your external clients (large insurance carriers) both need confidence in the plan.

As of July 2026, knok jobradar tracks 2 open TPM roles at Guidewire. Nationally, there are 313 TPM openings across all companies, with Bangalore leading at 41 roles, followed by Delhi (14 roles) and Pune (13 roles).

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Three sample answers using STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result). These are illustrative; adapt them with your own experience.

---

Q: A critical dependency slips by three weeks. Walk me through how you respond.

*Situation:* At my previous company, a third-party data migration vendor missed a key milestone, putting our product launch timeline at risk. The delay had a direct knock-on effect on three downstream engineering teams.

*Task:* My job was to contain the impact, keep stakeholders informed, and find a path to recover some of the lost time without burning out the teams.

*Action:* I called an urgent sync with the vendor and our internal leads to understand the root cause. I mapped every downstream dependency in the program tracker and identified two workstreams that could be resequenced while the blocker was being resolved. I updated the program risk log, drafted a concise executive summary with three mitigation options and a clear recommendation, and presented it to leadership within the same day.

*Result:* We recovered two of the three weeks through resequencing and delivered the launch just one week past the original date. The client appreciated the transparency and the structured escalation.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you had to push back on an unrealistic deadline.

*Situation:* A senior VP wanted a major platform feature shipped in six weeks. Based on the engineering team's estimates and the existing backlog, the realistic timeline was closer to three months.

*Task:* I needed to push back credibly without damaging the relationship, and give the VP options rather than just a 'no.'

*Action:* I prepared a scope breakdown showing the full feature set, team capacity, and known risks. I proposed two options: ship a reduced scope in six weeks hitting the must-have requirements, or ship the full feature in three months. I framed each option around the business outcomes the VP cared about.

*Result:* The VP chose the phased approach. The reduced scope shipped on time, and the second phase followed two months later. The VP later told my manager it was one of the clearest trade-off conversations she had seen.

---

Q: How do you keep engineering and business stakeholders aligned when priorities conflict?

*Situation:* During a cloud platform program, the product team kept adding new requirements mid-sprint while the engineering lead was focused on stability and paying down technical debt.

*Task:* I needed to create a shared decision-making process so that conflicts were resolved in a structured way rather than through escalation every time.

*Action:* I set up a weekly prioritization meeting with both leads and the program sponsor. I introduced a simple scoring method: each new request was assessed on business value, engineering effort, and risk. Items above a set threshold went to the sponsor for a final call. I made the process visible to both sides so neither felt overruled arbitrarily.

*Result:* Escalations to the sponsor dropped noticeably over the following months, and both teams reported higher satisfaction with how conflicts were handled in the retrospectives.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for program management stories. Every behavioral question at Guidewire can be answered with Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep the Situation brief (two to three sentences). Spend most of your time on the Action section, since that is where interviewers assess your thinking and ownership. Close with a concrete Result, even if it is qualitative. A focused, well-paced answer leaves a stronger impression than an exhaustive one.

For 'how would you approach X' questions. Guidewire interviewers often present hypothetical program scenarios. A strong structure: (1) clarify the scope and constraints, (2) identify the key stakeholders and dependencies, (3) lay out your risk mitigation approach, (4) describe how you would communicate progress. This shows structured thinking without sounding formulaic.

Technical credibility without overreach. TPMs at Guidewire are not expected to write code, but you should understand system architecture well enough to ask the right questions. When discussing technical trade-offs, frame your role as 'I work with the engineers to surface the right options for leadership' rather than claiming deep technical ownership.

Handling domain gaps. If asked about insurance, be honest about your experience level and immediately follow up with how you would learn: reading carrier documentation, shadowing solution architects, and spending time with the client's operations team. Interviewers value intellectual curiosity and a structured learning plan over false confidence.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Guidewire TPM interviewers are typically looking for five things.

Program ownership. Can you take a complex, multi-year program and drive it to completion without needing constant direction? Come with examples where you identified a problem before it was escalated to you.

Stakeholder management. Guidewire's clients are large insurance companies with demanding delivery expectations. Interviewers want evidence that you can manage client relationships, set realistic expectations, and handle difficult conversations professionally.

Technical credibility. You do not need to be an engineer, but you must be comfortable in a room full of architects. Show that you understand software delivery lifecycles, cloud migrations, and integration complexity at a level that earns engineering team trust.

Structured risk management. Program risks at Guidewire can span vendor delays, regulatory constraints, and client change management. Interviewers want to see a repeatable process, not ad-hoc firefighting. Be ready to describe your risk tracking and escalation approach in concrete terms.

Domain learning speed. P&C insurance is a specialized domain. Candidates who show genuine curiosity about the business and a clear plan to ramp up on domain knowledge stand out from those who treat the TPM role as purely domain-agnostic.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Understand Guidewire's business. Read up on Guidewire's three core products (PolicyCenter, BillingCenter, ClaimCenter) and their cloud platform. Understand what a core system replacement means for an insurance carrier: it is a multi-year, high-stakes program touching every part of the business. This context will help you frame your answers in language that resonates with the interviewers.

Week 2: Build your story bank. Write out five to seven STAR stories covering: a major program you owned end to end, a time you managed a client escalation, a time you pushed back on scope or timeline, a time you recovered a program that was off track, and a time you worked across multiple engineering teams. Make sure each story has a clear, concrete result.

Week 3: Practice out loud. Candidates typically report multiple rounds including a recruiter screen, program management depth interviews, and a final leadership round. Practice each story out loud and get feedback from a friend or colleague on clarity and structure. Concise answers with a clear arc leave a stronger impression than exhaustive ones.

Day before: Prepare questions for them. Ask about the specific program or product area the TPM will support, the team structure, and how success is measured in the first year. Thoughtful questions signal genuine interest and preparation.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Being too vague about ownership. Saying 'I was part of a team that delivered X' is weak. Interviewers want to know what you specifically owned, decided, and drove. Use 'I' not 'we' when describing your actions in a STAR story.

Not researching the insurance domain. Showing up without any knowledge of P&C insurance, core system implementations, or what Guidewire's products actually do signals poor preparation. Even one hour of reading makes a visible difference.

Skipping the result. Many candidates give strong Situation and Action sections but trail off before the Result. Always close with a concrete outcome. If the result was mixed, own it and say what you learned.

Overcomplicating the answer. Long, meandering answers with too many sub-stories lose the interviewer. Aim for a clear arc: the problem, what you did, what happened.

Not asking questions. Ending with 'I think I covered everything' when offered time to ask questions reads as disinterest. Prepare at least three thoughtful questions about the role, the team, and the program you will be managing.

Underselling technical depth. Some TPM candidates over-index on process and forget to show that they understand the technical work. Reference specific technologies, architectural decisions, or integration challenges you have personally navigated.

How many interview rounds does Guidewire typically have for a TPM role?

Candidates report that Guidewire's TPM hiring process typically involves three to five rounds. This usually includes an initial recruiter or HR screen, one or two program management depth interviews, and a final round with senior leadership or cross-functional stakeholders. The exact structure can vary by team and region, so it is worth asking the recruiter to outline the process when you first connect.

Do I need prior insurance industry experience to get a TPM role at Guidewire?

Insurance domain experience is a plus but is not always a hard requirement, based on what candidates report. Guidewire hires TPMs from enterprise software, cloud platforms, and consulting backgrounds. What matters more is your ability to manage complex programs and learn a specialized domain quickly. Come prepared with a clear plan for how you would ramp up on P&C insurance if you are new to it, and show genuine curiosity about the business.
