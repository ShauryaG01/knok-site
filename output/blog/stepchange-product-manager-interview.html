02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates at Stepchange typically face questions across product strategy, execution, metrics, and behaviour. The following are the most commonly reported question areas:

  1. Walk me through a product you built or significantly improved. What decisions did you make and why?
  2. How do you prioritise features when engineering bandwidth is limited?

  4. Tell me about a time you had to push back on a stakeholder request. How did you handle it?
  5. How do you define success for a product, and how do you measure it?
  6. Describe a situation where data and gut instinct gave you conflicting signals. What did you decide?
  7. How would you improve a specific Stepchange product or feature?
  8. Walk me through a product launch that did not go as planned. What did you learn?
  9. How do you collaborate with engineering teams to scope and ship features?
  10. Tell me about a time you made a major product decision with limited data.
  11. How do you balance short-term business goals with a longer-term product vision?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for every behavioural question. Here are three examples:

Q: Tell me about a time you prioritised features with limited engineering resources.

*Situation:* Our team had a large backlog of requests from multiple business units, but only limited engineering capacity for the quarter.

*Task:* I needed to build a prioritisation framework that was transparent, defensible, and aligned with company OKRs.

*Action:* I ran a scoring exercise using impact, confidence, and effort. I also held one-on-ones with each business unit lead to understand their underlying needs, not just their surface-level requests. A clear set of priorities emerged from the exercise.

*Result:* The top priorities shipped on time. Business unit leads who did not get their features that quarter felt heard and understood the reasoning. Stakeholder trust improved noticeably.

---

Q: Describe a product launch that did not go as planned.

*Situation:* We launched a new onboarding flow that we expected to reduce drop-off, but activation numbers fell in the first week.

*Task:* I had to diagnose the problem quickly, communicate it to leadership, and propose a fix.

*Action:* I pulled session recordings and heatmaps and found that a new mandatory step was confusing users on mobile. I worked with engineering to ship a hotfix within days, and set up a lightweight test to validate the fix.

*Result:* Activation recovered and then exceeded the original baseline. I also introduced a pre-launch mobile checklist that the team now uses for every release.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you pushed back on a stakeholder.

*Situation:* A senior sales leader requested a custom reporting feature for a single enterprise client, arguing it would secure a large renewal.

*Task:* I had to evaluate whether building this was the right call for the product, not just for one deal.

*Action:* I asked the sales team to help me understand the client's underlying need. It turned out the client wanted faster data exports, not a custom dashboard. I proposed a generalised export improvement that would benefit all clients, made the case to leadership with data on how many users had requested similar things, and got buy-in.

*Result:* The generalised feature shipped, the renewal was secured, and several other clients referenced the improvement in their own renewal conversations.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Having a go-to structure for each question type helps you stay clear under pressure.

For prioritisation questions, use a simple scoring approach: rate each initiative by impact on the metric that matters most, your confidence in that estimate, and the effort required. Walk the interviewer through your reasoning out loud. Stepchange interviewers typically want to see that you can make a defensible call, not just list tradeoffs.

For product improvement questions, lead with the user. Start by identifying who the core user is, what their key goal is, and where the current experience falls short. Then propose a focused improvement or two with a hypothesis about impact. Avoid listing every possible feature.

For metrics questions, name a north-star metric, explain why it captures real user value, then identify a few supporting metrics that indicate health and catch regressions. Be ready to say what you would NOT measure and why.

For 'first few weeks on the job' questions, structure your answer in phases: listen and learn (talk to users, read existing data, understand the current roadmap and OKRs), identify quick wins versus longer-term bets, and set up the feedback loops you would need. This shows planning instinct without overpromising.

For behavioural questions, use STAR without making the Situation too long. Interviewers want to hear your Action and Result. Aim for a brief sentence or two on Situation and Task, then spend the bulk of your answer on what you personally did and what changed as a result.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Stepchange interviewers, based on candidate reports, look for a few consistent qualities.

Clear problem framing. Before jumping to a solution, PMs are expected to articulate the problem precisely. Interviewers often push back on early answers to see if you can reframe and sharpen.

Data fluency without data dependency. You should be comfortable pulling numbers to support a decision, but also able to act when data is thin. Candidates who say 'I would need more data' for every scenario tend to score lower.

Cross-functional ownership. Stepchange PMs work closely with engineering, design, and business teams. Expect questions about how you handle disagreements, earn trust, and keep projects moving without direct authority.

Business grounding. Product decisions should connect to revenue, retention, or cost. Interviewers appreciate candidates who can articulate the business case behind a feature, not just the user benefit.

Self-awareness and learning. When asked about failures or mistakes, interviewers want to hear genuine reflection and a concrete change in behaviour, not a polished story where everything worked out.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused preparation plan for Stepchange PM interviews:

Step 1: Understand the company. Research what Stepchange does, who their customers are, and what their key products look like. Read recent press, LinkedIn posts from their team, and any available product reviews. Form a point of view on where their product could go next.

Step 2: Prepare your product stories. Pick several products or features you have worked on. For each, know the problem you solved, the decision you made, the metrics you tracked, and what you would do differently. These stories become the raw material for most questions.

Step 3: Practice the core frameworks. Be fluent in prioritisation (impact, confidence, effort scoring), user research (jobs-to-be-done), and metrics (north star plus supporting indicators). Practice saying these out loud, not just writing them.

Step 4: Prepare your 'first few weeks' answer. This question comes up often. Have a structured answer ready: how you would learn the product, users, and business context before making recommendations. Keep it grounded in listening before acting.

Step 5: Do a mock interview. Ask a friend or mentor to give you several behavioural questions and provide feedback on whether your answers are crisp and specific. Vague answers are the most common reason candidates do not progress.

Step 6: Prepare smart questions for the panel. Ask about how the PM team works with engineering, how success is measured for PMs, and what a strong first few months looks like in this role. Avoid questions already answered on the company website.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Being too solution-focused too fast. When given a product scenario, many candidates jump straight to ideas. Interviewers at Stepchange typically want to see you define the problem and identify the user before proposing anything.

Giving generic answers. Saying 'I would do user research and look at data' without specifics signals shallow preparation. Ground every answer in something you have actually done or a real product you have studied.

Not knowing your own numbers. If you built a feature, know the metric it moved. If you do not have exact numbers, say so and explain what you tracked and why.

Over-engineering frameworks. Using a complex framework without explaining why you chose it can feel performative. Interviewers care more about clear thinking than about recognising the name of a framework.

Treating every tradeoff as equal. Product management is about making calls. If you present every option as equally valid, interviewers may feel you lack conviction. State your recommendation clearly and defend it.

Not asking clarifying questions. Especially in product design and improvement questions, jumping in without asking about the user, the business goal, or any constraints is a missed signal. Candidates report that clarifying questions are seen as a positive sign at Stepchange.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Stepchange PM interview typically have?

Candidates typically report a multi-stage process that includes an initial screening, product and case rounds, and a final panel or leadership interview. The exact number of rounds varies by level and team. It is best to confirm the structure with your recruiter at the start of the process.

Does Stepchange give take-home assignments?

Some candidates report receiving a written product case as part of the process, typically before or between interview rounds. These assignments usually ask you to analyse a product problem and present a recommendation. Treat it like a real product decision: define the problem, identify the user, propose a solution with clear reasoning, and state the metrics you would track.

What kind of metrics questions should I expect?

Expect questions about how you would define success for a product, which metric you would choose as a north star, and how you would diagnose a drop in a key number. Interviewers look for candidates who can name a specific metric, explain why it captures real user value, and identify what could cause it to move in the wrong direction.

How do I handle the 'first few weeks on the job' question?

Structure your answer in phases rather than listing actions at random. Start with learning: talk to users, review existing data, understand the current roadmap and company OKRs. Then describe how you would identify priorities based on what you learned. Avoid promising big deliverables or sweeping changes before you have context. Interviewers want to see planning instinct and humility, not overconfidence.

How should I prepare for the 'improve our product' question?

Use Stepchange's own products before the interview and form a genuine point of view on an area that could be better and why. In your answer, start with the user and the problem before jumping to a solution. Interviewers are not looking for a perfect answer: they want to see how you think, whether you connect user needs to business impact, and whether you can make a clear recommendation.

Is it worth applying to Stepchange if I do not have a PM title yet?

Many PMs break into the role from adjacent positions in engineering, business analysis, or consulting. Stepchange, like most companies, cares about your ability to think like a PM and demonstrate impact, not just your title.
