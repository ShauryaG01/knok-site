01 Overview

Overview

FPT Asia Pacific Pte Ltd is the regional arm of FPT Software, one of Asia's largest IT services groups, with delivery centres and client projects spanning the Asia-Pacific region including India. As of July 2026, the company has 30 open Software Engineer roles in India, reflecting active hiring across experience levels.

Candidates report a process that typically spans two to four rounds: an initial screening call, a technical coding or problem-solving round, a system design discussion, and a final round that often covers behavioural questions and culture fit. The exact structure varies by role and seniority, so treat this as a guide, not a guarantee.

FPT AP works heavily in digital transformation, enterprise software, and outsourced product development for global clients. Knowing this context helps you frame your experience in terms of delivery, client collaboration, and adaptability, which is exactly what the company values in its engineers.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions are commonly reported by candidates interviewing for Software Engineer roles at FPT Asia Pacific and similar IT services firms. Prepare specific, story-backed answers for each.

  1. Tell me about yourself and your most impactful project at your current or previous company.
  2. Why do you want to join FPT Asia Pacific over other IT services or product companies?
  3. Walk me through a complex technical problem you solved from scratch.
  4. How do you approach designing a system that needs to handle high traffic or scale over time?
  5. Describe a time you worked with an offshore or cross-functional team. How did you manage communication?
  6. How do you handle a situation where client requirements change significantly mid-sprint?
  7. What cloud platforms have you worked with in production, and what did you build or maintain?
  8. How do you ensure code quality in a team setting? Walk me through your review and testing approach.
  9. Tell me about a time you disagreed with a technical decision made by your lead or manager. What happened?
  10. How do you keep up with new technologies? Give an example of something you learned and applied in the last year.
  11. When would you choose a microservices architecture, and when would you avoid it?
  12. How do you prioritise when you have multiple deliverables competing for your time?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use these as templates. Replace the specifics with your own real project details.

Q: Tell me about yourself and your most impactful project.

*Situation:* At my previous company, we had a monolithic order processing system that caused frequent downtime during peak sales periods.

*Task:* I was given ownership of migrating the core order processing module to a microservices architecture.

*Action:* I broke the module into three independent services, containerised each with Docker, wrote unit and integration tests, and coordinated a phased rollout with the QA and DevOps teams.

*Result:* Downtime incidents dropped noticeably in the quarter after release. The team could deploy individual services independently, which made future releases faster and safer.

---

Q: Describe a time you worked across time zones or with an offshore team.

*Situation:* In my last role, our Bangalore team collaborated daily with a client team based in Singapore.

*Task:* I was the technical point of contact for a module integration that both teams owned jointly.

*Action:* I set up a shared documentation page, scheduled overlap hours for sync calls, and created a clear API contract so both sides could work independently outside the overlap window.

*Result:* The integration was delivered on schedule. The client specifically mentioned the smooth communication in their project feedback.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you disagreed with a technical decision made by your lead.

*Situation:* My team lead decided to store event logs in a relational database, which I felt would not scale well as log volumes grew.

*Task:* I was responsible for building the logging component and was concerned about long-term performance.

*Action:* I prepared a brief written comparison of relational vs. NoSQL options, shared it with the team lead privately first, and suggested a small proof-of-concept test before committing.

*Result:* After reviewing the results together, the team agreed to use a NoSQL store. The decision saved us a significant refactor later when log volumes grew as expected.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For behavioural questions ('Tell me about a time...'): Use the STAR framework. Situation sets the context briefly. Task clarifies your specific responsibility. Action covers what you personally did, step by step. Result explains what changed because of your actions. Keep each element concise. Most candidates spend too long on Situation and too little on Action and Result.

For technical and system design questions: Use a four-step structure. First, restate the problem and clarify scope. Second, state your assumptions out loud. Third, outline your approach before diving into code or diagrams. Fourth, after explaining your solution, discuss trade-offs and what you would change if constraints changed. This shows structured thinking, which interviewers at services companies value highly.

For 'Why FPT AP?' questions: Use a three-part structure. Start with something specific you know about the company, such as a client segment, a technology area, or a recent initiative. Then connect it to your own experience or career goal. End with one concrete thing you want to contribute. Avoid generic phrases like 'great culture' or 'growth opportunities' without backing them up with specifics.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

At an IT services firm like FPT Asia Pacific, the bar is not just technical ability. Interviewers are evaluating whether you can work with clients, adapt to changing requirements, and communicate clearly with people across different teams and time zones.

Communication clarity matters more than it does at pure product companies. You will often be the bridge between a technical team and a non-technical client stakeholder, so being able to explain your thinking in plain terms is a genuine skill being assessed.

Practical delivery experience is valued over algorithmic perfection. Being able to describe how you took a feature from requirement to production, including testing, review, and deployment, is a strong signal.

Adaptability is a recurring theme. Candidates who show they have handled scope changes, unclear requirements, or shifting priorities tend to leave a better impression than those who have only worked in stable, well-defined environments.

Ownership stands out. Interviewers look for engineers who do not wait to be told what to do next, who flag problems early, and who follow through on commitments.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A structured plan helps you cover the key areas without last-minute scrambling.

Step 1: Understand the company. Read about FPT Software's business model and the industries they serve. Banking, manufacturing, and retail are publicly reported focus areas. This will help you answer 'Why FPT AP?' with real substance.

Step 2: Revise your technical fundamentals. Focus on data structures, algorithms, system design basics, and the specific tech stack on your resume. Do not claim experience with a tool you cannot answer basic questions about.

Step 3: Build your story bank. Pick two or three strong project stories that you can adapt to common behavioural themes: delivering under pressure, handling conflict, recovering from a mistake, and leading without formal authority.

Step 4: Practice coding problems. Focus on arrays, strings, trees, and dynamic programming. Aim for clarity and correctness over speed in the first pass.

Step 5: Prepare your personal introduction. Keep it focused: who you are, your key experience, and why you are interested in FPT AP. Practice saying it naturally so it does not sound rehearsed.

Step 6: Prepare questions to ask. Good questions signal genuine interest. Ask about the team structure, the types of clients you would work with, the technology stack in use, and how engineers grow into senior roles.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Vague behavioural answers: Saying 'I am a good team player' is not an answer. Every behavioural question needs a specific story with a real outcome. Interviewers at services firms hear generic answers constantly.

Over-engineering system design: Some candidates try to impress by designing the most complex possible system. Instead, start simple, explain your reasoning, and add complexity only when the interviewer asks or when requirements demand it.

Not asking questions at the end: Ending the interview with 'No, I think I am good' signals low interest. Prepare at least two thoughtful questions, and make sure they are not ones whose answers are on the company website.

Hiding team contributions: Services companies care deeply about collaboration. If you only talk about what you did individually and never mention how you worked with others, it raises a flag with interviewers.

Claiming skills you cannot back up: If your resume lists Kubernetes or a specific framework, expect a follow-up question. Only list technologies you can discuss with confidence.

Generic 'Why this company?' answers: FPT AP interviewers can tell when a candidate has not done research. Mention something specific to the company, not just 'I want to work in a global environment.'
