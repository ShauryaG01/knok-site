01 Overview

Overview

Sanity builds a leading headless content platform used by product teams worldwide to manage structured content at scale. Engineering Managers at Sanity are expected to be both people leaders and technically credible, capable of guiding distributed teams building developer-focused products.

Sanity currently has 26 open roles, signalling an active hiring phase. EM interviews here typically span multiple stages covering leadership philosophy, technical decision-making, cross-functional collaboration, and how you handle the unique challenges of a remote-first, product-led engineering organisation. Candidates report that Sanity places strong emphasis on clarity of thinking, how you grow engineers, and your approach to building developer-centric products.

Salary ranges for Engineering Manager roles, based on knok jobradar data, run from 35-60 LPA at the Manager level, 55-90 LPA at Senior Manager, and 90-150+ LPA at Director level. Compensation at Sanity is publicly reported to be competitive for a growth-stage SaaS company.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions reflect what Engineering Manager candidates at Sanity typically encounter, based on public interview reports and the nature of Sanity's product and culture:

  1. How do you manage a team of engineers working on a developer-facing product? Sanity's customers are developers, so interviewers want to know if you can bridge the gap between your team's output and the technical users who consume it.
  1. Describe a time you drove a significant technical decision without being the one writing the code. This tests your ability to influence without authority and stay technically grounded as a manager.
  1. How do you handle disagreements between product managers and engineers about scope or priority? Sanity operates with tight product-engineering collaboration, so conflict resolution is a first-class topic.
  1. How do you build and maintain engineering culture in a remote-first, globally distributed team? Sanity is remote by design, so candidates who have managed distributed teams have a clear advantage here.
  1. Tell us about a time you had to re-prioritise a roadmap mid-quarter. How did you communicate it to the team? Expect follow-up questions on stakeholder management and keeping morale intact during the change.
  1. How do you think about developer experience (DX) as an engineering leader? Since Sanity's core promise is great DX, they look for EMs who treat this as a first-class concern, not an afterthought.
  1. Describe how you run performance reviews and give difficult feedback. Sanity reportedly cares deeply about people growth, so a vague answer here is a clear miss.
  1. How do you balance technical debt reduction with feature delivery? A classic EM question, but at Sanity expect it tied to real product scenarios around content APIs or real-time collaboration.
  1. What is your approach to hiring engineers? How do you define a strong hire for your team? This often includes a discussion of structured interviewing and reducing bias at the panel level.
  1. How have you contributed to a product strategy conversation, not just the execution? Sanity wants EMs who are strategic partners to product, not just delivery managers.
  1. Tell us about a time an engineer on your team was underperforming. What did you do? Expect a nuanced answer covering early signals, coaching conversations, and clear documentation of the process.
  1. How do you measure team health and engineering velocity without micromanaging? Sanity's engineering culture values autonomy, so this question probes whether you trust your team while staying genuinely accountable.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How do you handle a situation where a senior engineer disagrees with a technical direction you have set?

*Situation:* At my previous company, we decided to migrate our content delivery pipeline to an event-driven architecture. Our most senior backend engineer believed the synchronous approach we had was 'good enough' and was vocal in team meetings about the change.

*Task:* My job was to move forward with the migration without losing the trust or buy-in of someone who was both a technical pillar and a cultural anchor on the team.

*Action:* I arranged a one-on-one to genuinely understand his concerns rather than defend the decision. He raised valid points about operational complexity. I brought him into the design review with the platform team and gave him ownership of the observability layer, which was the part he cared most about. I also set up a structured spike to address his performance concerns with real numbers before the migration began.

*Result:* He became one of the migration's strongest advocates once he felt heard and had real ownership. The rollout completed on schedule and the observability tooling he built became a standard across three other teams.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you had to restructure your team's roadmap mid-quarter.

*Situation:* We were three weeks into a quarter when a key enterprise customer flagged a critical API reliability issue that could have caused them to churn. This was not in our roadmap.

*Task:* I needed to respond to the customer issue quickly while protecting the team's morale and avoiding wholesale roadmap chaos.

*Action:* I called an async planning session (we were fully remote) and walked the team through the customer impact transparently. Together we identified two features that could slip without significant downstream harm. I negotiated an extension with the product manager on one initiative and personally communicated the change to stakeholders, owning the decision rather than blaming the customer escalation.

*Result:* The reliability issue was resolved in eight days. The customer renewed their contract. Both slipped features shipped by end of quarter. The team appreciated the transparency and the fact that I handled the stakeholder conversation myself rather than passing it down.

---

Q: How do you grow engineers who are strong technically but struggle with communication?

*Situation:* I managed an engineer who was consistently the best technical contributor on my team but would often deliver code reviews and design documents in ways that confused or alienated peers, leading to friction across the team.

*Task:* I needed to develop her communication without making her feel like her technical strengths were being dismissed or treated as secondary.

*Action:* I framed communication as a technical skill, not a soft skill. I paired her with a staff engineer for two months specifically to observe how he structured written RFCs. We held monthly practice sessions where she presented a design to me as if I were a PM, building the habit of layered explanations. I also gave her small, visible wins, like running one section of a team meeting each sprint.

*Result:* Within one quarter her design documents were cited by two other teams as reference examples. She was promoted to Staff Engineer the following cycle, with cross-functional collaboration listed explicitly as a strength in her promotion packet.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

The STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the baseline for all behavioural questions. Sanity interviewers typically look for specificity in the Action step and honesty in the Result step, including what you would do differently next time.

For technical direction questions, use a simple three-part structure: what data or signal informed the decision, how you involved the team in the process, and how you measured whether it was right. Avoid framing decisions as ones you made alone.

For conflict or disagreement questions, lead with listening before resolving. A strong answer at Sanity shows you sought to understand the other perspective before pushing your own view. This aligns with their publicly stated value of candour paired with respect for others.

For roadmap or prioritisation questions, show that you can hold two things at once: the business goal and the team's wellbeing. Mention how you communicated changes, not just what you changed. Interviewers at product-led companies like Sanity care as much about your communication process as the final outcome.

For people development questions, give concrete examples with timelines. Vague answers like 'I coach my team regularly' do not land. Name the specific behaviour you wanted to change, the method you used, and the result in terms of the engineer's career trajectory.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Sanity interviewers, based on candidate reports, are looking for Engineering Managers who combine three things: genuine technical depth, a strong people-first philosophy, and product thinking that goes beyond just shipping tickets.

Technical credibility without micromanaging. You do not need to write code daily, but you must be able to have substantive conversations about architecture, API design, and trade-offs. At Sanity, where the product itself is developer infrastructure, an EM who cannot engage technically will struggle to earn team trust.

Remote leadership fluency. Sanity is remote-first and has been for years. Candidates who have only managed co-located teams should prepare specific examples of how they have built culture, handled conflict, and maintained alignment across time zones using async communication.

Developer empathy as a leadership value. Sanity's customers are developers. Interviewers want to see that you think about developer experience not just in product decisions but in how you structure your team's processes, documentation, and internal tooling.

Psychological safety and direct feedback. Sanity's culture values honesty. They look for EMs who can give hard feedback directly and early, not managers who avoid difficult conversations until they become a crisis.

Comfort with ambiguity. Sanity is a growth-stage company. EMs are expected to operate with incomplete information, set direction without a perfect playbook, and iterate. Candidates who prefer heavily structured environments may find this a genuine mismatch.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Company and product depth

Use Sanity's public documentation and developer portal to understand how their content platform works, how GROQ queries differ from REST or GraphQL, and who their typical customer is. Read their engineering blog if one is available. You do not need to become an expert, but you need enough context to speak intelligently about the product space and its developer-first priorities.

Week 2: Story bank

Write out eight to ten STAR stories covering: a difficult people situation, a technical decision you influenced, a roadmap reprioritisation, a hiring decision, a team culture moment, and a clear failure with a lesson. Sanity interviews are heavily behavioural so your stories are your primary asset going in.

Week 3: Mock interviews and live practice

Practise your stories out loud, ideally with a peer or coach. Trim anything that takes more than two minutes to tell. Prepare two to three questions for each interviewer that show you have thought about Sanity's specific engineering challenges, for example around real-time collaboration, content APIs, or scaling a developer tool business.

Before every round, review Sanity's publicly stated values and recent product announcements. Reference specific product decisions or engineering choices in your answers where relevant. This signals genuine interest, not generic interview preparation.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

1. Talking about technical work as if you still own the code. Sanity EMs lead engineers, they do not ship features themselves. Answers that default to 'I built X' rather than 'my team built X and here is how I enabled that' signal you have not fully made the transition from individual contributor to manager.

2. Vague answers on people development. Saying 'I care about my team's growth' without a specific example is one of the most common interview mistakes for EM roles. Have named, concrete stories ready before every round.

3. Ignoring the remote context. Candidates who do not address the distributed dimension of their leadership stories miss a key signal Sanity looks for. If your example happened in a co-located office, explain how you would adapt it for an async, global context.

4. Overpromising on product strategy. Some candidates try to impress by pitching product ideas for Sanity during the interview. This can backfire if the ideas show insufficient research. Stick to thoughtful questions and observations rather than unsolicited strategy proposals.

5. Not asking good questions. EMs who arrive with no questions, or only ask about salary and benefits, leave a weak impression. Ask about team structure, how the EM role interacts with product leadership, or what a successful first six months looks like in the role.

6. Skipping the 'what you would do differently' part of failure stories. Sanity interviewers want to see reflection and growth, not just a polished narrative of success. If you describe a failure, always close with what you learned and what you changed as a result.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Sanity Engineering Manager interview process typically have?

Candidates report that the process typically runs four to six rounds, covering an initial recruiter screen, a hiring manager conversation, one or two behavioural rounds, and often a panel or cross-functional interview. Some candidates also report a brief values or culture conversation at the end of the process. Round structures can change quarter to quarter, so confirm the current format with your recruiter at the start.

Does Sanity expect Engineering Managers to code during interviews?

Candidates typically do not report a live coding round for EM roles at Sanity. However, Sanity does assess technical depth through discussion, system design questions, and architecture trade-off conversations. You should be comfortable talking through technical decisions at a conceptual level, even if you are not expected to write code on a whiteboard or shared editor.

What salary can I expect for an Engineering Manager role at Sanity?

Compensation at growth-stage SaaS companies like Sanity is publicly reported to vary significantly based on level and location. Based on knok jobradar data, Engineering Manager roles in the Indian market generally range from 35-60 LPA at the Manager level and 55-90 LPA at Senior Manager. Sanity's exact internal bands are not publicly disclosed, so use your recruiter conversation to get a clear range early in the process.

How important is prior experience with headless CMS or content platforms?

Direct experience with headless CMS is not a strict requirement, but familiarity with developer tools, content APIs, or structured content workflows is a genuine advantage. More important is that you can demonstrate empathy for developer users and an understanding of what makes developer-facing products succeed. Candidates who invest time understanding Sanity's product before the interview consistently report a stronger overall experience.

How should I prepare if I have only managed co-located teams?

Sanity is remote-first, so prepare concrete answers about how you would adapt your leadership style for async communication and distributed teams. Think through how you would handle one-on-ones, team rituals, conflict resolution, and onboarding across time zones. Acknowledging the gap directly and showing a clear plan for bridging it is much stronger than suggesting your co-located experience maps over perfectly.
