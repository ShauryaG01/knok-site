01 Overview

Overview

Manatal is a Thailand-based HR tech company whose recruitment software is used by hiring teams across Asia and beyond. A Platform Engineer at Manatal typically owns the cloud infrastructure, internal developer platforms, and CI/CD pipelines that product engineering teams rely on every day.

As of July 2026, knok job radar data shows 204 Platform Engineer openings across India, with Manatal itself carrying 22 open roles. Bangalore leads the national count with 29 Platform Engineer jobs, followed by Delhi (12) and Pune (10).

Candidates report a process that typically spans three to four rounds: an initial screening call, one or two technical rounds covering infrastructure design and hands-on scenarios, and a final round focused on team fit and values. Two to three weeks of focused preparation is commonly cited by candidates who have cleared this role.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Platform infrastructure and design

  1. Walk us through how you would design multi-tenant cloud infrastructure for a SaaS recruitment product.
  2. How have you used Kubernetes in production? What problems came up and how did you solve them?
  3. Describe your CI/CD pipeline setup from scratch. Which tools did you choose and why?

Reliability and incidents

  1. Tell us about a major incident you owned. What did you do, and what changed afterward?
  2. How do you set up observability (logs, metrics, and alerts) for a platform your teams depend on?
  3. How do you decide when to page on-call versus handle an alert during business hours?

Infrastructure as code and tooling

  1. Which IaC tools have you worked with (Terraform, Pulumi, CDK)? How did you manage state and drift?
  2. How do you handle secrets management and access control across environments?

Developer experience and collaboration

  1. How do you balance platform stability with the speed developers expect from their pipelines?
  2. Tell us about a time you introduced a new platform tool or process that engineers were reluctant to adopt.
  3. How do you prioritise platform work when multiple product teams are requesting different things at the same time?

Product and company context

  1. What do you know about Manatal's product, and how does that shape what a Platform Engineer here should focus on?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell us about a major incident you owned.

*Situation:* Our production Kubernetes cluster started evicting pods during peak traffic, causing a partial outage for the API layer.

*Task:* I was the on-call Platform Engineer and had to diagnose the root cause, restore service, and prevent recurrence.

*Action:* I checked node resource usage and found that memory limits on several workloads had not been tuned after a recent scaling event. I coordinated with two app-team leads to temporarily reduce replicas on non-critical services, freeing up headroom. I then applied corrected resource requests and limits via a Terraform change and triggered a rolling restart.

*Result:* Service was restored within the same shift. We documented the fix as a runbook entry and added a weekly resource-utilisation review to our platform health checks, preventing similar evictions afterward.

---

Q: How do you handle secrets management across environments?

*Situation:* At a previous company, developers were storing database credentials in environment variable files that were sometimes accidentally committed to Git.

*Task:* I was asked to lead a secrets hygiene initiative across the engineering org.

*Action:* I evaluated HashiCorp Vault and AWS Secrets Manager, then chose Secrets Manager because the team was already deeply on AWS. I wrote Terraform modules for provisioning secrets, created IAM policies scoped to each service, and ran a workshop to walk developers through the migration. I also added a pre-commit hook to block accidental credential commits.

*Result:* Within a single sprint the team migrated all hardcoded secrets. A security audit the following quarter found zero hardcoded credentials in the codebase, a result the CISO highlighted as a positive milestone.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you introduced a platform tool engineers were reluctant to adopt.

*Situation:* Our CI pipelines were a patchwork of shell scripts with no caching, causing builds that were painfully slow and flaky.

*Task:* I proposed moving to a standardised pipeline-as-code approach using GitHub Actions with reusable workflows.

*Action:* Instead of mandating a switch, I picked two volunteer teams and helped them migrate first. I documented the before-and-after experience, kept a shared Slack thread for questions, and ran fortnightly office hours. I made the reusable workflow templates easy to extend so teams did not feel locked in.

*Result:* Both pilot teams saw noticeably faster feedback loops. Word spread organically, and by end of quarter all core product teams had migrated. Flaky-build complaints in our platform channel dropped sharply.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For infrastructure and system design questions, start by clarifying scope: what scale, what cloud provider, what team size? Then walk through your design in layers (compute, networking, storage, security) before discussing trade-offs. Interviewers want structured thinking, not just a list of tool names.

For incident and reliability questions, use the STAR method (Situation, Task, Action, Result). Be specific about what you personally did versus what the team did. End with what changed after the incident, since platform teams care deeply about learning loops and preventing repeats.

For 'why this tool' questions, a format that works well is: problem you faced, options you considered, reason you chose what you chose, and what you would do differently in hindsight. Ground every answer in a real constraint you faced, not a generic preference.

For prioritisation and collaboration questions, show that you think in terms of impact on product teams, not just technical elegance. Mention how you communicate trade-offs to non-platform stakeholders, since this is a skill Manatal interviewers consistently probe for.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Hands-on cloud depth. Manatal runs a cloud-native SaaS product, so interviewers typically probe for real experience with AWS, GCP, or Azure, not just surface familiarity. Expect questions that go one level deeper (not just 'have you used EKS' but 'what broke and how did you fix it').

Kubernetes and container fluency. Candidates report that Kubernetes comes up in almost every technical round. Know resource management, networking basics (CNI, Ingress, Services), and how you have handled cluster upgrades or node failures.

IaC discipline. Terraform is the most commonly cited tool in Platform Engineer job descriptions. Interviewers want to see that you treat infrastructure code with the same care as application code: modules, state management, code review, and drift detection.

Developer empathy. A platform team exists to serve developers. Interviewers look for candidates who have thought about developer experience and can explain platform decisions in terms a product engineer would care about.

Incident ownership. Expect at least one question on how you handle production problems under pressure. They want calm, methodical thinking and a demonstrated habit of writing postmortems and acting on them.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Technical foundations

Review your cloud provider's managed Kubernetes service (EKS, GKE, or AKS) and practise explaining how you would set up a production cluster from scratch. Revisit Terraform state management, modules, and workspace strategies. Study Manatal's product to understand what the platform needs to support.

Week 2: System design and scenarios

Practise designing a multi-tenant SaaS platform on paper, covering compute, storage, networking, and security layers. Write down three incidents from your own experience and structure each as a STAR story. Prepare a clear answer for 'why platform engineering at Manatal' that connects to the company's stage as a growing SaaS business.

Week 3: Mock interviews and polish

Do at least two mock technical rounds, ideally with a peer who can ask follow-up questions. Review the questions listed in this guide and time your answers (aim for two to three minutes per behavioural answer). Prepare two or three questions to ask your interviewer about the platform team's roadmap and how they measure success.

Day before: Scan Manatal's recent product announcements or news. Have your key examples ready in a notes doc so you are not blanking mid-interview.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

  1. Listing tools without context. Saying 'I used Terraform and Kubernetes' tells the interviewer nothing. Always follow with the problem you were solving and the outcome you delivered.
  1. Vague incident stories. 'We had an outage and fixed it' is not an answer. Interviewers want to know your specific role, the steps you took, and what changed afterward to prevent recurrence.
  1. Ignoring developer experience. Platform Engineers who only talk about uptime and cost, and never mention how their work helped product teams ship faster, miss a key signal Manatal looks for.
  1. Not researching the product. Manatal is a recruitment SaaS. Knowing that helps you frame platform decisions (data isolation for multi-tenancy, API reliability for integrations) in terms that resonate with the interviewer.
  1. Overselling solo heroics. Interviewers notice when every answer starts with 'I' and never involves collaboration. Show that you work well with app teams, security, and leadership.
  1. Skipping the 'what would you do differently' reflection. Most interviewers ask it. Have a genuine, specific reflection ready. It signals maturity and a learning mindset.

Is there a live coding or hands-on lab round?

Some candidates report a take-home or live exercise involving IaC (usually Terraform) or a Kubernetes troubleshooting scenario, though this is not universal across all hiring cycles. Prepare to write or review infrastructure code on the spot just in case. Practising small Terraform modules and reading Kubernetes manifests before your technical round is a strong use of prep time.
