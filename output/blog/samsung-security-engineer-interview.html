01 Overview

Overview

Samsung is one of the most recognised electronics companies in the world, and its India offices are major engineering hubs. Samsung Research Institute Bangalore (SRI-B) is one of the company's largest R&D operations outside South Korea. Security Engineers here work across mobile platforms (Galaxy devices), IoT products, SmartThings, Tizen, and enterprise solutions under the Knox umbrella.

As of July 2026, knok jobradar tracks 8 open Security Engineer roles at Samsung, against a total market of 628 Security Engineer positions across all companies in India. Bangalore leads with 69 openings, followed by Delhi and Pune (12 each), Hyderabad (10), Mumbai (7), and Chennai (6).

Interviews at Samsung typically span three to five rounds covering security fundamentals, system and product design, applied cryptography, and mobile or IoT-specific knowledge. Candidates report that the process rewards depth of reasoning over surface-level tool familiarity.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions reflect what candidates report encountering in Samsung Security Engineer interviews. Expect technical depth and product-context framing.

  1. Walk us through a real vulnerability you discovered. How did you identify it, report it, and verify the fix?
  2. Samsung ships products across mobile, IoT, and enterprise. How would you approach threat modelling for a new connected home device?
  3. Explain TrustZone and Trusted Execution Environments (TEE). How do they protect data on an Android device?
  4. You are reviewing C or C++ code for a firmware update handler. What classes of vulnerabilities do you look for first, and why?
  5. How would you design a secure key management system for a consumer device with constrained hardware resources?
  6. A zero-day affecting a shipped Galaxy product is disclosed publicly. Walk us through your response plan from the moment you are alerted.
  7. What is your experience with Android application security? Name specific tools and techniques you have used.
  8. Compare the OWASP Mobile Top 10 and the OWASP Top 10. Which risks are unique to mobile, and why?
  9. Describe how you would perform a black-box penetration test on a Samsung mobile application. What is your methodology?
  10. How do you balance thorough security requirements against a tight product launch deadline?
  11. What fuzzing or static analysis tools have you used? Give a concrete example of a bug you found with one of them.
  12. How would you design a secure over-the-air (OTA) update mechanism? What are the key risks you would mitigate?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use these as a structural guide. Replace the specifics with your own real experience.

Q: Walk us through a real vulnerability you discovered and how you handled it end to end.

*Situation:* I was conducting a routine security code audit of our Android SDK at my previous company, which was integrated by several banking clients.

*Task:* My responsibility was to review the token storage logic before a major release.

*Action:* I found that refresh tokens were stored in SharedPreferences without encryption, making them accessible to any app on a rooted device. I documented the finding with a proof-of-concept, raised a priority-one bug in our tracker, and worked directly with the development team to migrate storage to the Android Keystore. I also drafted a secure coding guideline for token handling so this class of issue could not recur.

*Result:* The fix shipped before the release. The secure coding guideline was adopted team-wide and caught two similar issues in subsequent reviews.

---

Q: How would you approach threat modelling for a new connected home device?

*Situation:* I was brought into a smart lock project early in the design phase, before architecture decisions were finalised.

*Task:* I needed to produce a threat model early enough that security could be built in, not added on later.

*Action:* I facilitated a STRIDE session with the firmware, cloud, and mobile app teams. We drew data flow diagrams covering all three surfaces and catalogued threats by likelihood and impact. I flagged the Bluetooth pairing handshake as the highest-risk interface and pushed for a challenge-response mechanism instead of a static key.

*Result:* Three design changes were made before any production code was written. The product passed an external security audit in 2024 with no critical findings.

---

Q: A zero-day affecting a shipped product is disclosed publicly. Walk us through your response.

*Situation:* A researcher published details of a memory corruption vulnerability in our device firmware, including a working proof-of-concept.

*Task:* I was the on-call security engineer and responsible for coordinating the response.

*Action:* I confirmed reproducibility in our lab, assessed exploitability across current firmware versions, and escalated to leadership with a severity rating and proposed response plan. I coordinated with the firmware team on a patch, opened a private communication channel with the researcher, and drafted customer advisories in parallel.

*Result:* A patch reached customers within two weeks of disclosure. The response process we documented became our standard incident response playbook.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use STAR for behavioural questions. Structure every story as Situation (one or two sentences of context), Task (your specific responsibility), Action (what you personally did, not what the team did), and Result (a concrete or measurable outcome). Keep the full answer under two minutes when spoken.

Use a structured walkthrough for technical and design questions. For questions like 'how would you design X' or 'how would you test Y', candidates report that Samsung interviewers reward a clear structure: state your assumptions first, define the threat surface or scope, walk through your approach step by step, call out trade-offs, then summarise. Avoid jumping straight to tool names without showing your reasoning process.

For incident or crisis scenarios, lead with triage (confirm and scope the issue), then escalation, then containment, then root cause analysis, then remediation, then post-mortem. This structure mirrors real security operations and signals that you have worked under actual pressure.

Bring Samsung product context wherever possible. Frame answers around mobile platforms, IoT, or embedded systems. Referencing Android security, TEE, Knox, or OTA update security shows you have researched the company's actual product surface rather than preparing for a generic security role.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Deep technical fundamentals, not just tool familiarity. Samsung interviewers typically probe the 'why' behind a control, not just whether you know the tool name. Be ready to explain the cryptographic primitives behind TLS, the memory safety issues that lead to buffer overflows, or the trust boundaries inside a Trusted Execution Environment.

Product-first security thinking. Security Engineers at Samsung are embedded in product teams and must work within real constraints. Interviewers want to see that you treat security as an enabler, not only a blocker. Show that you can translate a risk into a business impact and propose proportionate mitigations.

Influence without authority. Candidates report being asked how they convince non-security engineers to prioritise fixes. Prepare examples where you changed a team's direction through reasoning and evidence, not just escalation.

End-to-end ownership. In every story you tell, make clear that you drove the issue from discovery to closure. Engineers who flag issues and then move on are valued less than those who see a problem through.

Genuine interest in Samsung's product surface. Showing you have thought about Galaxy device security, Knox, SmartThings, or Tizen signals real interest in the role, not just a willingness to take any security job.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Core technical review. Refresh your fundamentals in cryptography (symmetric vs asymmetric encryption, hashing, digital signatures, PKI), network security (TLS, firewall architectures), and application security (OWASP Top 10, injection classes, authentication and authorisation flaws). If mobile security is not your strength, prioritise the Android security model, the Android Keystore, and the basics of TEE and TrustZone.

Week 2: Samsung product deep-dive. Read Samsung's publicly available Knox and Galaxy security whitepapers. Review public CVEs affecting Samsung products to understand the vulnerability classes their team deals with in practice. Work through the OWASP Mobile Top 10 if you have not already, and compare it with the standard OWASP Top 10.

Week 3: Stories and practice. Write out four to five STAR stories covering: a vulnerability you found and fixed end to end, a security design you led or contributed to, a time you convinced a team to prioritise a fix, and an incident you responded to. Practice these out loud. Do two or three mock interviews with a peer if you can arrange it.

Week 4: Coding and systems. Candidates report occasional coding questions involving secure implementation or bug-spotting in a code snippet. Brush up on reading and writing C, C++, or Python with a security lens. Review CERT C Secure Coding guidelines and Android secure coding practices.

Before each round: Research the interviewer on LinkedIn if their name is shared with you in advance. Prepare two or three specific questions about the team's product area or current security priorities. These show genuine engagement and give you useful information about the role.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Talking about tools without showing reasoning. Saying 'I use Burp Suite for this' without explaining your methodology tells the interviewer nothing about your thinking. Always connect the tool to what you are looking for and why.

Generic answers with no Samsung context. Framing every story around a generic enterprise IT environment, when Samsung is a hardware and software product company, signals low preparation. Where honest, weave in mobile, IoT, or embedded context.

Saying 'we' instead of 'I' in STAR answers. Interviewers are evaluating you, not your former team. Be precise about what you personally did, decided, and owned.

Proposing mitigations without discussing trade-offs. If you recommend a security control, be ready to discuss the cost, performance impact, or user experience implications. Samsung engineers operate under real product constraints, and ignoring trade-offs signals inexperience.

Not preparing questions to ask. Candidates who have no questions at the end of a round are often perceived as less engaged. Prepare two or three thoughtful questions per round about the team's projects, security priorities, or how the security function is structured.

Overstating experience in areas you are not strong in. Samsung's technical rounds go deep. Claiming expertise and then stumbling on follow-up questions does more damage than being honest about your current level.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Samsung Security Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report a process of three to five rounds. This typically includes an initial recruiter or HR screen, one or two technical rounds covering security fundamentals, coding, and system design, a practical or case-study discussion, and a final managerial or team-fit conversation. The exact structure varies by team and seniority level, so ask your recruiter what to expect for your specific role.

Does Samsung test DSA or competitive programming for Security Engineer roles?

Candidates report that Samsung Security Engineer interviews focus more on security domain knowledge than on algorithmic problem-solving. You may encounter a question involving code review or a small secure-implementation task, so being comfortable reading and writing C, C++, or Python is useful. A heavy LeetCode-style preparation is typically not the priority for this role.

What salary should I expect for a Security Engineer at Samsung India?

Samsung does not publicly disclose salary bands, and the current dataset does not have enough Samsung-specific data points for a reliable figure. Publicly reported figures on Glassdoor and levels.fyi suggest Security Engineer compensation at large technology companies in Bangalore varies significantly by level and experience. Check those platforms for recent community-reported numbers and compare with offers in similar roles at comparable companies.

How important is Samsung Knox knowledge for the interview?

Knowing Knox at a working level is a strong signal that you have researched the company. You do not need to memorise every feature, but you should be able to explain what Knox does at a high level: hardware-backed security, secure boot, and workspace separation for enterprise customers. Samsung publishes Knox security whitepapers publicly, and reading them is one of the most targeted preparation steps you can take for any Samsung security role.

Is Bangalore the only city where Samsung hires Security Engineers?

Bangalore has the most openings by a wide margin, with 69 Security Engineer positions tracked across all companies in the city as of July 2026.
