02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly in CoinDCX Software Engineer interviews, based on candidate reports. Expect a mix of algorithms, system design, and fintech or crypto domain knowledge.

  1. Design a high-availability order book for a crypto exchange. What data structures would you use, and how would you handle concurrent reads and writes at high volume?
  1. How would you build a rate limiter for the trading API? Walk through your design and the trade-offs you are making between throughput and platform protection.
  1. A user places an order at the same moment a deposit updates their balance. How do you ensure atomicity across these two operations in a distributed system?
  1. How would you detect wash trading or suspicious order patterns in real time without adding latency to the main order flow?
  1. Explain the CAP theorem with a concrete example from a crypto exchange context. When would CoinDCX choose availability over consistency, or the reverse?
  1. How would you design a wallet reconciliation service that catches discrepancies between on-chain balances and the internal ledger?
  1. The CoinDCX app must work well in low-connectivity conditions common in tier-2 and tier-3 Indian cities. How would you design the client-side data sync strategy?
  1. How would you build a price alert notification system that can fan out to a very large number of users without overwhelming the infrastructure?
  1. Walk us through a production incident you debugged under pressure. What was your root cause analysis process?
  1. Tell us about a time you pushed back on a product requirement due to technical constraints. How did the conversation go?
  1. How would you migrate a monolithic trading service to microservices with zero downtime on a live platform?
  1. What do you understand about blockchain transaction finality, and how does it affect how an exchange like CoinDCX credits user deposits?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format for every behavioural question: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep each answer tight and under a couple of minutes when spoken aloud.

---

Q: Walk us through a production incident you debugged under pressure.

*Situation:* Our payment processing service started throwing timeout errors during peak evening hours. Alerts fired and users could not complete withdrawals.

*Task:* I was the on-call engineer. My responsibility was to identify the root cause quickly and restore service without losing any transaction data.

*Action:* I pulled logs from the past hour and noticed a spike in database connection wait times. I checked the connection pool configuration and found it had not been updated after a recent traffic increase. I raised the pool size immediately, added a circuit breaker to the downstream payment gateway call, and deployed the config change within about twenty minutes.

*Result:* Withdrawals recovered quickly. I followed up with a postmortem, documented connection pool sizing as a runbook item, and added a proactive alert so the on-call team would catch this before users noticed in future.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you pushed back on a product requirement.

*Situation:* Product wanted to ship an 'instant deposit credit' feature quickly. It required crediting balances before blockchain confirmation, which exposed the platform to double-spend risk.

*Task:* My job was to surface the technical risk clearly without blocking the business goal entirely.

*Action:* I wrote a short risk document showing the potential fraud window, then proposed an alternative: show a 'pending balance' immediately for good UX, while the actual tradeable balance unlocked only after the required confirmations. I walked the PM through both options in a focused call.

*Result:* Product accepted the revised design. We launched on schedule, the UX improvement went live, and fraud risk was contained. The pattern became our standard approach for handling deposits going forward.

---

Q: Describe a time you improved the performance of a critical system.

*Situation:* Our analytics pipeline ran hourly batch jobs that took a very long time to complete, leaving dashboard data stale for most of the hour.

*Task:* I was asked to reduce staleness without a full rewrite of the pipeline.

*Action:* I profiled the job and found the bottleneck was a large join on an unindexed column in the transactions table. I added a composite index, rewrote the query to avoid a full table scan, and introduced incremental processing so only new records were handled each run instead of the full dataset.

*Result:* Job runtime dropped substantially according to our internal benchmarks. Dashboard data refreshed far more frequently, and the operations team could make decisions from near-real-time data instead of waiting for the next hourly batch.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the standard for all behavioural questions. Keep each part tight: one or two sentences on Situation and Task, the bulk of your answer on Action (what you specifically did, not what the team did), and a clear observable or measurable Result.

For system design questions, use this structure: clarify requirements and constraints first, define the data model, then walk through components (API layer, processing layer, storage, caching), then cover scaling and failure modes. CoinDCX specifically cares about consistency guarantees and fault tolerance, so always address what happens when a component goes down before the interviewer asks.

For algorithm questions, think out loud from the start. State the brute-force approach, explain why it falls short, then walk toward the optimal solution. Candidates report that CoinDCX interviewers value clear reasoning over arriving at the perfect answer immediately.

For domain questions (blockchain, fintech regulation, exchange mechanics), it is fine to say 'I have not worked directly with this, but here is how I would reason about it.' Showing structured thinking on unfamiliar ground is valued more than bluffing with half-remembered facts.

The PREP framework (Point, Reason, Example, Point again) works well for opinion questions or 'what do you think about X' prompts. State your position, give your reasoning, back it with a real example, then restate your conclusion. Keep it conversational, not lecture-like.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

CoinDCX engineers work in a fast-moving, regulated, high-stakes environment. Interviewers are typically looking for a few specific signals.

Comfort with ambiguity. Crypto markets move unpredictably. Candidates who ask good clarifying questions and adapt their answers when requirements shift score well. Those who wait to be led through a problem do not.

System reliability thinking. This is a financial platform. Interviewers want to see that you think about failure modes, data loss, and recovery paths automatically, not only when prompted. Bring up consistency trade-offs before you are asked.

Ownership and speed. CoinDCX is in active growth mode. Candidates who describe taking end-to-end ownership (from spotting an issue to shipping the fix and measuring the outcome) stand out over those who describe waiting for others to define the solution.

Crypto or fintech curiosity. You do not need deep blockchain expertise, but you should understand basic concepts: how exchanges work, what transaction finality means, why KYC and AML matter for a regulated exchange. Candidates who have done even basic reading on CoinDCX's product leave a noticeably stronger impression.

Communication clarity. Several candidates report that interviewers interrupt during system design to ask 'why did you choose that approach?' This is not adversarial. It tests whether you can explain technical decisions clearly, which engineers at CoinDCX do regularly with non-technical stakeholders.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

About a week before your first technical round:

Start with data structures and algorithms. Focus on arrays, hash maps, trees, graphs, and sliding window patterns. Do timed practice on an online judge. For system design, study distributed systems fundamentals: consistent hashing, message queues, database replication, and API rate limiting. These topics come up directly in CoinDCX interviews.

Spend time understanding how a crypto exchange works at a high level: the order book, matching engine, wallet infrastructure, and deposit and withdrawal flows. You do not need to have built these, but you should be able to reason through the design from first principles.

A few days before:

Prepare several STAR stories from your past work. Map them to common themes: debugging under pressure, pushing back on product, improving system performance, cross-team collaboration, and handling a failed launch. Having a ready story for each theme means you are not improvising under pressure.

Research CoinDCX specifically. Check their engineering blog if available, look at their product changelog, and understand what sets them apart from other Indian crypto exchanges. Interviewers consistently notice candidates who have done this homework.

The day before:

Do a light review, not a cram. Rehearse your STAR stories out loud. Prepare a few thoughtful questions to ask your interviewer about the team, the codebase, and how engineering decisions get made. Avoid asking about salary in technical rounds.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping into code before understanding the problem. CoinDCX interviewers specifically note that candidates who start coding immediately without asking clarifying questions often build the wrong solution. Spend the first few minutes nailing down constraints, scale expectations, and edge cases.

Giving vague STAR answers. Saying 'I improved the system' or 'we fixed the bug' is not enough. Interviewers want to know what you personally did. Use 'I' deliberately in your Action section, and be specific about the technical decision you made and why.

Treating the system design round as a monologue. The interviewer will redirect you. Treat it as a collaborative conversation, not a presentation. Pause and check in: 'Does this approach make sense for your constraints?' This signals the kind of engineer CoinDCX actually wants on the team.

Ignoring the fintech context. Candidates who design systems as if data loss or inconsistency is acceptable fail to signal that they understand the stakes on a trading platform. Always factor in what happens to user funds if a component fails.

Not preparing questions to ask. Finishing an interview with 'I have no questions' reads as lack of genuine interest. Prepare questions about the engineering culture, how on-call works, what the biggest technical challenge the team is currently facing, and how code reviews are handled.

Underselling past impact. Indian candidates in particular tend to be modest about outcomes. If you improved a system, say so clearly. If your fix unblocked a team or reduced infrastructure costs, say that too. Concrete results are not boasting, they are evidence.
