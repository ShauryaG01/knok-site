01 Overview

Overview

QuickSell is a B2B commerce platform that helps merchants build digital catalogs and manage orders, primarily through WhatsApp-first workflows. Its core users are small and medium businesses across India who want to move their selling online without needing complex software. As a PM at QuickSell, you are expected to think deeply about the SMB buyer-seller journey, design for users with low digital literacy, and balance growth with retention in a fast-moving market.

As of July 2026, QuickSell has 10 open Product Manager roles. Candidates typically report 3-4 rounds: an initial screen, a product thinking discussion with a PM or hiring manager, a take-home assignment or live case study, and a final discussion with senior leadership. The process is known to be practical and problem-focused. Interviewers tend to test how you think through real merchant pain points rather than how well you recite frameworks.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly, based on what candidates report from QuickSell PM interviews.

  1. Tell us about a product you built or improved for non-tech-savvy users. What made the experience work for them?
  2. QuickSell merchants primarily communicate with buyers over WhatsApp. How would you design a feature to help them track inventory without leaving WhatsApp?
  3. How would you define and measure success for a new digital catalog feature launched to existing merchants?
  4. A merchant reports that buyers are not opening the catalog links they share. Walk us through how you would diagnose this problem.
  5. How would you prioritize a feature that improves retention for existing merchants versus a feature that could help onboard a much larger pool of new merchants?
  6. Tell us about a time you had to make a product decision with very limited data. What was the situation and what did you do?
  7. How would you increase the catalog share rate among QuickSell merchants? What levers would you pull first?
  8. What does a good onboarding experience look like for a merchant who has never used digital selling tools before?
  9. A competitor launches a free version of QuickSell's core catalog feature. How would you respond as the PM owning that area?
  10. Describe a product you love. What is one thing you would change about it and why?
  11. How would you think about expanding QuickSell into markets outside India while keeping the product simple for low-tech users?
  12. Walk us through how you would handle sprint planning when the engineering team is stretched thin relative to the backlog.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format for all behavioral questions. Here are three examples tailored to the QuickSell context.

Q: Tell us about a product you improved for non-tech-savvy users.

*Situation:* At my previous company, we ran a B2B platform for small retailers and noticed a significant drop-off in catalog creation: merchants would start but not finish uploading their products.

*Task:* My goal was to understand why this was happening and increase catalog completion rates.

*Action:* I interviewed several merchants over two weeks and found that most were uploading photos one at a time and did not understand how to add pricing. I introduced bulk photo upload with auto-fill templates for pricing and added a progress indicator so users could see how close they were to finishing. I also added an in-app WhatsApp support option so merchants could ask questions without switching apps.

*Result:* Catalog completion rates improved in the following month. More importantly, merchants who completed their catalogs had much higher retention after the first month, which validated the idea that completion was a leading indicator of long-term engagement.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you made a product decision with limited data.

*Situation:* We were deciding whether to build a 'buyer wishlist' feature. We had no quantitative data showing buyers wanted it, only a handful of support requests.

*Task:* I had to recommend a path forward without waiting months for a full user study.

*Action:* I ran a lightweight experiment. I added a 'Save for later' button to the catalog page and measured click rate over two weeks using a simple analytics flag. I also spoke to a handful of buyers directly over WhatsApp. Click rates were low, but the interviews surfaced a deeper insight: buyers did not want wishlists, they wanted a quick way to share a subset of the catalog with a family member before buying.

*Result:* Instead of building wishlists, we shipped a 'share selection' feature, which became one of the more used features that quarter. The key lesson was to treat limited data as a signal to go talk to users rather than a reason to delay the decision.

---

Q: How would you prioritize features for existing merchants versus acquiring new merchants?

*Situation:* At a previous company, we had competing backlog priorities: a bulk order tracking tool for power users and a simplified onboarding flow for new signups.

*Task:* I needed to make a call on what the team would build next with limited engineering capacity.

*Action:* I mapped both features to business outcomes. The onboarding flow had a direct tie to top-of-funnel growth, which was the company's primary goal that quarter. The bulk order tool would reduce churn among a smaller segment of high-revenue users. I scored both using an impact-versus-effort matrix and presented the tradeoffs to leadership with data on segment size and projected revenue impact.

*Result:* We shipped the onboarding flow first, and new merchant activation improved the following month. The bulk order tool was queued for the next cycle with clearer prioritization criteria that everyone had agreed on. The process itself became a template for future backlog decisions.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

These frameworks are well-suited for the types of questions QuickSell interviewers ask.

CIRCLES (for product design questions): Comprehend the situation, Identify the customer, Report the customer needs, Cut through prioritization, List solutions, Evaluate tradeoffs, Summarize. Use this when asked to design or redesign a feature from scratch. It forces you to stay user-first before jumping to solutions.

RICE (for prioritization questions): Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort. Useful when asked to compare features or roadmap items. QuickSell values pragmatism, so always tie your scores back to actual merchant or buyer behavior rather than abstract estimates.

Jobs-to-be-Done: Focus on the outcome a merchant is hiring the product to achieve, not the feature itself. For QuickSell, most merchants are hiring the product to 'make selling feel as simple as chatting.' This lens helps you avoid over-engineering solutions and keeps your answers grounded.

5 Whys (for root cause questions): When asked to diagnose a problem such as low catalog opens or high churn, use 5 Whys to move from symptom to root cause before proposing solutions. Jumping to solutions without this step is the most common miss in diagnostic questions.

North Star Metric approach: For metrics questions, define a North Star that reflects long-term value (for example, 'number of orders placed through a QuickSell catalog per week') and then identify input metrics that drive it. Avoid vanity metrics like app downloads or page views.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates report and QuickSell's public product positioning, interviewers are typically looking for a few specific qualities.

SMB empathy: QuickSell's users are often first-time digital sellers. Interviewers want to see that you understand their constraints: limited time, low digital literacy, high sensitivity to cost, and a preference for WhatsApp over apps or portals. Answers that assume users are comfortable with dashboards and analytics tools tend to miss the mark entirely.

Comfort with ambiguity: QuickSell operates in a fast-moving space with limited historical data on some user segments. Interviewers want to see that you can make reasonable decisions without perfect information and that you know how to validate assumptions cheaply and quickly.

First-principles thinking: Candidates who cite frameworks without connecting them to the actual problem get lower scores. Interviewers want to see you reason from the user's real situation to the solution, not retrofit a solution onto a framework.

Collaboration signals: QuickSell is a relatively compact team. Interviewers pay close attention to how you describe working with engineering, design, and sales. Answers that position you as the sole decision-maker raise flags.

Business awareness: You should be comfortable talking about growth levers, retention metrics, and how product decisions connect to revenue. Candidates who cannot explain why a feature matters to the business beyond 'users want it' typically do not make it to the final round.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused two-to-three week preparation plan for the QuickSell PM interview process.

Week 1: Know the product deeply. Use QuickSell as a merchant yourself if possible. Create a test catalog, share it over WhatsApp, and walk through the order flow end to end. Note every friction point you encounter. Read their blog, product release notes, and any publicly available founder or PM interviews. Map out their core user journey and identify where the biggest drop-off risks likely sit.

Week 2: Practice the core question types. Work through several product design questions using CIRCLES or a similar structure. Practice metric definition questions by picking one QuickSell feature and writing out a North Star metric plus three input metrics. Prepare 5-6 STAR stories from your own experience that cover: making decisions under uncertainty, shipping something for non-tech users, handling a failed product decision, and cross-functional collaboration.

Week 3: Case study readiness. Candidates typically report a take-home assignment or live case as part of the process. Practice writing a short product spec for a simple feature. Aim to produce a clear problem statement, proposed solution, success metrics, and one named tradeoff within a reasonable time limit. Have a peer review it for clarity, specifically checking whether someone unfamiliar with the product can follow your reasoning.

Before each round: Prepare 2-3 thoughtful questions for the interviewer about the product roadmap, how PMs at QuickSell measure success, or how the team is structured. These signal genuine interest and give you useful information for evaluating the role.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Candidates who have gone through QuickSell PM interviews report a few patterns that consistently hurt their chances.

Designing for tech-savvy users: The most common miss is proposing solutions that assume merchants are comfortable with apps, dashboards, or web portals. QuickSell's core value is WhatsApp-first simplicity. Solutions that add layers of complexity, even useful complexity, often do not fit the user or the product vision.

Jumping to solutions too fast: Interviewers report that candidates who skip the problem-definition step and go straight to features get lower scores. Take a couple of minutes to clarify the user, the goal, and the constraints before proposing anything. This signals maturity as a PM.

Vague metrics: Saying 'we would measure engagement' is not enough. Interviewers want specifics: which event, which user segment, over what time window, and compared to what baseline. The more concrete you are, the more you signal that you have actually thought about how to know whether something worked.

Ignoring tradeoffs: QuickSell operates with finite engineering resources. Candidates who propose long feature lists without acknowledging build cost or opportunity cost signal poor judgment. Always name the tradeoff explicitly and be willing to defend your prioritization choice.

Sounding memorized: QuickSell interviewers tend to ask follow-up questions that probe your real thinking. If your answer sounds rehearsed verbatim, the follow-up questions will quickly reveal whether you actually understood what you said. Practice explaining your ideas conversationally, not reciting them.

How many rounds does the QuickSell PM interview typically have?

Candidates typically report 3-4 rounds for PM roles at QuickSell. Does QuickSell give a take-home product assignment?

Candidates report that a take-home assignment or written product case study is a common part of the QuickSell PM process, though this varies by role and level. If you receive one, focus on clarity over length. A tight one-page spec with a clear problem statement, proposed solution, success metrics, and one named tradeoff will stand out more than a long document with no clear point of view.

How important is B2B or SMB experience for QuickSell PM roles?

QuickSell's entire product is built for small and medium businesses, so interviewers place high value on candidates who understand the SMB mindset: cost-conscious, low digital literacy, and a preference for simple tools over powerful ones. Direct B2B or SMB experience is a strong plus but is not always required. What matters more is your ability to demonstrate genuine empathy for this type of user in how you approach problems during the interview.

How should I prepare for the product design round?

Start by using QuickSell yourself as a merchant before the interview. The best candidates can speak to specific friction points they noticed in the actual product, which signals real preparation over generic framework recitation. Practice structuring your answers using a framework like CIRCLES: define the user, their goal, the constraints, and then propose a couple of solutions before picking one and naming the tradeoff. Always anchor your solution to what makes sense for a low-tech, WhatsApp-first user.
