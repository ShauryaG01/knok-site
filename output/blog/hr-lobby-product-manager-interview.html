02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions reflect what HR LOBBY interviewers commonly probe, based on the company's focus on HR tech, recruiter workflows, and talent marketplace products. Expect at least one product case question and at least one metrics or data question in a typical loop.

  1. Walk me through a product you built end to end. What was the problem, who was the user, and what did you ship?
  2. HR LOBBY connects employers, recruiters, and job seekers. How would you prioritize features when these three groups have conflicting needs?
  3. A recruiter using our platform says the quality of applicants has dropped. How do you diagnose and fix this?
  4. Design a feature that helps a first-time small-business owner post a job and find a good candidate quickly.
  5. How would you define and measure 'successful placement' as a north-star metric for an HR marketplace?
  6. We want to grow the number of active employers on the platform significantly. What would your first-quarter roadmap look like?
  7. Tell me about a time you had to kill a feature or project you believed in. What happened and what did you learn?
  8. How do you work with engineering when you disagree on scope or timelines?
  9. An A/B test shows click-through is up but downstream conversion is flat. How do you interpret this and what is your next step?
  10. What is the biggest risk to an HR tech platform's business model and how would you build product strategy around that risk?
  11. How would you improve the job-seeker experience on a platform like HR LOBBY without increasing cost to the employer side?
  12. Tell me about a time you used data to change your own mind about a product decision.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: A recruiter using our platform says the quality of applicants has dropped. How do you diagnose and fix this?

*Situation:* At my previous company, our B2B SaaS platform saw a notable drop in recruiter satisfaction scores over one quarter, and the top complaint was irrelevant applicant profiles.

*Task:* I owned the applicant-matching module and needed to identify the root cause and propose a fix without disrupting active recruiter workflows.

*Action:* I pulled funnel data and found that a recent change to the job description template had removed structured fields, so our matching algorithm was falling back to keyword search only. I ran qualitative interviews with several recruiters to confirm the pain. I then worked with engineering to reintroduce structured fields as optional but weighted heavily in match scoring, and set up an A/B test gating a subset of new job posts.

*Result:* Within a few weeks, recruiter satisfaction on those posts improved noticeably, and structured-field adoption climbed organically because recruiters saw better results. We rolled it out to all job posts in the following sprint.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you had to kill a feature or project you believed in.

*Situation:* I had championed an in-app video interview tool for a recruitment platform I worked on. We spent several sprints scoping and building an MVP.

*Task:* Midway through, I needed to present the business case to senior leadership for continued investment.

*Action:* I ran a usage audit and found that only a small fraction of our target recruiters had tried the feature, and those who did rated setup friction as the main barrier. I also found that established video-interview tools already integrated with our platform via API. The honest conclusion was that we were rebuilding something users could already access. I presented the data clearly to leadership and recommended pausing internal development in favour of deepening those integrations.

*Result:* Leadership agreed. We freed up engineering capacity for a higher-priority workflow feature. The decision was uncomfortable because I had publicly advocated for the feature, but it was the right call and built trust with the team.

---

Q: How do you prioritize features when employers, recruiters, and job seekers have conflicting needs?

*Situation:* At a marketplace product I managed, the sales team was pushing a feature for employers, while support tickets from job seekers pointed to a different urgent pain. Engineering had capacity for one.

*Task:* I needed to make a defensible prioritization call and get alignment across sales, support, and the leadership team.

*Action:* I mapped each request to the north-star metric (successful placements per month) and estimated the incremental impact using funnel drop-off data and publicly reported industry benchmarks. I also assessed which user group, if underserved, would most quickly hurt platform retention. Job seekers abandoning the platform would reduce supply and hurt employer value. I framed the job-seeker fix as protecting the platform's core liquidity.

*Result:* The team aligned on the job-seeker fix first. Employer-facing work was slotted for the following sprint with a clear rationale the sales team could accept. The process became a reusable template for future prioritization calls.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the baseline for behavioural questions. Every story you tell should move through these four beats. Keep Situation brief (one or two sentences), spend the most time on Action (what you specifically did, not what 'the team' did), and always close with a concrete Result.

CIRCLES (Comprehend, Identify, Report, Cut, List, Evaluate, Summarize) is useful for design questions like 'design a feature for...' It forces you to clarify the user before jumping to solutions. In an HR LOBBY context, always establish first: is this employer-facing, recruiter-facing, or job-seeker-facing? Each group has very different mental models and success criteria.

The Metrics Framework for analytical questions: start with the goal (what outcome does the business care about), define a north-star metric, break it into input metrics (the levers you can pull), then identify where the data signals something is broken. For HR LOBBY, a strong north-star candidate is 'successful placements per month' or 'time-to-hire for active employer accounts.'

The Prioritization Stack for roadmap questions: state your framework upfront (ICE, RICE, or a simple effort-vs-impact grid), score the options transparently, and then show you can override the framework with strategic judgment. Interviewers want to see you use data AND context, not just a formula.

The 'So What?' Check applies to every answer: after each point, ask whether a smart non-PM could have said the same thing. If yes, add the PM-specific layer, whether that is a user insight, a trade-off, or a metric implication, that only someone thinking about product would surface.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

HR LOBBY interviewers are typically looking for five things, roughly in this order of importance.

Domain empathy. Because HR LOBBY serves recruiters, employers, and job seekers, they want PMs who have thought seriously about hiring workflows, not just quoted generic PM theory. Reference real pain points in the recruitment process wherever you can.

Structured thinking under pressure. Product case questions are designed to see how you handle ambiguity. Interviewers watch whether you clarify before you solutionize, whether your logic holds under follow-up pushback, and whether you can adapt a framework without losing the thread.

Data fluency. Candidates report that interviews typically include at least one question where you interpret a metric, design an experiment, or decide what to measure. You do not need to be a data scientist, but you need to speak comfortably about funnels, cohorts, and A/B test design.

Collaboration and influence. PMs at HR LOBBY work across engineering, sales, and customer success. Expect behavioural questions that probe how you handle disagreement, how you build buy-in without authority, and how you balance short-term sales pressure against long-term product vision.

Self-awareness. Questions about failures or killed projects are common. Interviewers want to see that you can separate your ego from the outcome and extract a real lesson. Candidates who are defensive or reframe failures as successes tend to score lower on culture fit.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Foundation

Spend the first few days understanding HR LOBBY's product. Use the platform as both a recruiter and a job seeker if you can get access. Map out the core user journeys, identify friction points, and note which features are most prominent. Read any publicly available product announcements or job description language to understand their vocabulary and priorities.

Revise the PM interview fundamentals: STAR for behavioural questions, CIRCLES or a similar structured approach for design questions, and a clear mental model for metrics and prioritization.

Week 2: Practice

Practice at least two full case answers out loud, not just in your head. Record yourself if possible. Most candidates underestimate how different it feels to verbalize a structured framework versus think it through silently. Use the 12 questions listed above as your mock question bank.

Prepare 4-5 adaptable stories from your own experience covering: a product you built, a prioritization call, a time you used data to make a decision, a time you navigated disagreement, and a failure or project you killed.

Week 3: Polish and logistics

Research the level you are applying for. If targeting a PM (3-6y) role, the salary band is typically 24-40 LPA. Senior PM roles run 40-60 LPA. Know your number going in so the compensation conversation does not catch you off guard.

Prepare 3-4 specific questions for your interviewers: what does success look like in the first few months, how does the PM team structure discovery time, and what is one product decision the team is actively debating. Generic questions signal low preparation.

Confirm interview format, number of rounds, and whether there is any pre-read or case material with your recruiter contact before the first round.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping to solutions before clarifying the user. In a marketplace like HR LOBBY, 'the user' is always a choice. Candidates who skip clarification and immediately propose features for a generic user look shallow. Always ask first: is this employer-facing, recruiter-facing, or job-seeker-facing, and what does success look like for that person?

Using jargon without substance. Terms like 'north-star metric' or 'zero-to-one product' are fine if you can back them up with specific reasoning. Interviewers at HR LOBBY report frustration with candidates who use PM vocabulary as a substitute for actual analysis.

Treating metrics questions as arithmetic exercises. Candidates sometimes focus on calculation accuracy and forget to interpret the number in context. What does a drop in this metric mean for users? What is the next step? That interpretive layer is what separates a PM answer from an analyst answer.

Giving team answers instead of 'I' answers. In STAR stories, every 'we' should be unpacked into what you specifically decided, drove, or shipped. Interviewers cannot assess your contribution if the story stays collective throughout.

Ignoring the business model. HR LOBBY runs a marketplace with multiple revenue streams (typically job postings, subscriptions, or placement-based fees). Candidates who design features without considering monetization or unit economics come across as naive. You do not need exact numbers, but acknowledge that product decisions affect the business.

Not asking good questions. Candidates who have no questions at the end signal low interest or low preparation. Prepare specific questions about the team, the roadmap, or current product challenges. Generic questions like 'what is the culture like' are forgettable.
