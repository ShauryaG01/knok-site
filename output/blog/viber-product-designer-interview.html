02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates report these questions coming up across Viber's Product Designer interview rounds. The mix covers portfolio deep-dives, product thinking, research methods, and cross-functional collaboration.

  1. Walk us through a messaging or communication product you designed. What were the biggest UX challenges?
  2. Viber is used by people across dozens of countries. How do you design for cultural diversity and varying levels of digital literacy?
  3. Describe a time when user research changed the direction of a design you were working on.
  4. How would you approach redesigning Viber's group chat experience to make it easier for community admins to manage large groups?
  5. Tell us about a situation where you had to push back on a product requirement because it would hurt the user experience.
  6. How do you think about accessibility and inclusive design in a messaging app where users range from teenagers to senior citizens?
  7. Describe your design handoff process. How do you make sure engineering builds what you intended?
  8. Viber competes with WhatsApp and Telegram. How do you keep up with the competitive landscape and factor it into your design decisions?
  9. Walk us through a feature that did not perform as expected after launch. What did you learn and how did you apply it?
  10. How do you handle conflicting design requests from multiple product managers or stakeholders at the same time?
  11. How would you design a new monetization feature for Viber (such as a premium tier) without making free users feel left out?
  12. Describe a time you had to present a design decision to senior leadership that was questioned. How did you defend or revise your approach?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time when user research changed the direction of a design you were working on.

*Situation:* I was leading the redesign of the onboarding flow for a fintech app. Our product manager assumed users wanted to see all features upfront, so the original brief called for a feature showcase carousel.

*Task:* My job was to design the onboarding screens and validate the concept before development started.

*Action:* I ran several moderated usability sessions with new users before committing to any design direction. Every participant skipped the carousel and went straight to the signup button. Two participants said the feature list felt 'like an ad.' I shared the recordings with the team, proposed a value-first onboarding that led with one clear benefit, and ran a second round of tests to validate the revised concept.

*Result:* The team adopted the revised approach. Post-launch data showed a meaningful improvement in onboarding completion, which the team publicly reported as a key metric win for that quarter.

---

Q: Tell us about a situation where you had to push back on a product requirement because it would hurt the user experience.

*Situation:* A product manager at my previous company wanted to add three promotional banners to the home screen of our messaging app to support a marketing campaign.

*Task:* I was responsible for the home screen experience and needed to raise the concern constructively without blocking the campaign.

*Action:* I pulled up industry surveys on notification fatigue and showed the PM how cluttered home screens reduce session length, a metric he owned. I proposed a single, dismissible banner placed lower on the screen and created a quick prototype to show both options side by side. I framed it as 'helping the campaign land better' rather than just saying no.

*Result:* The PM agreed to the single banner. Candidates at similar companies report that this kind of data-backed framing typically works better in stakeholder discussions than a pure design argument.

---

Q: Walk us through a feature that did not perform as expected after launch. What did you learn?

*Situation:* I designed a reactions feature for a community chat product. I was confident the visual design was solid and that users would discover it naturally.

*Task:* The goal was to increase lightweight engagement in group chats, but actual usage after launch was lower than the team expected.

*Action:* I went back to the data and ran a short user survey. Respondents said they had not noticed the long-press gesture that triggered reactions because there was no visual affordance for it. I redesigned the trigger with a subtle animation cue and worked with engineering to ship an update quickly.

*Result:* Engagement on the feature improved after the update. The lesson I took away: in messaging apps, users move through conversations quickly and will not explore hidden gestures on their own. Affordances matter more than you expect.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

The STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the baseline for behavioural questions. Keep Situation and Task brief (two to three sentences combined) and spend most of your time on Action and Result.

For product thinking questions, use a simple three-part structure: first, define the user and their problem clearly before touching solutions. Second, walk through two or three design directions with trade-offs. Third, explain how you would measure success. Interviewers at product-led companies like Viber want to see you think out loud, not just arrive at a polished answer.

For 'how would you redesign X' questions, a useful sequence is: clarify the goal, identify who uses the feature and what frustrates them, map the current flow's pain points, propose changes, and state what metric would tell you if the change worked. At Viber, 'X' often means a messaging or community feature, so tie your answer to real communication patterns such as reading receipts, group admin tasks, or media sharing.

For competitive comparison questions, avoid saying a competitor 'is better.' Instead, describe specific interaction patterns you find interesting and explain what user problem each one solves. This shows awareness without sounding dismissive of Viber's own product.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Candidates who have gone through Viber's Product Designer process typically report that interviewers look for a few consistent things.

Clear communication of design decisions. Viber's team spans multiple countries, so being able to explain the 'why' behind a design choice in plain language matters more than portfolio polish.

Evidence of user empathy over aesthetic preference. Questions about research and user testing come up in almost every round. Interviewers want to see that you talk to users regularly and that findings actually change your work.

Cross-functional collaboration instinct. Because messaging products are engineering-heavy, designers who understand technical constraints and communicate well with developers stand out. Bring specific examples of working closely with engineers or data analysts.

Comfort with ambiguity. Viber operates in a competitive, fast-moving space. Candidates report being asked how they handle incomplete briefs or shifting priorities. Show that you can make a reasonable decision with limited information and course-correct quickly.

Global and inclusive thinking. Given Viber's international user base, any answer that considers users outside metros (low-bandwidth environments, lower-end devices, non-English speakers) signals the kind of empathy the team values.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Two to three weeks before your first round: Review your three to five strongest portfolio pieces and prepare a two-minute verbal summary for each. Focus on the problem, your process, and the outcome. Pick at least one project where something went wrong and you course-corrected.

Use Viber actively. Install the app if you have not already. Go through the group chat, community, and business messaging features. Note two or three specific UX decisions you find interesting or that you would approach differently. Interviewers appreciate candidates who have clearly spent time in the product.

Prepare for the design exercise. Candidates typically report some form of design exercise, either take-home or live on a shared screen. The prompt usually involves a messaging or communication product scenario. Practice working through design problems out loud: pick a feature from any messaging app, walk through a redesign from user problem to final screens, and time yourself so you stay within a realistic window.

Map your experience to Viber's product areas. Messaging flows, notification design, onboarding, community management tools, and business messaging are all active areas. Identify which parts of your portfolio match these and lead with those in your presentation.

One week before: Prepare STAR stories for the questions listed above. Have at least two stories ready for: a time research changed your direction, a time you pushed back on a requirement, and a time a launch did not go as planned. Practice saying them out loud, not just writing them down. 07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Talking about aesthetics instead of decisions. Saying 'I chose this colour palette because it felt calming' without connecting it to a user insight or a business goal is a common miss. Always anchor visual choices to a reason a non-designer can understand.

Skipping the problem framing. Many candidates jump straight to solutions in product thinking questions. Interviewers at Viber want to see that you can define the user and the problem before you sketch anything. Take a moment to frame the problem clearly first.

Showing only one type of portfolio project. Presenting only visual redesigns without any research-driven or data-informed projects makes it hard for interviewers to assess your full process. Include at least one project where user testing or analytics shaped a significant decision.

Being vague about outcomes. Saying 'the feature was well received' is less convincing than describing a publicly reported improvement or a specific qualitative signal like reduced support tickets or clear stakeholder buy-in. If you do not have hard numbers, describe what changed in observable terms.

Not asking clarifying questions in the design exercise. Jumping straight into wireframes without asking about constraints, users, or success metrics suggests you skip discovery in real work. Treat the exercise prompt like a real brief: ask before you sketch.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Viber Product Designer interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically includes a recruiter screen, a portfolio presentation, a design exercise or case study round, and one or two behavioural rounds with the hiring manager or cross-functional partners. The total number of rounds can vary by team and seniority level. Confirm the expected structure with your recruiter early so you can prepare for each stage appropriately.

Is there a take-home design task at Viber?

Candidates typically report some form of design exercise, either take-home or live on a shared screen. The prompt usually involves a messaging or communication product scenario. If it is take-home, focus on showing clear thinking and a structured process over pixel-perfect screens. A focused, well-reasoned submission often reads better than an exhaustive one that tries to cover every edge case.

What salary can a Product Designer expect at Viber in India?

Viber-specific salary data for India is not publicly reported in a reliable way. Broadly, knok jobradar tracks mid-level Product Designers (3-5 years) at 14-24 LPA and senior designers (6-9 years) at 26-40 LPA across the Indian market. Actual offers depend on your experience, the specific team, and how negotiations go. Use Glassdoor and levels.fyi to check recent data points before entering salary discussions.

Does Viber hire Product Designers based in India?

Viber has open roles that include India-based positions, and knok jobradar shows active Product Designer hiring in the Indian market. The company has a distributed team, so some roles may involve collaboration with colleagues in other time zones. Confirm the working location and day-to-day collaboration model with your recruiter, since this can vary by role and team.

What tools should I know for a Viber Product Designer interview?

Figma is the industry standard and candidates report it is expected at most product companies. Familiarity with prototyping within Figma (not just static screens) is useful for demonstrating interaction flows in a messaging context. Knowledge of design systems, component libraries, and handoff workflows is a plus. Tool proficiency alone will not get you the role, but not knowing Figma is typically a flag at this level.

How should I structure my portfolio to stand out for a Viber role?

Lead with two or three case studies that show end-to-end process: discovery, ideation, testing, and outcome. If you have experience with messaging, social, or communication products, put those first since they are closest to Viber's domain. Each case study should make it easy for the interviewer to answer: 'What problem did this person solve, and how do I know it worked?' Keep the portfolio easy to navigate since reviewers typically spend only a short time before deciding whether to dig deeper.
