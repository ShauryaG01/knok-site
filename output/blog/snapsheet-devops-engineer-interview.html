01 Overview

Overview

Snapsheet is a US-based insurtech company that builds cloud-native claims automation software. As of mid-2026, they have 15 open DevOps roles, a strong signal that their engineering team is actively scaling. Their stack is heavily AWS-based, with Kubernetes at the centre of their container orchestration strategy.

Candidates report a process that typically includes an initial HR screen, a technical discussion with the hiring manager, and one or more hands-on rounds covering system design and practical DevOps tasks. Snapsheet values engineers who can work with autonomy and communicate clearly with product and development teams. If you are targeting this role, expect deep questions on production reliability, CI/CD design, and cloud cost control.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Based on Snapsheet's insurtech domain and their known AWS-plus-Kubernetes stack, candidates report questions across these themes:

  1. Walk me through how you manage Kubernetes clusters in production. How do you handle upgrades with zero downtime?
  2. Describe a CI/CD pipeline you built or significantly improved. What tools did you choose and why?
  3. Tell me about a production incident you owned end to end. What was your investigation and resolution process?
  4. How do you implement infrastructure as code? Which tools do you prefer, Terraform or CDK, and what drives that choice?
  5. Which AWS services would you use to design a highly available microservices backend, and how would you approach cost control?
  6. How do you handle secrets and credentials in a containerised workload?
  7. Describe a time you reduced deployment failures or improved release reliability.
  8. Walk me through your observability setup: metrics, logs, and traces. What does a healthy alerting strategy look like to you?
  9. How do you work with developers to unblock deployments without compromising stability?
  10. What is your approach to auto-scaling: how do you decide thresholds and avoid over-provisioning?
  11. How do you harden container images and Kubernetes workloads against security threats?
  12. Have you worked with GitOps workflows? What is your experience with tools like ArgoCD or Flux?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a production outage you handled end to end.

*Situation:* At my previous company, our core API service went down during a high-traffic period on a weekday afternoon.

*Task:* I was the on-call engineer responsible for identifying the cause and coordinating the fix without causing further disruption.

*Action:* I checked our Grafana dashboards, spotted a spike in memory usage tied to a pod deployed that morning, isolated the affected deployment, rolled back to the previous stable image, and opened a war-room channel with the dev team to trace the memory leak in the new build.

*Result:* Service was restored within the same business day. We conducted a post-mortem, added a memory-limit alert to catch similar regressions early, and the root-cause fix shipped in the next release cycle.

---

Q: Describe a CI/CD improvement you led.

*Situation:* Our team's release process was manual and error-prone. Developers would package builds, upload to S3, and trigger deployment scripts by hand. Releases sometimes slipped by a full day.

*Task:* The engineering manager asked me to redesign the pipeline for reliability and speed.

*Action:* I migrated the pipeline to GitHub Actions, containerised the build environment, added automated test gates as quality checks, and introduced a blue-green deployment strategy on ECS so releases could happen with zero downtime.

*Result:* Releases became repeatable and same-day. Developer confidence in the pipeline improved noticeably, and rollbacks, when needed, became a single command rather than a manual, multi-step process.

---

Q: How have you improved security in your infrastructure?

*Situation:* An internal security audit flagged that our Kubernetes workloads had overly permissive RBAC roles and that secrets were stored as plain environment variables in deployment manifests.

*Task:* I was given ownership of remediating the findings before an upcoming compliance review.

*Action:* I introduced HashiCorp Vault for secrets injection, rewrote RBAC policies following least-privilege principles, and added Trivy to the CI pipeline to scan container images on every build.

*Result:* We passed the compliance review with no critical findings. The image-scanning step became a permanent gate in the pipeline, and the team adopted the RBAC templates as a company-wide standard.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use STAR for every behavioural question. STAR stands for Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep the Situation and Task brief, then spend most of your time on the Action, because that is where your skill and judgement are visible.

For technical design questions, use a three-step structure:

  1. Restate your constraints: scale, budget, existing stack, uptime requirements.
  2. Walk through your architecture choice and the trade-offs you considered.
  3. Describe how you would validate, monitor, and iterate on that design in production.

For incident response questions, interviewers want to see a methodical mind, not heroics. Cover: detection, hypothesis, isolation, fix, and follow-up such as a post-mortem or alerting improvement. Avoid stories where you 'just restarted the service' with no root-cause analysis.

For cost or scaling questions, anchor your answer in data: the metrics that told you something was wrong, the thresholds you set, and how you measured the result. If you do not have exact figures, say 'we saw a meaningful drop in resource waste' rather than inventing a percentage.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Snapsheet's DevOps interviewers, based on candidate reports, typically look for a few key things:

Ownership mindset. They want engineers who treat the production environment as their responsibility, not just a hand-off point after a deployment script runs. Mention post-mortems, alerting you set up proactively, and times you flagged a risk before it became an incident.

Depth in AWS and Kubernetes. Surface-level answers about 'using EKS' will not stand out. Be ready to go deep: how you manage node groups, how you handle persistent storage, and how you think about pod disruption budgets during rolling updates.

Communication with non-DevOps teams. Snapsheet builds software for insurance carriers, where reliability matters deeply. Interviewers want to know you can explain an outage or a deployment delay to a product manager or a client-facing team without jargon.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Core technical review

Revise Kubernetes concepts: pod lifecycle, resource limits, RBAC, and networking such as services, ingress, and CNI. Practice writing Terraform modules from scratch. Review common AWS services for a microservices setup: EKS, ECS, RDS, ElastiCache, ALB, IAM, and CloudWatch.

Week 2: Scenario practice

Practise STAR answers for several real incidents or improvements from your own career. If you are earlier in your career, draw from projects or internships. Record yourself answering out loud and peer-review with a colleague if possible.

Week 3: Snapsheet-specific prep

Read Snapsheet's engineering blog and any public job postings to understand their language and priorities. Look at their open roles (currently 15 DevOps positions listed) to identify recurring skill mentions. Prepare a few questions to ask the interviewer about deployment cadence, on-call culture, and incident response process.

Day before the interview

Review your resume line by line. For every tool or project you list, prepare a short story covering what you built, why you chose that approach, and what the outcome was. Do a mock system-design question on paper.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Giving textbook answers without context. Saying 'I use Terraform for infrastructure as code' tells the interviewer nothing. Always attach a real scenario: what you built, why you chose that tool, and what the outcome was.

Skipping the result. STAR answers that end at the Action leave the interviewer guessing whether the work actually helped. Even a qualitative result such as 'the team shipped with more confidence' or 'on-call alerts dropped noticeably' is better than trailing off.

Over-claiming on tools. If you have used Helm but only for basic chart installs, do not imply you have written complex custom charts. Interviewers at product companies like Snapsheet often have deep hands-on experience and will probe further.

Not asking questions. Candidates who ask nothing signal low curiosity. Prepare thoughtful questions about their incident response culture, deployment frequency, and how DevOps and product teams interact.

Ignoring the business context. Snapsheet serves insurance carriers. Mentioning that you understand why uptime and compliance matter in a regulated industry, even briefly, shows you think beyond the terminal.
