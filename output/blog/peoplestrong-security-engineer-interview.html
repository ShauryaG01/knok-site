01 Overview

Overview

PeopleStrong is a Gurugram-based HR technology company that builds cloud SaaS platforms for enterprise workforce management, covering payroll, performance, attendance, and more. As of July 2026, knok tracked 111 open roles at PeopleStrong, placing them among the more actively hiring companies in Indian HR-tech.

A Security Engineer here typically works on securing a multi-tenant SaaS product that handles sensitive employee and payroll data for large enterprise clients. Responsibilities span cloud security, application vulnerability management, compliance support, and helping product teams build securely from the ground up.

Candidates report the interview process typically spans three to four rounds: a recruiter or HR screening call, one or two technical rounds, and a final discussion with a senior leader or HR. 02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These are the questions candidates report coming up most often for Security Engineer roles at PeopleStrong. Prepare concrete stories and specific examples for each.

  1. Walk us through a security incident you detected and contained. What was your process from alert to resolution?
  2. How do you approach a vulnerability assessment for a web application?
  3. PeopleStrong handles sensitive HR and payroll data for large enterprise clients. How would you design a data classification and access control policy for a SaaS product like ours?
  4. What is your hands-on experience with cloud security on AWS or Azure? Walk us through specific controls you have configured.
  5. You have a long vulnerability backlog and limited engineering bandwidth. How do you decide what gets fixed first?
  6. Have you worked with compliance frameworks such as the ISO information security management standard or SOC 2? Walk us through your direct involvement.
  7. How would you perform a threat model for a new product feature before it ships?
  8. A developer pushes code with a known security flaw to production during a release crunch. How do you handle that?
  9. What tools do you use for SIEM and log monitoring, and how have you tuned alerts to reduce noise?
  10. How do you explain a critical security finding to a product manager who has no security background?
  11. Describe your penetration testing experience. Have you run assessments in-house, coordinated external vendors, or both?
  12. Security sometimes means slowing down a release. How do you balance security controls with the pace of a fast-moving SaaS team?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Practise these out loud before your interview. Each follows the STAR format.

Q: Walk us through a security incident you detected and contained.

*Situation:* Our SIEM flagged unusual outbound traffic from one of our application servers late at night.

*Task:* I was the on-call engineer and had to determine quickly whether this was a false positive or an active breach.

*Action:* I isolated the server from the network immediately, then pulled logs from the SIEM and the cloud provider to trace the traffic. I found a compromised third-party dependency that had been injected with a cryptominer. I worked with the dev team to identify the exact release that introduced it, rolled back the affected service, patched the dependency, and wrote a post-mortem. I also added a dependency-scanning step to the CI pipeline as a permanent control.

*Result:* The incident was contained within four hours. The post-mortem led to automated dependency checks being added to every build, preventing similar issues going forward.

---

Q: How do you prioritise a large vulnerability backlog?

*Situation:* After our first full vulnerability scan at a previous company, we came back with hundreds of findings across severity levels.

*Task:* I had one week to present a prioritised remediation plan to engineering leadership.

*Action:* I used a risk-based approach combining standard severity scores with business context. For each finding I asked: is this asset internet-facing? Does it store sensitive data? Is there a known exploit available? I sorted findings into three buckets: fix this sprint, fix this quarter, and accept with documentation and owner sign-off. I also set up a recurring scan cadence so findings would never pile up to that scale again.

*Result:* Leadership approved the plan, and we cleared all critical and high findings within two months. The tiered backlog model became our standard vulnerability management process.

---

Q: How do you communicate a critical security finding to a non-technical stakeholder?

*Situation:* I found a broken authentication flaw in our customer portal that could allow an attacker to access another user's account data.

*Task:* I needed the product head to agree to delay a scheduled release so we could fix it first.

*Action:* I skipped the technical jargon entirely. I said: 'Imagine one of your customers logs in and can see another customer's payslip. That is what this bug allows right now.' I framed the risk in terms of data breach notification obligations and damage to customer trust, not severity scores.

*Result:* The release was delayed by three days, the fix shipped cleanly, and the product head later asked me to review the security messaging in customer-facing documentation. The plain-language framing built lasting trust between the security team and the product org.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For incident and behavioural questions, use the STAR format throughout: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep each part tight. The Result is the section most candidates forget to make concrete. State what changed, what was prevented, or what process was put in place.

For technical design questions, follow a three-step structure. Start by mapping the threat surface (what data exists, who can access it, where it flows). Then propose layered controls (authentication, authorisation, encryption, monitoring). Finally, explain your trade-offs: why you chose one approach over another, and what you would revisit if the threat model changed.

For compliance questions, lead with your hands-on involvement rather than reciting definitions. 'I helped prepare evidence for an SOC 2 audit and owned the access review process' tells the interviewer far more than 'I know what SOC 2 is.' Name the control, describe your role, and state the outcome.

For prioritisation questions, always tie your answer to business risk, not just technical severity. Interviewers at product companies want to hear that you understand which vulnerabilities actually threaten the business and can communicate that to non-security stakeholders.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

PeopleStrong builds SaaS for enterprises that trust it with employee records and payroll data. Interviewers are looking for engineers who treat data protection as a first principle, not a box-ticking exercise.

Practical, hands-on experience. Candidates report that interviewers probe for specifics. 'I have worked with SIEM tools' will not land as well as walking through a real detection and response you personally led.

Communication skills. Because PeopleStrong is a product company, security engineers need to influence product and engineering teams who are optimising for speed. Your ability to explain risk in plain language is as important as your technical depth.

Compliance and regulatory awareness. Because PeopleStrong serves large enterprise clients, familiarity with information security management frameworks and data privacy obligations matters. You do not need to be a compliance specialist, but you should be able to speak to controls you have directly owned.

Good security judgement under pressure. Interviewers typically present scenarios where there is no perfect answer: a developer wants to ship, you have found a moderate-severity flaw, the deadline is tomorrow. They want to see that you can reason through trade-offs rather than defaulting to 'block everything' or 'approve it and hope for the best.'

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Cloud security fundamentals. Review IAM policies, network security groups, encryption at rest and in transit, and logging or monitoring setup for AWS or Azure. Be ready to describe specific configurations you have set up, not just name the services.

Week 2: Web application security. Go through the OWASP Top 10 and prepare a real or realistic example for each vulnerability category. Practise explaining each one to a non-technical listener in plain language.

Week 3: Build your STAR stories. Write out at least five incidents or projects: one incident response, one vulnerability remediation, one compliance or audit involvement, one time you pushed back on a feature for security reasons, and one time you communicated risk to leadership. Practise saying each one out loud within three minutes.

Week 4: Research PeopleStrong's product. Understand what data they handle (employee records, payroll, attendance, performance) and what security controls a multi-tenant HR SaaS product needs. Think about tenant isolation, role-based access, and audit logging as natural conversation starters.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping the 'why' in compliance answers. Saying 'we followed the information security management standard' without explaining which control you owned or what it was protecting tells the interviewer nothing useful. Name the control, describe your role, and state the outcome.

Over-indexing on tools. Listing several SIEM or scanner products is less impressive than explaining how you tuned one specific alert to catch a real attack. Interviewers want evidence of depth, not breadth of tool names.

Not connecting security to business risk. At a product company like PeopleStrong, interviewers want to hear that you understand why a vulnerability matters to the business, not just that its severity rating is high. Always tie your findings back to customer impact or regulatory exposure.

Vague incident stories. 'We handled it and everything was fine' is not a result. Practise giving stories with specific outcomes: what changed, what was prevented, what process was put in place afterward.

Ignoring the SaaS and multi-tenancy context. PeopleStrong is not a bank or government agency. Show that you understand the balance between security controls and product velocity, and that you know how to work with product and engineering teams rather than acting as a blocker.
