01 Overview

Overview

upGrad is one of India's largest edtech platforms, offering online degrees and upskilling courses in partnership with universities and industry. The Engineering Manager role sits at the core of product delivery, with EMs owning squad-level execution, people growth, and cross-functional alignment with product and business teams.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These are the questions that come up most often in upGrad EM interviews, based on candidate reports and the nature of the role.

  1. Tell us about a time you had to rebuild a low-performing engineering team. What specific steps did you take?
  2. upGrad runs multiple learning products simultaneously. How do you prioritize when two squads need the same platform resource at the same time?
  3. Describe your approach to setting OKRs for an engineering team in a fast-moving edtech environment.
  4. How have you handled a situation where a product deadline conflicted with your team's technical debt backlog?
  5. Tell us about a time you influenced a decision without direct authority, for example convincing a product manager to delay a launch.

  9. How do you handle a senior engineer who is technically strong but struggles with collaboration or communication?
  10. Walk us through how you managed a major incident or production outage. What was your role and what changed afterwards?
  11. How do you balance feature velocity with platform reliability for a product that runs around the clock for active learners?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell us about a time you had to rebuild a low-performing engineering team.

*Situation:* I joined a team of six engineers that had missed three consecutive quarterly targets. Morale was low and the codebase had significant technical debt from rushed feature work.

*Task:* My job was to stabilize delivery within two quarters and improve team engagement, without losing anyone from the team.

*Action:* I started with individual one-on-ones to understand each person's specific blockers. I found two engineers were overloaded while others were underutilized. I rebalanced ownership, introduced a lightweight sprint review process, and carved out a dedicated portion of each sprint for debt reduction. I also set up monthly career growth conversations with each engineer, tied to a visible internal career ladder.

*Result:* On-time delivery improved significantly within two quarters. One engineer who had been on an informal performance plan was promoted within the year. The process changes stayed in place even after I moved to a new team.

---

Q: How do you prioritize when two squads need the same platform resource?

*Situation:* Two product squads at my previous company both needed the same data-platform team's bandwidth in the same quarter. Both had committed to external stakeholders.

*Task:* I needed to facilitate a fair, transparent decision without either squad feeling blindsided or de-prioritized unfairly.

*Action:* I brought both engineering leads and the platform lead into a single working session. We mapped each request to a specific business outcome and estimated the cost of delay for each. I proposed splitting the platform team's bandwidth by priority, giving the higher-impact squad more allocation for that quarter, with a documented commitment to reverse the priority the following quarter. I shared the written decision with both product managers so there were no surprises.

*Result:* Both squads delivered their critical paths on time. The facilitation approach became a template the organization reused for future cross-team prioritization conflicts.

---

Q: Walk us through how you managed a major production outage.

*Situation:* Our platform went down during a live examination window, affecting a large number of learners mid-test.

*Task:* I was the on-call EM. My job was to coordinate resolution, communicate clearly with stakeholders, and make sure we never had a repeat.

*Action:* I set up a war room immediately, assigned a dedicated incident commander, and separated the 'fix it now' team from the 'communicate to stakeholders' stream. I sent short, factual status updates to leadership at regular intervals throughout the incident. After resolution, I facilitated a blameless post-mortem within two days and identified three process gaps: monitoring coverage, runbook completeness, and escalation paths.

*Result:* We shipped fixes for all three gaps in the following sprint. Leadership cited the post-mortem process as a model for other teams, and we had no repeat incidents of the same class in the following quarter.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for leadership and behavioral questions. Keep the Situation brief (two to three sentences at most). Spend most of your time on Action and Result. upGrad interviewers want to see that you made a deliberate choice, not just that you survived the situation.

Quantified trade-offs for prioritization questions. Show that you can weigh business impact, cost of delay, and team capacity against each other. Interviewers look for structured thinking, not gut feel. Use phrases like 'the cost of delaying Squad A was higher because...' to signal that thinking clearly.

Three-part structure for 'how do you handle X' questions. First, state your default philosophy. Second, give a concrete example from your past. Third, say what you would adapt in a new context like upGrad. This shows self-awareness and flexibility, both of which upGrad values in EMs.

Constraints-first for system design or technical questions. EMs are not expected to code in the interview, but you should speak fluently about architectural decisions your team has made. Start with the constraints and trade-offs you faced, then your decision rationale, then the outcome.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

upGrad interviewers typically look for four qualities in EM candidates.

Delivery ownership. They want to see that you treat missed deadlines as your own problem, not the team's. Stories where you took personal accountability (not just supervised from a distance) land better than stories where 'we' did everything.

People depth. Growth conversations, performance management, and hiring are expected to be genuine strengths, not afterthoughts. Candidates who treat people processes as overhead rarely progress past the early rounds. Be ready to talk about a real performance conversation you led, including how it resolved.

Product empathy. upGrad is product-led. EMs who can speak the language of user outcomes and product metrics impress more than those who stay purely in engineering. Know what a learner completion rate or activation rate means, and be ready to connect your engineering decisions to those outcomes.

Platform thinking. upGrad's scale means decisions made by one team ripple into others. Candidates report that interviewers probe for awareness of downstream impact, shared infrastructure dependencies, and API contracts. Show that you think beyond your squad's velocity.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Research and story mining.
Read upGrad's public blog and any publicly available engineering posts. Map your past experience to the four qualities described in this guide. Write down eight to ten STAR stories covering: team turnarounds, cross-functional conflict, hiring under pressure, and incident management. Writing them down forces you to be specific and stops you from rambling in the actual interview.

Week 2: Practice.
Do timed mock interviews with a peer or record yourself. For each answer, check three things: Is the Situation under two minutes? Is the Action specific to what *you* did, not what the team did? Is the Result measurable or at least clearly observable? Practice the trade-off prioritization framework using a hypothetical edtech scenario so it feels natural in the room.

Week 3: Alignment and questions.
Prepare three to four questions for your interviewer that show you have done your homework on upGrad's products and engineering scale. Candidates report that asking about team structure, engineering culture, and what 'good' looks like in the first six months signals genuine interest, not just a checkbox. Review the salary bands so you know your range before the offer stage and do not get anchored low.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Talking about the team instead of yourself. 'We shipped X' tells the interviewer nothing about your contribution. Say 'I decided X' and 'I coached Y through Z.' Engineers moving into management for the first time often fall into this trap, and upGrad interviewers are trained to spot it.

Skipping the Result. A STAR answer without a Result is just a story. Even if the outcome was mixed, state what you learned and what changed. Interviewers score you on the full arc, not just the Action portion.

Being vague about conflict. upGrad values directness. Candidates who soften every conflict story to avoid looking bad come across as non-committal. It is fine to say you disagreed with a product decision and explain how you handled it professionally. That is a strength, not a red flag.

Not knowing upGrad's products. Interviewers typically ask how your experience applies to edtech specifically. If you cannot name one upGrad product or describe a learner pain point, you signal that you are treating this as a generic EM application.

Waiting to be asked about weaknesses. Strong EM candidates proactively mention what they are still learning and working on. It builds trust and shows the self-awareness that senior leadership looks for when promoting someone to a management role.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the upGrad EM interview process typically have?

Candidates report the process typically involves several rounds, often covering an initial HR screen, a leadership and behavioral round, a technical or system design discussion, and a final round with senior leadership. The exact structure can vary by team and the level of the role. Confirm the format with your recruiter after the first screen so you can prepare accordingly and avoid surprises.

Does upGrad expect Engineering Managers to still write code?

Candidates report that upGrad EMs are not expected to write production code regularly. However, technical credibility matters and interviewers typically probe your understanding of your team's architectural decisions, your ability to spot technical risk early, and your fluency in system design trade-offs. Stay sharp on the fundamentals even if you are not coding day to day.

How important is edtech domain knowledge for this role?

You do not need prior edtech experience to get the job, but you do need to show genuine curiosity about the domain. Knowing basic product metrics relevant to online learning, such as course completion rates or learner engagement, and being able to connect your engineering decisions to learner outcomes will set you apart from candidates who treat the role as a generic EM position. Research upGrad's core products before any interview round.

What salary range should I expect and how do I negotiate?

Based on knok salary data, Manager-level EM roles in this market typically range from 35-60 LPA, and Senior Manager roles from 55-90 LPA. Candidates report upGrad has some flexibility, especially if you have a competing offer. Know the components you care about (base, variable, stock, joining bonus) before the conversation, and avoid revealing a specific number before the formal offer stage if you can help it.

How does upGrad's acquisition history affect what they look for in EMs?

upGrad has grown through acquisitions, which means EMs often inherit teams from different engineering cultures and tooling backgrounds. Interviewers typically look for experience in team integration, handling ambiguity around process alignment, and bringing together engineers with different working styles. If you have any experience leading or joining a team post-acquisition, prepare a STAR story around it before your interview.
