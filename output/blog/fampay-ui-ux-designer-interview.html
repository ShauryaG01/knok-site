01 Overview

Overview

FamPay is one of India's few neobanks built specifically for teenagers, offering a numberless prepaid card, UPI payments, and a parent-to-child pocket money flow. As a UI/UX Designer here, you shape how young users experience money for the very first time, which makes this role more demanding than a typical fintech design job.

Candidates report the interview process typically runs two to three rounds: a portfolio or introductory call, one or two design rounds (which may include a live brief or a take-home exercise), and a final culture or working-style discussion. The exact structure varies by seniority, so confirm with your recruiter before your first call. FamPay currently has 20 open roles across functions, which suggests the team is in an active growth phase and building out its product organisation.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These are the questions candidates commonly report from FamPay UI/UX Designer interviews. Prepare for each one using your own portfolio work.

  1. Walk us through a project where you designed for a young or first-time user. What specific choices did you make for that audience?
  2. FamPay's users may be opening a financial product for the first time. How do you make money concepts approachable without being patronising?
  3. How do you balance RBI compliance requirements (KYC, consent flows) with a clean, simple UX for young users?
  4. Design a flow that helps a teenager track monthly spending by category. Walk us through your thinking from research to final screen.
  5. Tell us about a time you pushed back on a product requirement because it would hurt the user experience. What happened?
  6. How do you run user research when your core audience is a minor and you need parental consent before speaking to them?
  7. FamPay's card is a core brand moment for young users. How would you think about refreshing the card design without alienating people who already love it?
  8. Describe a design decision you made with limited or no data. What trade-offs did you accept and how did you validate later?
  9. How do you think about readability and accessibility for users who are completely new to financial apps?
  10. How would you measure whether a redesigned onboarding flow is actually better than the current one?
  11. Tell us about a time you had to simplify your ideal design because of a technical constraint. How did you work with the engineering team?
  12. What feature do you think FamPay should build next, and why?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use these three STAR answers as templates. Replace the specifics with your own projects and be as concrete as possible about your actions.

Q: Walk us through a project where you designed for a young or first-time user.

*Situation:* At a previous company I was tasked with redesigning a savings goal feature. The product manager flagged that users were abandoning the goal-setup step at a high rate.

*Task:* My job was to reduce that drop-off by making the flow feel like setting a personal goal, not filling out a banking form.

*Action:* I ran five quick hallway interviews with young adult users, found that terms like 'corpus' and 'maturity value' caused confusion, and rewrote all copy as plain questions: 'what are you saving for?' and 'when do you want the money?' I added a visual progress indicator and a small celebration screen at the end of setup.

*Result:* The redesigned flow went to A/B testing and became the internal benchmark for how we wrote financial copy across other features. I can walk you through the before-and-after screens in my portfolio.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you pushed back on a product requirement.

*Situation:* A product manager wanted to add a promotional banner inside the spending summary screen to upsell a premium tier to existing users.

*Task:* I was asked to design it in, but I felt placing a sales message inside a personal, reflective screen would damage user trust.

*Action:* I pulled session recordings showing that users spent most of their time on that screen reading their own transaction data. I proposed an alternative: surface the upsell only after the user had scrolled to the end of their summary, as a contextual card rather than a banner. I mocked both versions and shared them with the PM and a small group of users for quick feedback.

*Result:* The PM agreed to test my version. The contextual card performed better in testing without disrupting the summary view. The pattern was reused for other upsell placements across the app.

---

Q: Describe a design decision you made with limited data.

*Situation:* We were redesigning the onboarding flow but had no analytics baseline because the previous version had zero instrumentation.

*Task:* I had to recommend a new flow structure without any quantitative evidence to guide the decision.

*Action:* I ran moderated usability tests with a small group of participants, mapped every point where someone paused or asked a question, and used those moments as a proxy for friction. I also reviewed publicly available teardowns of comparable apps. I documented every assumption clearly in the design rationale so engineering and product could challenge anything I had wrong.

*Result:* We shipped the new flow with proper instrumentation from day one. At the three-month review the team had real data and could iterate quickly. My upfront documentation of assumptions also helped when one assumption proved incorrect, because we had already identified it as a risk.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For design walkthroughs (portfolio questions): Open with the problem and who was affected, describe the research you did (even if it was small), explain the key decision you made and why, then finish with what you measured or learned. FamPay interviewers are reportedly more interested in your reasoning than your visual output, so spend more time on 'why' than 'how it looks.'

For hypothetical design briefs: State your assumptions out loud before sketching anything. For FamPay, always define: who is the user (teen, parent, or both?), what device and context, and what does success look like? This shows structured thinking even when your solution is imperfect.

For 'tell me about a time' questions: Use a tight STAR structure. Keep Situation and Task to two or three sentences each so you have room for Action and Result. The Action is where interviewers assess your craft; the Result shows self-awareness.

For 'what should FamPay build next' questions: Anchor your answer in a real user problem, not a feature idea you personally find interesting. Propose a way to validate the idea cheaply before committing to a full build. Interviewers want to see product thinking alongside design thinking.

For compliance or constraint questions: Show that you see compliance as a design material, not a blocker. Explain how you would make a required consent or KYC step feel honest and clear rather than buried or confusing.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates report, FamPay design interviewers are looking for three qualities above everything else.

Genuine empathy for a non-adult audience. FamPay's users are teenagers who may have never managed money before. Interviewers want to see that you have thought about how this audience reads, trusts, and makes decisions differently from adult users. Generic 'user-centred design' language is not enough. Show specific choices you have made for young or first-time users in your past work.

Simple, opinionated thinking. FamPay's product philosophy is extreme simplicity. Candidates who propose dense, feature-heavy solutions or who hedge every design decision tend to get pushed back on. Be willing to say what you would remove or cut, not just what you would add.

Deep craft and a clear point of view. Portfolio reviews are reportedly thorough. Be ready to explain every screen, every copy choice, and every colour decision. If you used a design system, explain where you deviated from it and why.

Collaborative confidence under real constraints. With 20 open roles across functions at FamPay right now, the team is building out fast. Interviewers want people who can work alongside engineers and PMs without needing perfect conditions. Stories where you adapted your design to a real constraint land better than stories where everything went smoothly.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Audit the FamPay app before your first call. Download it, go through onboarding yourself, and note two or three specific things you would change and why. Interviewers frequently ask 'what would you improve?' and a screen-level, reasoned answer is far stronger than a general one.

Prepare three to four portfolio case studies. Pick projects that show range: one for a young or non-expert audience, one involving a real constraint (technical, compliance, or resource-based), and one where you can speak to outcomes or measurement. Prepare a short, crisp walk-through for each.

Practice live design briefs with a peer. Ask someone to give you a short product prompt, then talk through your approach out loud. Focus on structuring your assumptions and defining your user before you put pen to paper. You do not need a polished finished design; you need a clear, audible process.

Read up on the product space. Look for publicly available articles on teen fintech in India and RBI guidelines for prepaid payment instruments. This context will help you frame answers to compliance and audience questions without sounding unprepared.

Prepare strong questions to ask. Strong candidates ask about the design team's current challenges, how design decisions get made, and what the biggest unsolved user problem is today. These questions signal that you are evaluating FamPay as much as they are evaluating you.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Designing for adults, not teenagers. The most common pattern interviewers report is candidates who present adult-focused solutions to FamPay briefs. If you are asked to design a money transfer flow for a young user, your information architecture, your copy tone, and your onboarding assumptions should all reflect that audience specifically.

Skipping the 'why' in portfolio walkthroughs. Showing polished screens without explaining the problem you were solving, the alternatives you rejected, or the outcome you saw will not impress a senior interviewer. Always connect the visual to the decision behind it.

Over-engineering the solution. FamPay's brand is simplicity. A candidate who proposes a complex multi-step flow when a two-screen solution would work is signalling a mismatch with the product philosophy.

Jumping into a brief without asking questions. Starting to sketch before you have defined the user, the context, or the success metric makes your answer look unfocused. Spend the first few minutes of any brief asking the right questions, not drawing.

Treating compliance as an afterthought. For a fintech serving minors, parental consent and KYC requirements are central to the product, not a footnote. If your proposed design ignores these, it signals that you have not engaged with the product context deeply enough.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the FamPay UI/UX Designer interview typically have?

Candidates report two to three rounds in most cases: a portfolio or introductory call, one or two design rounds (which may be a live brief, a whiteboard exercise, or a take-home assignment), and a final discussion on culture and working style. The exact number can vary by seniority level, so confirm with your recruiter after the first call.

Is there a take-home design assignment in the FamPay interview?

Many candidates report receiving a take-home brief at some stage, though some report a live whiteboard session instead. The briefs are typically product-focused and teen-specific, asking you to design or improve a flow within the FamPay ecosystem. Treat any take-home as an opportunity to show your process and your reasoning, not just a finished-looking screen.

What kind of portfolio work impresses FamPay interviewers?

Candidates report that interviewers pay close attention to your reasoning, not just the visual output. Projects where you can clearly explain the user problem, the constraints you worked within, and what you measured or learned after launch tend to land well. At least one project showing design work for a young or non-expert audience is a strong asset for this role specifically.

Do I need fintech experience to be considered for this role?

Fintech experience helps but is not a reported hard requirement. Candidates without it have cleared the process by showing strong empathy for FamPay's audience (teenagers managing money for the first time) and by demonstrating that they have done their homework on the product. Downloading the app and auditing it before the interview is one of the most practical things you can do.

How should I approach the 'what would you improve about FamPay' question?

Pick one specific screen or flow, describe the user problem it creates, and propose a focused improvement with a clear rationale. Avoid sweeping feature requests or vague feedback about 'simplifying the UI.' Interviewers want to see that you can identify a real friction point, think about why it exists, and propose a change grounded in user need rather than personal taste.

FamPay currently has 20 open roles across functions, suggesting an active growth phase.
