01 Overview

Overview

Pango is a cybersecurity and privacy technology company best known for VPN services and identity protection products. As of July 2026, Pango has 12 open roles tracked by knok across all functions, which puts it among the more active hirers for engineering talent right now.

The iOS Engineer role at Pango sits at the intersection of mobile development and security-focused product engineering. You will typically work on features like encrypted tunneling, network traffic management, and privacy dashboards. Interviewers value both strong Swift fundamentals and a genuine appreciation for the security constraints that shape every design decision.

Candidates report the process typically runs three to four rounds: an initial recruiter screening, a technical phone or video round, a coding exercise (take-home or live), and a final panel with senior engineers and sometimes a product manager. Confirm the structure with your recruiter after the first call, as the format varies by team.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions appear frequently in Pango iOS Engineer interviews, based on candidate reports and the nature of Pango's security-focused product line.

  1. Walk me through how you have implemented a VPN or Network Extension in iOS. What challenges did you face?
  2. How does App Transport Security work, and when would you configure an exception for it?
  3. Explain the iOS networking stack from URLSession down to the socket layer. Where would you add security checks?
  4. How do you handle Keychain storage securely? What is the difference between kSecAttrAccessibleWhenUnlocked and kSecAttrAccessibleAfterFirstUnlock?
  5. Describe a time you debugged a memory leak in a Swift application. What tools did you use and what did you find?
  6. How do you keep the UI responsive when running heavy cryptographic operations on device?
  7. Pango products run as background processes and system extensions. How does iOS background execution work, and what are its hard limits?
  8. How would you design an offline-first feature that syncs encrypted user data when connectivity returns?
  9. How do you write testable Swift code for networking and Keychain layers without hitting real services?
  10. How do you implement certificate pinning in iOS, and how would you handle a certificate rotation without forcing an app update?
  11. How would you structure a modular iOS app so that the VPN module ships as a separate Network Extension target?
  12. How do you stay current with Apple's evolving privacy APIs such as App Tracking Transparency and PrivacyInfo.xcprivacy, and how have you applied them in a shipped product?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format for every behavioural question: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Here are three worked examples.

Q: Tell me about a time you improved app performance on iOS.

*Situation:* At my previous company we had a VPN client whose main screen took several seconds to load because it was decrypting and rendering a full connection log on the main thread.

*Task:* I was asked to bring the load time under one second without changing the visual design.

*Action:* I profiled the app with Instruments and confirmed the decryption loop was blocking the main thread. I moved the work to a background serial queue, introduced pagination so only the most recent entries were decrypted on first load, and cached results in memory using NSCache.

*Result:* Load time dropped significantly in internal benchmarks. The fix shipped in the next release and the team saw a publicly reported improvement in App Store review sentiment around performance.

---

Q: Describe a situation where you had to learn a new Apple framework quickly to meet a deadline.

*Situation:* Apple announced NetworkExtension updates at WWDC that our product manager wanted us to adopt within six weeks for a per-app VPN feature a large enterprise client had requested.

*Task:* I was the only engineer on the team who had not worked with NEAppProxyProvider before, but I was assigned as the lead.

*Action:* I spent the first week reading Apple's documentation end to end, then built a throwaway prototype to understand the packet flow. I set up check-ins with a senior engineer who had used a related extension type, watched relevant WWDC sessions, and wrote notes that became internal documentation.

*Result:* We shipped within the deadline. The documentation I wrote was later used to onboard two new iOS engineers, which my team lead noted in my performance review.

---

Q: Give me an example of a time you caught a security issue in your own code before it reached production.

*Situation:* I was building a feature that synced user credentials to iCloud Keychain for a cross-device login flow.

*Task:* The feature had passed code review and was ready to merge, but something felt off about the Keychain accessibility flags I had set.

*Action:* I paused the merge and re-read Apple's Keychain accessibility documentation. I found I had used kSecAttrAccessibleAlways, which exposes the item even when the device is locked. I changed it to kSecAttrAccessibleWhenUnlocked, added a comment explaining the choice, and wrote a unit test asserting the correct flag.

*Result:* The security team confirmed in their next review that the original code would have failed a compliance check. Catching it early avoided a remediation cycle the team estimated would have cost real time.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For technical 'how does X work' questions, use a three-layer structure: start with the concept in one sentence, then explain the iOS-specific implementation details, then tie it to a real example from your work. This shows you understand theory and have applied it, which matters at a company where the product depends on getting these details right.

For system design questions, think out loud in four phases: requirements (what does this feature need to do, and what are the security constraints?), component breakdown (which Apple frameworks and custom layers are involved?), data flow (how does data move and where is it encrypted or protected?), and trade-offs (what did you choose and why?). Pango interviewers particularly care about the security constraint phase, so do not skip it.

For behavioural questions, keep Situation and Task brief, spend most of your time on Action (your specific decisions), and always end with a concrete Result. Vague outcomes like 'it went well' are weak. Prefer measurable or observable changes that were visible to stakeholders.

For 'why Pango' questions, connect your answer to a specific product area. Candidates who can name a Pango product, describe how it works technically, and explain what engineering challenge within it excites them stand out from generic 'I care about privacy' answers. Review Pango's App Store listings and any recent blog posts or press before your interview.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Deep Swift and Apple framework knowledge, not just syntax familiarity. Pango's products depend on low-level iOS capabilities: Network Extensions, background modes, Keychain, and on-device cryptography. Interviewers want to see that you have worked inside these APIs, not just consumed high-level wrappers.

Security mindset as a default, not an afterthought. Because Pango builds privacy and security products, engineers are expected to think about threat models, data at rest versus data in transit, and principle of least privilege without being prompted. Bring this framing to every technical answer unprompted.

Ownership and early communication. Candidates report that Pango values engineers who can take a feature from design to production independently and who surface blockers early. In behavioural rounds, show evidence of both: times you drove a decision and times you flagged a risk before it became a problem.

Pragmatism about Apple's platform constraints. iOS imposes real limits on background execution, memory, and third-party access to system resources. Interviewers want engineers who know these limits and design around them, not engineers who fight the platform or assume Android patterns will translate directly.

Genuine curiosity about the product space. Showing that you use security products, follow relevant vulnerability disclosures, or have opinions about privacy regulation signals that you will care about the work beyond the ticket.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Core Swift and iOS internals.
Revise memory management (ARC, retain cycles, weak and unowned references), concurrency (Swift Concurrency with async/await and actors, plus legacy GCD patterns), and the URLSession stack. Write small code samples from memory rather than just reading documentation.

Week 2: Security and networking deep dive.
Read Apple's documentation on Network Extension, NEVPNManager, and NEAppProxyProvider. Understand the Keychain Services API thoroughly. Review certificate pinning approaches and their trade-offs. Read Apple's App Transport Security technical note end to end.

Week 3: System design practice.
Practice designing iOS features that involve background sync, encrypted storage, or network proxying. Do this out loud or write it down. Focus on where security boundaries sit in your design and what constraints iOS places on each component.

Week 4: Behavioural prep and mock interviews.
Write out five to eight STAR stories from your career covering a technical challenge, a disagreement with a colleague, a feature you owned end to end, and a mistake you made and recovered from. Practice saying them aloud so they sound natural, not rehearsed.

Before each round, review Pango's App Store page and any recent press so you can reference the actual product in your answers. 07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating security as a separate concern. Candidates who answer a design question and then add 'and of course we would layer security on top' signal that they see security as an add-on. At Pango it is the core of every product decision. Build it into your answers from the start.

Shallow knowledge of Network Extension. Many iOS engineers have used URLSession but have never built a Network or VPN extension. If this is you, spend time with Apple's documentation and sample code before the interview. Pretending familiarity and stumbling on a follow-up is worse than honestly describing what you have read but not yet shipped.

Vague STAR answers. Saying 'I improved performance' without a before/after outcome, or 'the team was happy' without describing what changed, makes your answer forgettable. Use numbers where you have them, and use specific observable outcomes where you do not.

Not asking clarifying questions in system design. Jumping straight into an architecture without asking about scale, security requirements, or offline behaviour signals that you skip requirements gathering in real work. Ask one or two focused questions before you start designing.

Ignoring iOS background execution limits. A common mistake in take-home exercises is designing a sync or monitoring feature that assumes the app can run freely in the background. Show you know iOS will suspend or terminate background tasks and design with those constraints in mind.
