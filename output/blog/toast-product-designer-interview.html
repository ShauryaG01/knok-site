02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These are the questions candidates report most often in Toast Product Designer interviews. Because Toast's product is deeply operational (restaurant staff depend on it during live service), expect questions that probe your ability to design for real-world constraints, not just visual polish.

  1. Walk us through a product you designed end-to-end. What was the problem, how did you frame it, and how did you validate your solution?
  2. Toast's users are often restaurant staff under time pressure during a lunch or dinner rush. How do you design for high-stress, time-sensitive workflows?
  3. Describe a time you simplified a complex feature without losing core functionality. What trade-offs did you make and why?
  4. How do you design for users with varying levels of digital literacy? Give a specific example.
  5. Tell us about a project where you worked closely with engineers. How did you handle technical constraints that affected your design?
  6. How do you balance business goals with user needs when the two conflict? Walk us through a real situation.
  7. Describe your approach to user research. How do you turn research insights into concrete design decisions?
  8. Toast is used in high-stakes operational environments. How do you design for error prevention and recovery?
  9. Tell us about a time you received critical feedback on your design from a stakeholder or teammate. How did you respond?
  10. How do you prioritise improvements in a product that serves many different user types with different needs?
  11. Describe a situation where data or user feedback caused you to change your design direction mid-project.
  12. How do you present and defend design decisions to stakeholders who do not have a design background?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use these as a starting point. Adapt them to your actual experience, and always end with a result you can speak to confidently.

---

Q: Walk us through a product you designed end-to-end.

*Situation:* Our restaurant-facing mobile app had a high drop-off rate on the table-management screen. Staff were skipping it and reverting to paper.

*Task:* I was asked to redesign the screen to reduce friction and increase adoption among floor staff.

*Action:* I ran several contextual interviews during service hours to observe how staff actually used the screen under pressure. The colour-coding system was not visible in dim restaurant lighting, and tap targets were too small for staff wearing gloves. I redesigned with high-contrast status indicators and larger interactive areas, then tested a prototype with a small group of staff members before the final handoff.

*Result:* After launch, the team tracked a meaningful increase in daily active use within the first month, and the operations manager reported fewer seating errors during peak hours.

---

Q: How do you handle technical constraints that affect your design?

*Situation:* During a project to redesign the order-editing flow, the engineering team flagged that real-time sync between the kitchen display and the POS was not feasible within the project timeline.

*Task:* I had to redesign the flow to work within that constraint without creating confusion for restaurant staff.

*Action:* I held a working session with the lead engineer to understand exactly what was and was not possible. We identified a middle path: a brief 'pending' state that made the delay visible to staff rather than hiding it. I updated the designs to include this state, wrote clear micro-copy to explain what was happening, and added a lightweight confirmation step.

*Result:* The feature shipped on time. In post-launch feedback sessions, staff said the 'pending' indicator actually built trust because they could see the system working, rather than wondering if their edit had registered.

---

Q: Describe a time you received critical feedback on your design.

*Situation:* I presented a redesigned onboarding flow for new restaurant managers and the product lead said it felt 'too consumer-app' and did not match how restaurant managers actually think about setting up a new location.

*Task:* I needed to rethink the structure without losing the usability improvements I had built in.

*Action:* Instead of defending the original design, I asked the product lead to walk me through how a manager typically sets up a new location step by step. I also did a couple of quick calls with restaurant managers. The original flow was organised by feature area. Managers actually think in terms of operational milestones (staff ready, menu ready, payments ready). I restructured the flow to mirror that mental model.

*Result:* The revised design scored higher in a preference test with a small group of managers, and the product lead approved it for development without further changes.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

The STAR format is the most reliable structure for Toast's behavioural questions. Situation and Task together should be brief. Action should be the largest part, with specific design decisions explained. Result should be concrete, even if you cannot share exact metrics.

For portfolio walkthroughs, use a problem-first structure: start with the user problem and the business context, then move to your process (research, ideation, iteration), then show the final design, then talk about outcome and what you would do differently. Avoid leading with visuals. Toast interviewers care more about your thinking than UI polish.

For design exercise rounds (which candidates report are common at Toast), think out loud from the start. State your assumptions, ask clarifying questions before jumping in, and structure your solution around user goals before adding features. Toast's product serves restaurant operators, so grounding your exercise in operational realism (what happens when the internet drops, what does a stressed staff member do on a busy Friday evening) will stand out.

For end-to-end design questions, use a problem-process-outcome structure: define the problem space, walk through your design rationale including what you ruled out and why, and close with how you would measure success. This shows you think beyond the screen.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates report and Toast's publicly visible design principles, the interview panel typically looks for a few specific qualities.

Deep empathy for operational users. Toast's customers are not browsing for fun. They are restaurant workers whose livelihoods depend on the tool working correctly under pressure. Interviewers want to see that you have spent time with users in their real context, not just in a usability lab.

Clear design reasoning. Every decision in your portfolio walkthrough should have a 'why' behind it. Saying 'I chose this layout because it felt cleaner' will not land. Saying 'I chose this layout because staff are scanning quickly and need to find status at a glance' shows you are solving a real problem.

Comfort with constraints. Toast operates in a domain with technical and operational constraints (offline mode, hardware limitations, varying network quality). Candidates who treat constraints as interesting design challenges rather than blockers tend to score well.

Cross-functional fluency. Design at Toast is collaborative. Interviewers will probe how you work with engineers, product managers, and researchers. Show that you can advocate for users without being precious about your designs.

Ownership and iteration. Toast interviewers reportedly favour candidates who can describe not just what they shipped, but what happened after and what they would change. A good 'what I learned' moment in a case study often matters as much as the final design.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Know the product. Sign up for a Toast demo or watch publicly available product walkthroughs. Understand the core screens: POS, kitchen display, online ordering, and reporting. The more specific your references to actual Toast workflows, the more credible you will sound.

Step 2: Prepare a few strong case studies. Each should follow the problem-first structure described in the Answer Frameworks section. At least one should show a project where you changed direction based on research or feedback.

Step 3: Practice under time pressure. Candidates report that Toast design exercises are timed. Set a timer and practice talking through a design problem from scratch, narrating your thinking as you go.

Step 4: Prepare for operational design questions. Think through how you would design for error states, low connectivity, and users who are not tech-savvy. Restaurant environments are physically demanding and digitally varied.

Step 5: Research the team. Look at the LinkedIn profiles of Toast designers in India. Note any open writing or talks. This gives you specific context to reference when you ask your own questions at the end of each round.

Step 6: Prepare your own questions. Ask about how design decisions are made, how the India team collaborates with the US team, and what success looks like for a new designer in the first few months. Avoid questions whose answers are easily found on the company website.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Showing polish over process. Toast interviewers are not looking for the most beautiful portfolio. They want to see how you think. Candidates who lead with Figma screens and spend little time on research and rationale tend to struggle.

Ignoring the operational context. Generic answers about 'user-centred design' without grounding them in restaurant operations miss the point. Reference the specific environment: shift workers, shared devices, loud kitchens, time pressure.

Being too precious about design decisions. If an interviewer pushes back on a choice you made, the right response is curiosity, not defence. Acknowledge the trade-off, ask a clarifying question, and show you can iterate.

Vague results. Saying 'users were happier' or 'the product improved' is weak. Even if you cannot share exact numbers, say something like 'support tickets for that flow dropped noticeably in the first quarter' or 'the ops team flagged it as one of the top improvements in their quarterly review'.

Under-preparing for the design exercise. Candidates sometimes treat the exercise as a chance to show off creativity. Toast typically wants structured thinking: define the user, define the problem, explore options, make a decision, and explain your reasoning. Clarity and structure matter more than novelty.

Not asking good questions. Ending an interview with 'I think you covered everything' is a missed opportunity. Thoughtful questions signal genuine interest and give the panel one more data point on how you think.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Toast Product Designer interview typically have?

Candidates report that the process typically includes a recruiter screen, a portfolio review with the design team, a design exercise or take-home challenge, and one or more cross-functional rounds with product managers or engineers. The exact number of rounds can vary by team and level. It is worth asking your recruiter for the current structure when you schedule your first call.

Does Toast give a take-home design exercise or an on-the-spot exercise?

Both formats have been reported by candidates. Some describe a timed on-the-spot whiteboard-style exercise, others describe a take-home prompt with a short window to complete it. The format may depend on the specific team or role level. Prepare for both by practising structured problem-framing and verbal explanation of your decisions.

How important is domain knowledge about restaurants for a Toast design interview?

Candidates who demonstrate genuine familiarity with restaurant operations tend to stand out. You do not need prior restaurant-industry work experience, but you should understand the core workflows: taking orders, managing tables, handling payments, and running a kitchen display. Spending time with the Toast product before your interview and being able to speak to specific operational challenges will make your answers more credible.

What is the best way to present my portfolio at Toast?

Lead with the problem and the user, not with visuals. Interviewers want to understand what you were solving, how you validated your understanding of the problem, and what trade-offs you made along the way. Pick a few strong case studies rather than showing every project you have worked on. End each case study with what you learned or what you would do differently, as Toast interviewers reportedly value self-awareness and a willingness to iterate.
