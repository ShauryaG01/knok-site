01 Overview

Overview

HBK (Hottinger Brüel & Kjær) builds precision test and measurement software used across automotive, aerospace, and industrial engineering. Their software teams work on data acquisition systems, signal processing pipelines, and real-time analysis tools. Candidates report a structured interview process that typically covers data structures, algorithms, system design, and topics relevant to engineering software such as real-time processing and multithreading.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions are commonly reported by candidates who have interviewed for Software Engineer roles at companies in the test, measurement, and engineering software space, including HBK:

  1. Walk me through a complex software system you built from scratch. What were the biggest design decisions you made?
  2. How do you handle real-time data streams with high throughput and low latency requirements?
  3. Explain the difference between multi-threading and multi-processing. When would you choose one over the other?
  4. How would you design a data acquisition system that captures sensor data from many channels simultaneously?
  5. Describe a bug that was very difficult to reproduce. How did you eventually track it down?
  6. How do you ensure software reliability in a safety-critical or instrumentation context?
  7. What is your experience with signal processing or digital filtering? Can you walk through a practical example?
  8. How have you approached performance profiling and optimization in a previous project?
  9. Tell me about a time you had to learn a new technology quickly to meet a project deadline.
  10. How do you manage technical debt while still delivering features on schedule?
  11. Describe a situation where you disagreed with a technical decision made by your team. How did you handle it?
  12. How do you approach writing code that other engineers, including non-software engineers, can maintain and understand?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a complex software system you built from scratch.

*Situation:* My team needed a new data logging service to replace a legacy system that kept crashing under heavy load.

*Task:* I was asked to lead the design and implementation of a replacement that could handle sustained high-volume writes without data loss.

*Action:* I broke the problem into an ingestion layer, a buffering queue, and a persistence layer. I chose an event-driven architecture, wrote detailed design docs for team review, and set up integration tests before writing any production code. I also coordinated with QA to define acceptance criteria upfront.

*Result:* The new service handled significantly higher peak load in staging tests, reduced data loss incidents to zero in the quarter after launch, and became the team standard for new logging work.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you had to learn a new technology quickly.

*Situation:* A client project required integrating with a proprietary hardware SDK I had never worked with. The timeline was tight.

*Task:* I needed to get productive with the SDK within a week and deliver a working prototype.

*Action:* I blocked two full days purely for reading documentation and running sample programs. I identified senior engineers who had used similar SDKs and set up short calls with each. I built a minimal spike solution to validate my assumptions before writing any production code.

*Result:* The prototype was ready by day six, the client demo went smoothly, and I documented everything so two other teammates could onboard in under a day.

---

Q: Describe a situation where you disagreed with a technical decision.

*Situation:* My team wanted to adopt a new framework for a module I felt was already stable and well-understood.

*Task:* I needed to either make a convincing case or accept the decision and move forward constructively.

*Action:* I prepared a short written comparison covering migration cost, learning curve, and actual benefits for our use case. I shared it before the meeting so everyone had time to read it, then listened carefully to the counterarguments.

*Result:* The team agreed to keep the existing framework for the stable module but adopted the new one for greenfield work. The outcome felt fair, and the process strengthened how we handle technical disagreements.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the go-to framework for behavioural questions. Keep each part concise: one to two sentences on Situation and Task, most of your time on Action, and a clear measurable or observable Result.

Problem Decomposition works well for system design and open-ended technical questions. State your assumptions first, break the problem into components (input, processing, output, failure modes), then build your solution layer by layer. HBK candidates report that interviewers appreciate structured thinking over jumping straight to code.

Clarify before coding. For algorithmic questions, spend the first minute asking clarifying questions about input size, edge cases, and whether an approximate solution is acceptable. This shows engineering maturity and prevents wasted effort.

Think aloud. HBK interviewers, like most technical interviewers, want to follow your reasoning. Narrate your thought process as you work. Saying 'I am considering a hash map here because lookups would be O(1)' is more valuable than silent coding followed by a correct answer.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates report from HBK and similar engineering software companies, interviewers typically look for a few key qualities.

Technical depth without overengineering. HBK builds software that runs on real hardware in demanding environments. They want engineers who write clean, correct, maintainable code, not engineers who reach for the most complex solution available.

Domain curiosity. You do not need a background in measurement hardware to get hired, but candidates report that showing genuine interest in the problem domain (sensors, signal processing, real-time systems) makes a strong impression. Reading about what HBK products actually do before your interview is worth the time.

Communication and collaboration. HBK teams are cross-functional, often including hardware engineers, test engineers, and software engineers working on the same product. Interviewers look for candidates who can explain technical decisions clearly to non-software colleagues.

Ownership mindset. Candidates who can describe not just what they built but why they made specific decisions, and how they handled things when those decisions turned out to be wrong, consistently receive positive feedback.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Coding fundamentals. Practise arrays, hash maps, trees, graphs, and sorting algorithms. Focus on writing clean, readable code under time pressure. Aim for several problems each day on a platform you already use.

Week 2: System design and HBK-specific prep. Study distributed systems basics: queues, databases, caching, and API design. Then spend time on topics relevant to HBK, such as real-time data processing, multithreading, and serial or parallel communication patterns. Read publicly available material on measurement software architectures.

Week 3: Behavioural stories and mock interviews. Write out several STAR stories covering projects, mistakes, conflicts, and learnings. Record yourself answering common behavioural questions and watch it back. Do at least two mock technical interviews with a peer or an online platform.

Before each round: Research HBK's current products, read recent news, and prepare two or three specific questions to ask your interviewer. Questions about the team's current technical challenges or the tools they use show genuine interest.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping to code without clarifying. Many candidates start typing the moment a coding question is read out. Take a minute to confirm constraints, edge cases, and expected output format. Interviewers notice and appreciate this.

Generic behavioural answers. Saying 'I am a good team player' without a specific story adds no value. Every behavioural answer should be a concrete example with a clear outcome.

Ignoring domain context. HBK is not a general software product company. Candidates who treat it like one miss an opportunity. Show that you understand the environment your software will run in.

Overcomplicating system design. Some candidates propose microservices and distributed caches for problems that fit on a single machine. Match your design to the stated requirements, and add complexity only when justified.

Not asking questions at the end. Interviewers often note when candidates have no questions. Prepare thoughtful questions about the team, the product roadmap, or the biggest technical challenges the team is currently facing.

Talking over feedback. If an interviewer hints that your approach has a problem, stop and listen. Candidates who push through corrections without engaging the feedback come across as difficult to work with.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the HBK Software Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report that HBK's process typically involves three to four rounds: an initial screening (phone or online coding test), one or two technical rounds covering algorithms and system design, and a final round with a hiring manager or HR. The exact structure can vary by team and location, so confirm with your recruiter after you apply.

What coding languages does HBK prefer?

Candidates report that C++ and Python are the most commonly used languages in HBK's engineering software teams, which aligns with the company's focus on performance-sensitive instrumentation software. Most interviewers allow you to code in a language you are comfortable with during the interview. Check the job description for specific language requirements before your round.

Is domain knowledge in measurement or signal processing required?

Not strictly required, but it helps. Candidates report that interviewers appreciate it when you show curiosity about measurement systems, sensors, and real-time data. You do not need a background in electrical engineering, but spending a few hours reading about how data acquisition systems work before your interview is a good investment.

What salary can I expect as a Software Engineer at HBK in India?

Based on knok jobradar data for Software Engineers in India, mid-level roles (3-5 years) typically range from 15-25 LPA, and senior roles (6-9 years) from 28-45 LPA. For HBK specifically, publicly reported compensation data is limited, so treat these as market benchmarks rather than confirmed HBK figures. Check Glassdoor and levels.fyi for community-reported numbers.

How long does the HBK hiring process take from application to offer?

Candidates report timelines ranging from two to six weeks from first contact to offer, depending on team availability and the number of rounds. Delays typically happen between rounds rather than within them. If you have not heard back within a week of completing a round, a polite follow-up to your recruiter is appropriate.

Should I prepare for system design even for junior roles at HBK?

For entry-level and early mid-level roles, candidates report that system design questions are lighter, often framed as 'how would you structure this module' rather than large-scale distributed systems design. That said, being able to explain your design decisions clearly and handle basic questions about scalability and reliability is useful at any level.
