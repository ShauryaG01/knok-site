03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How do you handle an underperforming engineer?

*Situation:* At my previous company, a senior engineer on my team was consistently missing sprint commitments. The rest of the team was absorbing the slack and morale was starting to dip.

*Task:* I needed to address the gap honestly without losing someone who had deep domain knowledge and long-term potential.

*Action:* I set up a private one-on-one to understand what was causing the slip. It turned out the engineer was getting unclear requirements late in the sprint, which made estimation nearly impossible. I worked with the product owner to improve how we groomed tickets, paired the engineer with a peer for the next sprint, and agreed on clear weekly check-ins to track progress.

*Result:* Delivery improved noticeably within a few sprints. The engineer became one of the more dependable contributors on the team and later mentored a junior joiner.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you had to recover a project that was going off track.

*Situation:* Midway through a cloud migration engagement, we discovered that the client's legacy systems had undocumented dependencies that were blocking our original timeline.

*Task:* I was accountable for delivery and needed to reset expectations while keeping the client confident in our team.

*Action:* I called an immediate working session with my team to map out the full dependency picture. We broke the migration into smaller independent modules so we could ship value incrementally. I then renegotiated the plan with the client, presenting a phased approach with clear milestones rather than one big go-live at the end.

*Result:* The client appreciated the transparency. We completed the first phase on the revised timeline, which rebuilt trust and opened the door to expanded scope in the next phase.

---

Q: How do you balance technical debt against feature delivery?

*Situation:* Our product team was under pressure to ship a new integration, but the codebase had accumulated debt that was slowing down every sprint.

*Task:* I needed to create space for debt reduction without pausing feature delivery or losing stakeholder confidence.

*Action:* I introduced a simple team norm: every feature pull request had to leave the code a little cleaner than it found it. I also negotiated a fixed capacity allocation each sprint for debt items and made debt visible in our sprint reviews so stakeholders could see the impact directly.

*Result:* Over several sprints, code review cycle times dropped and the team reported fewer production incidents. Stakeholders could see the improvement in delivery velocity and quality.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the most effective structure for behavioural questions at Accion Labs interviews. Keep your Situation and Task brief, spend most of your time on the Action (what you specifically did, not what the team did), and close with a concrete Result. Vague results like 'things improved' are weak. Use specifics from your real experience.

The Consulting Lens. Because Accion Labs is a services firm, many questions test your ability to manage client relationships alongside team management. Whenever a question involves a delivery challenge, always address the client communication angle, not just the internal team actions.

The People-First Frame. For people management questions, interviewers typically want to see empathy before action. Lead with 'I tried to understand the root cause first' before jumping to 'I put them on a performance plan.'

The Trade-off Frame. Engineering Manager questions at this level often have no perfect answer. Show you can hold two competing priorities at once (speed vs. quality, client pressure vs. team wellbeing) and explain how you make a reasoned call rather than defaulting to one extreme.

MECE for prioritisation questions. When asked how you handle conflicting stakeholder demands, structure your answer around impact, urgency, and dependencies. Show you have a repeatable method, not just instinct.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates report for EM roles at digital engineering firms like Accion Labs, interviewers are typically checking for four things.

Client ownership mindset. Accion Labs sells outcomes to clients. An EM who sees themselves only as an internal people manager will not fit. They want someone who treats client success as their own responsibility.

Concrete people management experience. Talking about team management in theory will not land. Interviewers want to hear how you ran one-on-ones, handled underperformance, coached engineers toward promotion, and resolved team conflict. Be specific about what you did personally.

Technical credibility without micromanagement. You do not need to write code every day, but you need to be able to challenge an architecture decision, review a design doc, or spot when a technical estimate is padded. Show you can engage at that level without taking over from your individual contributors.

Delivery accountability. Consulting firms track utilisation and margin closely. Interviewers want confidence that you will own a delivery commitment end to end, escalate early when things slip, and not wait for problems to surface on their own.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week one: Know the company. Read Accion Labs' recent case studies and practice areas (digital engineering, cloud, data). Understand their client profile. Most of their business is in building products for US and European firms. Frame your past experience in terms of outcomes delivered for external clients, not just internal stakeholders.

Week two: Build your story bank. Write out five to seven concrete stories from your career using the STAR format. Cover: a delivery recovery, a team conflict, a hiring decision, a technical trade-off, and a case where you pushed back on a stakeholder. Each story should have a measurable or observable result.

Week three: Practice out loud. Rehearse your answers aloud, not just in your head. Record yourself and listen back for filler words and vague language. Ask a peer to give you one hard follow-up after each answer.

Before the interview: Review the job description carefully and map each requirement to a story from your bank. Prepare two or three thoughtful questions about the team's current challenges, the client portfolio, and how engineering managers are measured in their first few months.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating it like a pure technical interview. Candidates sometimes over-prepare on system design and under-prepare on people management stories. At the EM level, your leadership judgment matters more than whether you can design a distributed cache from scratch.

Being vague about results. Saying 'the team improved' or 'the client was happy' tells the interviewer nothing. Even if you do not have exact metrics, describe what changed: delivery cadence improved, attrition dropped, the client renewed the engagement.

Answering for the team instead of yourself. 'We decided to...' and 'the team did...' are red flags. Interviewers want to know what you specifically chose, initiated, or influenced. Use 'I' deliberately.

Skipping the client angle. In a consulting firm, every delivery story has a client dimension. Candidates who only talk about internal team dynamics miss what Accion Labs actually cares about.

Not preparing questions. Asking nothing at the end signals low interest. One or two good questions about the team's current delivery challenges or how success is measured in the first few months show genuine intent.
