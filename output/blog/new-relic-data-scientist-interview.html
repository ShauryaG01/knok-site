01 Overview

Overview

New Relic is an observability platform company, and its Data Scientist roles sit at the intersection of large-scale telemetry analytics and product intelligence. The team works with petabyte-scale data from metrics, logs, and distributed traces to build models that reduce alert noise, detect anomalies, and help customers understand their software health.

As of mid-2026, knok jobradar tracks 937 Data Scientist openings across India. New Relic currently has 72 open roles in its India teams. Bangalore leads the broader market with 166 openings, followed by Delhi (46), Hyderabad (27), Pune (18), Mumbai (17), and Chennai (8).

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly in candidate accounts of Data Scientist interviews at New Relic. The focus is on observability, scale, and connecting technical work to product value.

  1. How would you design an anomaly detection system for time-series application metrics at scale?
  2. Describe a project where you worked with high-cardinality or high-volume data. What tradeoffs did you make?
  3. How do you detect and handle concept drift in a production model?
  4. What is your experience with streaming or near-real-time data pipelines? Which tools have you used?
  5. New Relic's core product serves SRE and DevOps teams. How would you use ML to reduce alert fatigue without missing genuine incidents?
  6. How would you measure whether an anomaly detection model is actually useful, beyond offline accuracy metrics?
  7. Walk me through a time you had to explain a complex model result to a non-technical audience. What did you simplify and why?
  8. How do you approach feature engineering when your raw input is unstructured log text?
  9. What is the practical difference between a causal model and a predictive model? When would each be the right choice?
  10. How would you build a churn prediction model for a SaaS observability product? Walk through your full approach.
  11. Describe your A/B testing experience. How do you decide when you have enough data to call a result?
  12. How do you ensure ML experiment reproducibility and model versioning in a team setting?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How would you design an anomaly detection system for time-series application metrics?

*Situation:* At my previous company, our monitoring dashboards generated a very high volume of alerts each day. Engineers were ignoring most of them because of false positives.

*Task:* I was asked to reduce alert noise by building a smarter detection layer on top of our existing metrics pipeline.

*Action:* I first segmented metrics by seasonality patterns, because CPU spikes on a Monday morning are expected, but the same spike at midnight is not. I used a seasonal decomposition approach to isolate residuals, then applied a threshold on the residual distribution rather than the raw value. For each metric, I estimated a rolling baseline using recent history and flagged points that deviated beyond a statistically meaningful bound. I also added a suppression layer: if multiple correlated metrics spiked together, we grouped them into a single alert rather than firing separately.

*Result:* Alert volume dropped significantly, and the engineering team reported higher trust in the remaining alerts. The SRE lead adopted it across multiple product lines.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you communicated a complex model to a non-technical stakeholder.

*Situation:* I built a customer health score model that the sales team wanted to use for upsell prioritisation. The model used many behavioral features and a gradient-boosted classifier.

*Task:* The VP of Sales needed to understand why certain customers were flagged as low health, so she could coach her team on how to act on the output.

*Action:* I dropped the technical explanation entirely. Instead, I created a one-page summary that said: 'this customer has not logged in recently, has opened fewer dashboards than similar accounts, and submitted a support ticket last month.' I mapped each model signal to a plain business observation. I also built a simple view in the dashboard showing the top reasons a customer's score dropped, in plain language.

*Result:* The sales team adopted the model within weeks. The VP later said it was the first data science output she had actually used in a customer conversation.

---

Q: How would you handle concept drift in a production ML model?

*Situation:* A fraud detection model I maintained started showing declining precision over several months after deployment.

*Task:* I needed to identify whether it was concept drift, data pipeline issues, or a genuine change in fraud patterns, and then fix it.

*Action:* I first ruled out pipeline issues by checking feature distributions against a baseline snapshot. I confirmed that the input data looked correct but the model's confidence scores on positive cases had shifted. I set up a monitoring dashboard that tracked prediction distribution and label drift separately. Once I confirmed concept drift, I retrained the model on a sliding window of recent labeled data, added a validation gate that checked performance before any model was promoted to production, and scheduled a monthly review cadence.

*Result:* Precision recovered to its original level within a single release cycle. The monitoring setup caught further drift events in the following months without manual intervention.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For technical system-design questions (anomaly detection, pipelines, model architecture), candidates report that New Relic interviewers respond well to a structured walkthrough: start with problem framing (what does 'good' look like for an end user?), then data (what do you have and what is its shape?), then modeling choices (what approach and why?), then evaluation (how do you know it works in production?), and finally deployment and monitoring.

For behavioral questions, use the STAR structure: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep the Situation brief. Spend most of your time on Action (what you specifically did, not what the team did) and make the Result concrete. If you do not have a number, describe the qualitative outcome clearly.

For product-thinking questions (such as 'how would you improve our alerting?'), show that you understand the end user first. An SRE using New Relic cares about signal-to-noise ratio, root cause clarity, and time to resolution. Ground your answer in that user context before jumping to a modeling approach.

For statistics or ML theory questions, give the definition, then immediately give a practical example from your own work. Abstract definitions without real examples tend to feel thin in this type of interview.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Candidates who have gone through New Relic's Data Scientist interview typically describe interviewers as focused on a few core qualities.

Depth in time-series and streaming data. New Relic's product is built on continuous telemetry. Comfort with seasonality, autocorrelation, and real-time pipeline concepts signals that you can hit the ground running.

Product sense. Interviewers often ask how you would measure whether a model is useful, not just accurate. They want to see that you think about the end user (an SRE or developer) and can connect model outputs to decisions those users actually make.

Communication clarity. Multiple candidates report a question about explaining a technical result to a non-technical audience. Prepare a specific, recent example before your interview.

Solid SQL and data manipulation fundamentals. Even at senior levels, candidates report at least a practical data problem. Be ready to write clean queries and reason about performance on large tables.

Intellectual honesty. If you do not know something, interviewers respond better to 'I have not worked with that directly, but here is how I would approach learning it' than to a bluffed answer.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Early in your prep: get the domain context right. Read New Relic's engineering blog and product documentation to understand what observability means in practice. Understand the difference between metrics, logs, and traces. This shapes how you talk about your past work in the context of what they actually build.

First week: shore up your ML and stats foundations. Review anomaly detection techniques (statistical methods, isolation forests, autoencoders), time-series concepts (seasonality, stationarity, decomposition), and evaluation metrics beyond accuracy (precision, recall, F1, AUC). Practice explaining these out loud, not just recalling definitions.

Second week: practice SQL and data problems. Work through window functions, aggregations, and query optimisation on large datasets. Candidates report that SQL problems at New Relic tend to be practical, involving grouped time-series calculations or cohort analysis.

Third week: prepare your STAR stories. Write out answers to the behavioral questions above. Cover a story on handling ambiguity, another on cross-functional communication, and another on a model that did not work as expected and what you did about it.

In the days before your interview: do a mock run of a system-design question from end to end, out loud. Tighten your answers so that each one opens with the business context before the technical detail.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping the 'why' before the 'how'. Candidates often jump straight into model architecture without first stating what problem they are solving and how they will know if it worked. Interviewers at product companies like New Relic want to see business grounding first.

Treating offline metrics as the finish line. Saying 'I achieved high accuracy' without addressing what happens in production, how you monitor for drift, or how the model's output gets used in a real workflow signals a gap in production ML experience.

Weak SQL. Data Scientist roles here involve hands-on data work. Candidates who rely entirely on Python and have not practiced SQL recently often stumble on the practical data problem.

Generic behavioral answers. Saying 'our team built a model' instead of 'I specifically did X' makes it hard for interviewers to assess your individual contribution. Use 'I' throughout your STAR answers.

Not asking clarifying questions on open-ended problems. When given a vague prompt like 'how would you improve our alerting?', launching straight into an answer without asking about constraints, user personas, or success metrics tends to produce a scattered response. A brief scoping conversation shows maturity.

Underselling domain knowledge. If you have worked with time-series, log data, or observability tooling in any context, make that explicit early. It is directly relevant to what New Relic builds.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the New Relic Data Scientist interview typically have?

Candidates typically report a process spanning a recruiter screen, a technical phone round covering ML and SQL, a take-home or live coding exercise, and a final panel that includes behavioral and system-design discussions. The exact number of rounds can vary by team and level. It is worth asking your recruiter at the start how many stages to expect so you can pace your preparation.

Does New Relic test coding in the Data Scientist interview?

Candidates report at least a practical data or coding component, often involving SQL or Python for data manipulation rather than pure algorithmic coding. You are unlikely to be asked competitive programming-style questions, but writing clean, efficient queries on a realistic dataset is commonly cited. Brush up on window functions, grouping, and filtering on large tables.

What programming languages and tools should I know for this role?

Python is the standard, and SQL is essential. Familiarity with data pipeline tooling (such as Spark or Kafka) is a plus given the scale of telemetry data New Relic handles. Candidates also mention that knowing observability tooling like New Relic itself, Prometheus, or Datadog helps you speak the domain language fluently in interviews.

Exact figures are not publicly confirmed by New Relic. Based on knok jobradar data for the broader Indian Data Scientist market, mid-level roles (3-5 years) typically fall in the 18-30 LPA range, and senior roles (6-9 years) in the 30-48 LPA range. How important is domain knowledge about observability for this interview?

Candidates report that you do not need to be a prior New Relic user, but understanding what observability means (monitoring application health via metrics, logs, and traces) meaningfully helps. It lets you ground your answers in the right user context: SRE and DevOps teams who need fast, accurate signals. Spending time on New Relic's public engineering content before the interview is a practical step.
