02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These are the questions candidates report most often in Cloud Engineer interviews facilitated by Professional Recruiters. Expect a mix of architecture design, troubleshooting, and behavioural questions.

  1. Walk me through a cloud migration you led from on-premise to AWS or Azure. What were the biggest blockers?
  2. How do you design a highly available, fault-tolerant architecture? Walk through your approach step by step.
  3. Explain the difference between vertical and horizontal scaling. When would you choose one over the other?
  4. How have you used Terraform or CloudFormation? What specific problems did infrastructure as code solve for your team?
  5. Describe a cost-optimisation initiative you led. How did you find the waste and what actions did you take?
  6. How do you approach security in a cloud environment? What IAM and network policies do you set up by default?
  7. Walk me through setting up a CI/CD pipeline for a containerised application running on Kubernetes.
  8. You get a call at 2 a.m. that the production API is throwing errors. Walk me through exactly what you do.
  9. What monitoring and alerting tools have you worked with, and how do you set thresholds that reduce alert noise?
  10. Explain VPC peering and transit gateways. When would you use each?
  11. Have you managed a multi-cloud or hybrid environment? What were the main challenges?
  12. Tell me about a time an architecture decision you made turned out to be wrong. How did you recognise it and fix it?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use these three STAR examples as a template for structuring your own stories. Replace the details with your actual experience.

Q: Walk me through a cloud migration you led from on-premise to AWS.

*Situation:* My previous employer ran a legacy platform on physical servers in a co-located data centre. A hardware refresh was approaching and the ops team was spending heavily on manual patching and capacity planning.

*Task:* I was asked to lead the migration to AWS to reduce operational overhead and improve deployment speed.

*Action:* I started with a discovery phase using AWS Migration Hub to map application dependencies. I grouped workloads into rehost, replatform, and refactor categories based on complexity and business criticality. Stateless services were containerised and moved to ECS, while the primary database migrated to RDS with a read replica for reporting queries. All infrastructure was provisioned through Terraform so every change was version-controlled and reviewable. I ran a parallel period with data synchronisation before cutting over during a planned low-traffic window.

*Result:* The migration finished within the agreed timeline. The team reported faster deployment cycles post-launch, and the monthly infrastructure spend came in below the previous co-location cost according to our internal finance tracking.

---

Q: Describe a cost-optimisation initiative you drove.

*Situation:* After joining a fintech startup, I noticed the AWS bill had grown significantly over several quarters without a proportional rise in active user traffic.

*Task:* I was asked to audit cloud spend and reduce it without impacting service performance.

*Action:* I used AWS Cost Explorer and Trusted Advisor to identify idle EC2 instances, oversized RDS instances, and unattached Elastic IPs. I introduced resource tagging to map costs to individual business units so ownership was clear. Long-running, predictable workloads were converted to Reserved Instances. Batch jobs moved to Spot Instances with proper interruption handling built in. I also applied S3 lifecycle policies to shift cold objects to Glacier automatically.

*Result:* Monthly cloud spend dropped noticeably, and I documented the changes in a report shared with the CTO. The tagging standards I introduced became a team-wide practice adopted in subsequent quarters.

---

Q: How do you handle a production outage on cloud infrastructure?

*Situation:* During peak hours our API gateway showed a sudden rise in error rates and elevated latency across multiple availability zones.

*Task:* As the on-call cloud engineer I needed to identify the root cause and restore service as quickly as possible.

*Action:* I pulled CloudWatch metrics and logs to determine whether the issue was compute, database, or network-related. I found that an auto-scaling group had hit its maximum instance limit because of a misconfigured scaling policy set during a previous sprint. I raised the limit temporarily, corrected the policy thresholds, and verified that new instances were passing health checks before standing down. Throughout the incident I sent brief status updates to stakeholders at regular intervals so nobody was left guessing.

*Result:* Service recovered within a short window. I produced a post-mortem with root cause analysis and a runbook for similar incidents, which the team adopted as standard procedure going forward.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the most reliable structure for behavioural and scenario-based questions. Use it whenever the interviewer asks 'tell me about a time' or 'walk me through a project.'

  • *Situation:* Set the context briefly. One or two sentences is enough.
  • *Task:* Clarify what you were specifically responsible for, not the team's goal.
  • *Action:* This is the most important part. Spend the most time here. Be specific about what YOU did, not what the team did collectively.
  • *Result:* End with a concrete outcome. If you cannot share exact figures, describe direction: costs went down, deployment time shortened, on-call incidents reduced.

CAR (Context, Action, Result) is a shorter version that works well for technical deep-dives where the problem context is self-evident from the question.

Design question approach: For architecture questions, structure your answer in this sequence: requirements gathering first (scale, availability, budget constraints), then component selection, then how pieces connect, then trade-offs. Interviewers want to see your thinking process, not just the final answer. Starting with 'I would first ask about the expected traffic and availability requirements' signals seniority.

Incident response approach: For outage scenarios, walk through a clear sequence out loud: detect and scope the problem, establish communication with stakeholders, isolate the root cause, apply a fix, verify recovery, then document. Saying this sequence explicitly shows the interviewer that you stay structured under pressure.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Professional Recruiters places Cloud Engineers with a range of client companies, so interviewers typically look for candidates who can adapt to different environments rather than those who know only one cloud platform deeply.

Practical depth over theory. Candidates who can describe real migrations, real outage responses, and real cost-reduction work stand out. Citing the exact tools, commands, and decisions you made is more convincing than reciting documentation.

Communication under pressure. Cloud engineers often own production incidents. Interviewers want evidence that you stay calm, communicate clearly, and follow a structured process rather than guessing randomly under stress.

Cost awareness. Candidates who mention infrastructure cost as a factor in design decisions signal business maturity. You do not need to memorise pricing tables, but showing that you routinely think about spend is valued by clients of a recruiter firm.

Security by default. Any mention of setting least-privilege IAM policies, encrypting data at rest and in transit, and network segmentation as standard practice scores well. Interviewers notice when security feels like an afterthought in your answers.

Ownership and follow-through. Interviewers notice when a candidate describes not just what they fixed but what they did to prevent recurrence: runbooks, alerting improvements, architecture changes. This signals a senior mindset and reduces risk for the client.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Work through this plan over two to three weeks before your interview.

Week 1: Core concepts
Review the fundamentals of your primary cloud platform. Focus on compute, networking, storage, IAM, and managed databases. Use the official free-tier labs on each provider's learning portal for hands-on practice. If you have gaps in Kubernetes basics, spend time here too.

Week 2: Tools and architecture
Practise writing Terraform configurations from scratch without auto-complete. Spin up a small Kubernetes cluster and deploy a containerised app end to end. Review CI/CD pipeline design patterns. Prepare at least two architecture diagrams you can talk through fluently: one covering a high-availability web application and one covering a data pipeline.

Week 3: Behavioural prep and mock rounds
Document three to five projects from your own experience using the STAR format. Practise answering out loud, not just mentally. Time yourself and record a mock answer to review. Ask a peer to run a mock technical round with you.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping the requirements phase in design questions. Jumping straight to an architecture without asking about scale, budget, or SLA targets makes you appear junior. Always clarify constraints before proposing a solution.

Vague answers to behavioural questions. Saying 'we migrated to the cloud and it went well' tells the interviewer nothing useful. Use STAR and be specific about your individual contribution, not the team's.

Claiming expertise you cannot back up. If Kubernetes is on your resume, expect a hands-on question. Only mention tools you can discuss with technical depth and real examples.

Ignoring security and cost in design answers. A strong cloud engineer weaves in IAM, encryption, and cost controls naturally. Candidates who mention these only when prompted signal that they treat security and cost as optional extras.

Not asking clarifying questions. Silence when faced with an ambiguous question reads as confusion. A short 'Can I clarify the expected traffic scale?' shows maturity and mirrors how senior engineers actually work.

Memorising answers word for word. Interviewers notice rehearsed delivery immediately. Know your material well enough to discuss it conversationally from understanding, not from a script.

Having no questions ready at the end. Asking nothing signals low interest in the role. Prepare two or three genuine questions about the team's cloud maturity, the biggest technical challenge they are solving, or how they handle on-call rotations.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does a Professional Recruiters Cloud Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates typically report two to three rounds. The first is usually a recruiter screening to confirm your background and salary expectations. The second is a technical round covering cloud fundamentals, architecture design, and tool-specific questions. Some roles include a third round with the client company's hiring manager, and the exact structure varies depending on which client the role is for.

Which cloud platform should I focus on for these interviews?

AWS is the most commonly requested platform in India's cloud job market, but Azure demand is growing, especially in enterprise and government projects. Focus deeply on whichever platform appears in the job description. Knowing Terraform well gives you a platform-agnostic edge since it works across AWS, Azure, and GCP and signals that your skills transfer regardless of what the client prefers.

What salary can I expect as a Cloud Engineer placed through Professional Recruiters?

The available data for this company does not include salary band details. Industry surveys and Glassdoor listings commonly cite a wide range for Cloud Engineers in India depending on years of experience, city, and the end-client involved. Bangalore and Delhi, which lead the current openings data, tend to show higher compensation bands according to publicly reported data. Check current listings on Glassdoor or levels.fyi for the most accurate benchmarks before negotiating.

Is coding asked in a Cloud Engineer interview at Professional Recruiters?

Candidates report that the focus is primarily on infrastructure concepts, design scenarios, and tool knowledge rather than algorithm-heavy coding. However, you may be asked to write or review a Terraform configuration, a basic shell script, or a Kubernetes manifest live. Practise writing infrastructure code by hand without IDE assistance so you are comfortable doing it under interview conditions.

How long does the hiring process take?

Candidates report that the timeline varies widely depending on the client company involved. Some processes move quickly (within two weeks) while others extend longer if client availability is limited. Following up politely with your recruiter contact after each round is standard and expected. Having your documents, certifications, and references ready in advance avoids delays at the offer stage.

Are Cloud Engineer roles at Professional Recruiters remote or office-based?

The current data shows openings in Bangalore, Delhi, Hyderabad, Pune, and Chennai, with zero listings in Mumbai. Whether roles are remote, hybrid, or fully on-site depends on the specific client company behind each opening. Check the job description carefully and ask the recruiter directly during the screening call, since the same job title can have very different work arrangements across different client engagements.
