01 Overview

Overview

Twin Health is a metabolic health company that uses AI-powered 'Digital Twin' technology to help patients reverse Type 2 diabetes and other chronic conditions. Their platform combines continuous glucose monitoring, wearable biometric data, and personalised coaching. As of July 2026, knok's jobradar shows 33 open roles at Twin Health, signalling active hiring across their data and product functions.

A Data Analyst here typically works at the crossroads of clinical outcomes, product engagement, and coaching effectiveness. Your core job is to turn dense, continuous biometric and behavioural data into clear decisions: which patients need early intervention, which product features are driving health outcomes, and how to measure whether the program is working. Candidates typically report a recruiter screen, a take-home SQL or case assignment, and one or two panel rounds with product and clinical teams.

Salary context: for mid-level profiles (3-5 years), Glassdoor and publicly reported data commonly cite 10-18 LPA for Data Analysts at health-tech companies. Senior profiles (6-9 years) are commonly cited at 18-30 LPA. Twin Health's funded, mission-driven positioning may push offers toward the higher end of these bands.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Based on candidates' reported experiences and Twin Health's public focus on metabolic health and AI coaching, these are the questions that come up most often:

  1. Walk us through a project where you worked with time-series or health data. What was the hardest part?
  2. How would you measure whether a patient is genuinely responding to the Twin Health program?
  3. How would you design a dashboard for a health coach managing a large patient caseload?
  4. A product team wants to know if a new onboarding flow is working. How do you define 'working' and how do you measure it?
  5. How would you handle missing, delayed, or inconsistent readings from wearable sensors?
  6. Write a SQL query to find all patients who have not logged any activity in the past week.
  7. How would you segment patients into priority groups for targeted coaching interventions?
  8. Describe a time your analysis directly changed a product or business decision.
  9. How comfortable are you with Python for data wrangling? Walk us through a script you wrote recently.
  10. How would you explain a complex statistical finding to a doctor or clinical lead who does not use data tools?
  11. What metrics would you track to evaluate whether the AI coaching recommendations are effective?
  12. How would you detect anomalies in a continuous biometric data stream, and what would you do once you spot one?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell us about a time you worked with complex or messy health data.

*Situation:* At a previous health-tech startup, I was responsible for analysing daily activity and sleep data from a wearable app. The data arrived in irregular intervals with frequent gaps caused by device syncing failures.

*Task:* I needed to build a reliable weekly engagement report for the product team and flag users who were consistently missing their health goals.

*Action:* I first wrote SQL queries to categorise gaps by device type and user cohort. For short gaps under a defined threshold, I applied a forward-fill strategy. For longer gaps, I marked the data as missing rather than imputing values and flagged those users separately. I then built a Tableau dashboard showing rolling weekly averages with colour-coded alerts for users with multiple consecutive days of no data.

*Result:* The product team used the dashboard to prioritise re-engagement outreach. Clinical leads reported higher confidence in data quality for their outcome reviews, and the pattern of device-specific gaps led the engineering team to identify and fix a sync bug.

---

Q: Describe a time you influenced a product or business decision with data.

*Situation:* My team was debating whether to add a new feature to our app. The product manager was confident users wanted it, based on qualitative feedback from a small group.

*Task:* I was asked to validate whether usage data supported investing in the build.

*Action:* I pulled usage logs and ran a cohort analysis comparing users who had interacted with a similar feature in an earlier version against those who had not. I segmented the data by user tenure and engagement level to check whether newer users behaved differently from long-term ones.

*Result:* The analysis showed that only a specific cohort of newer users was likely to use the feature. The product team narrowed the scope of the build accordingly, saving engineering effort and shipping a more focused solution.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you had to communicate a difficult finding to a non-technical audience.

*Situation:* At a wellness company, I discovered that a metric the clinical team had been tracking for several months was calculated incorrectly due to a timezone handling bug in the data pipeline.

*Task:* I had to inform senior clinical leads that several months of reports were unreliable and propose a path forward without creating panic.

*Action:* I prepared a one-page summary in plain language, showed the correct versus incorrect figures side by side, and offered two options: recalculate historical data with the corrected logic, or set a clean baseline from a fixed date going forward.

*Result:* The clinical lead chose to recalculate with corrected data. They appreciated the transparent approach and, as a result, the team introduced a peer-review step for all future metric definitions.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For metrics and measurement questions: Start by anchoring on the goal (what does success look like for the patient or the product?), then name your primary and secondary metrics, and explain how you would track movement over time. Twin Health cares about both health outcomes (biometric improvements) and engagement signals (app usage, coaching session attendance). Show that you understand the difference and can reason about both.

For data quality and wearable data questions: Acknowledge the specific challenges of sensor data: gaps, drift, irregular timestamps, and user non-compliance. Describe how you would distinguish a true signal from a data artefact. Mention your decision framework for imputing data versus flagging it as missing.

For SQL and technical questions: Think out loud. State your assumptions about the schema before writing the query. If you are unsure of a syntax detail, say so and write the logic clearly. Interviewers typically care more about your reasoning than about perfect syntax.

For communication questions: Use the 'headline first' approach. Lead with the finding, then the implication, then the supporting detail. Avoid leading with methodology when the audience is clinical or business-side.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Twin Health sits at the intersection of consumer tech and clinical health. Interviewers are typically looking for a few things that are easy to miss in a standard data interview.

Domain curiosity. You do not need a medical background, but you do need to show genuine interest in health data. Candidates who have read about continuous glucose monitoring or metabolic health before the interview consistently stand out. Know what a 'Digital Twin' means in the context of chronic disease management, in plain terms.

Comfort with messy, real-world data. Wearable and health data is rarely clean. Interviewers probe whether you default to assuming ideal conditions or whether you proactively ask about data quality, gaps, and edge cases. The latter signals real-world experience.

Stakeholder fluency. You will work with product managers, health coaches, and clinical leads. Interviewers want to see that you can translate between technical findings and business or clinical decisions without losing nuance in either direction.

Outcome orientation. Twin Health's core promise is measurable health improvement. Frame your past work in terms of decisions made and outcomes driven, not just reports produced or dashboards built.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Foundation

  1. Read Twin Health's public documentation, press releases, and available case studies. Understand what 'Digital Twin' means for metabolic health in plain terms.
  2. Refresh your SQL: window functions, CTEs, date-based aggregations, and handling NULLs. These come up consistently in take-home assignments.
  3. Review statistics relevant to health data: rolling averages, trend detection, and cohort analysis.

Week 2: Practice

  1. Write out answers to each question in the 'most asked' section above. Practise saying them out loud.
  2. Do at least two timed SQL exercises on platforms like HackerRank or StrataScratch focused on analytics and time-series data.
  3. Prepare three STAR stories from your own experience covering: a messy data challenge, a stakeholder communication win, and a finding that changed a decision.

Before the interview

  1. Prepare one or two thoughtful questions for the panel. Good examples: how the data team measures program success, or how clinical and product teams collaborate on metric definitions.
  2. If you received a take-home, be ready to walk through your reasoning and defend your choices.
  3. Note that Twin Health has 33 open roles on knok's jobradar as of July 2026. Mentioning this during the interview shows you have done your homework.
07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping to solutions without clarifying the problem. For open-ended questions like 'how would you measure patient success?', candidates who immediately list metrics often miss the mark. Start by asking: success for whom, over what time window, and what action would the answer drive?

Ignoring data quality. Saying 'I would pull the data and calculate X' without acknowledging that sensor and wearable data is often incomplete signals a lack of real-world experience. Always mention how you would validate the data first.

Being too generic. Answers like 'I would build a dashboard with KPIs' are forgettable. Tie your answer to Twin Health's specific context: patient cohorts, biometric trends, coaching effectiveness, or program adherence.

Overcomplicating the SQL. Some candidates try to write the most impressive query possible. Interviewers typically prefer a clean, readable query with clear logic over a clever one that is hard to follow.

Not knowing the product. Skipping basic research on what Twin Health actually does is a common and avoidable mistake. Spend time understanding the platform before you walk into any round.

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Twin Health Data Analyst interview typically have?

Candidates typically report a recruiter screening call, a take-home assignment (usually SQL or a case study), and one or two panel rounds with the data, product, or clinical team. The exact structure can vary by team and role level. Confirm the process with your recruiter after the first call so you can prepare accordingly.

What kind of take-home assignment should I expect?

Candidates report receiving SQL problems focused on aggregations, cohort analysis, or time-series data. Some have also received open-ended case questions asking them to define metrics for a hypothetical product feature. Budget a few hours for the assignment and focus on clean, well-commented work with a clear written summary of your reasoning and findings.

Do I need a healthcare background to interview at Twin Health?

No, a clinical background is not required. However, showing that you have read about metabolic health, continuous glucose monitoring, or digital health in general will set you apart from candidates who have not. Interviewers want to see intellectual curiosity about the domain, not medical qualifications.

How can I stand out in the Twin Health interview?
