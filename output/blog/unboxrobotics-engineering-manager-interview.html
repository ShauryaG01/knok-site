01 Overview

Overview

Unboxrobotics is an Indian robotics startup building autonomous solutions for warehouses and logistics operations. With 31 open roles currently listed, the company is in a clear growth phase, and Engineering Manager positions sit at the intersection of firmware, hardware, and software teams.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time your team missed a sprint commitment. What caused it and what did you change?

*Situation:* My team was building a fleet management dashboard for an autonomous vehicle product. Midway through the sprint, an unplanned firmware compatibility issue surfaced and blocked the backend engineers completely.

*Task:* I needed to understand the root cause, reset stakeholder expectations quickly, and put a process in place so this class of blocker would surface earlier in future sprints.

*Action:* I set up a same-day call with the firmware and backend leads to map the exact dependency gap. I communicated to the product manager within a few hours with a revised delivery date and a plain explanation of what had happened. I then introduced a lightweight dependency check as part of sprint planning, where each engineer flags any cross-team reliance before committing to a ticket.

*Result:* The feature shipped in the following sprint with no surprises. Over the next quarter the team consistently flagged cross-team dependencies early, and unplanned spillover dropped noticeably. The dependency check became a permanent part of how the team plans.

---

Q: How do you handle a senior engineer who consistently delivers late without communicating blockers?

*Situation:* A senior engineer on my team, strong technically, had a pattern of going quiet when stuck. Tickets would sit without updates, and I would find out about blockers only at the end of the sprint.

*Task:* I needed to address the behavior without damaging trust or losing someone who was genuinely skilled and valued by the team.

*Action:* I had a direct one-on-one where I named the pattern specifically, not as a personal criticism but as a team-impact issue. I asked what made it hard to surface blockers earlier. It turned out the engineer felt that asking for help signalled weakness. We agreed on a lightweight daily async update, just a short message in the team channel, not a formal status report. I also started acknowledging in team meetings when engineers flagged blockers early, normalizing it as a sign of good judgment.

*Result:* Within a few weeks the engineer was posting updates proactively. Other team members followed the same pattern. The team's ability to course-correct mid-sprint improved, and that senior engineer took on a mentoring role for a junior hire the following quarter.

---

Q: Unboxrobotics works with physical robots in customer warehouses. How do you manage a production incident when the root cause could be firmware, software, or hardware?

*Situation:* At a previous role, a fleet of autonomous carts at a client site began throwing navigation errors and stopping unexpectedly. The client's operations team was losing meaningful productivity and escalating urgency fast.

*Task:* As the engineering manager on call, I had to coordinate across firmware, software, and field operations to diagnose and resolve the issue, while keeping the client informed throughout.

*Action:* I pulled together a quick bridge call with one engineer from each discipline. We agreed upfront on who owned client communication so there was a single point of contact. Each engineer ran parallel checks on their own layer. I sent the client's operations head a brief, honest status note each time we had a meaningful update, avoiding any silence. When the firmware engineer identified a sensor calibration drift as the root cause, I coordinated a remote patch push and arranged for a field technician to do a physical verification on-site.

*Result:* The fleet was back online the same day. The client specifically mentioned the communication quality in their incident debrief. We documented the incident and added a calibration drift check to our automated monitoring suite, preventing a repeat.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioral questions. Structure every story as Situation (the context), Task (your specific responsibility), Action (what you personally did, not the team), and Result (the measurable or observable outcome). Keep Situation and Task brief. Spend most of your time on Action and Result.

CIRCLES lite for product and prioritization questions. When asked how you would prioritize competing roadmap asks, briefly state who the customer is, what the core need is, what constraints exist (time, resources, dependencies), and then rank options with a clear rationale. Robotics companies care a lot about field constraints, so always factor in hardware timelines when ranking.

Situation-Behavior-Impact for feedback and people questions. When describing how you gave feedback to an engineer, name the specific situation, the specific behavior you observed, and the impact it had on the team or product. Avoid generalizing ('they were always late') and stick to observable facts.

Horizon framing for strategy questions. For questions about technical direction or roadmap, split your answer into near-term (current quarter), mid-term (next couple of quarters), and longer-term thinking. This shows interviewers you balance execution with vision, which is critical at a growth-stage robotics company.

Lead with the outcome, then explain. Many EM candidates bury the result at the end of a long story. Train yourself to open with the outcome: 'My team shipped on time and the client renewed the contract. Here is how we got there.' This keeps interviewers engaged and signals confidence.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Comfort with hardware-software complexity. Unboxrobotics builds physical products that operate in real customer environments. Interviewers want to see that you understand how hardware timelines, firmware releases, and software deployments interact. You do not need to write firmware yourself, but you must show you have managed across these layers without getting lost.

Clear ownership and accountability. At a startup with 31 open roles, teams are lean and there is no room for ambiguity about who owns what. Interviewers will probe for situations where you stepped up without being asked, made a call under uncertainty, and owned the outcome whether it went well or not.

Structured communication under pressure. Warehouse robotics incidents affect customer operations directly. Interviewers want evidence that you can communicate clearly with clients, operations teams, and your own engineers simultaneously, without creating panic or information voids.

Hiring and people development instincts. A company growing its engineering org wants EMs who can attract talent, onboard quickly, and grow engineers into senior contributors. Expect detailed questions on your hiring process, how you evaluate candidates, and specific examples of engineers you have developed.

Pragmatic stance on technical debt. Startups move fast and accumulate debt. Interviewers at companies like unboxrobotics typically want to see that you can make deliberate trade-offs, not that you are dogmatic about clean code at the expense of shipping.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Understand the product and domain. Read everything public about unboxrobotics, including their website, LinkedIn posts, and any press coverage. Understand what kinds of robots they build, which industries they serve, and what their technical stack appears to be. This context makes your interview answers feel specific rather than generic.

Step 2: Build your story bank. Write out several career stories covering: a missed deadline, a difficult engineer conversation, a cross-functional conflict, a hiring decision, a production incident, and a technical direction call. Map each story to the STAR format before the interview, not during it.

Step 3: Prepare hardware-software scenarios. Even if your background is pure software, prepare at least a couple of stories that involve coordinating with a hardware or firmware team. If you lack direct experience, prepare a thoughtful answer about how you would approach it as a new EM at a robotics company.

Step 4: Prepare smart questions to ask. Candidates report that interviewers typically appreciate genuine curiosity. Prepare questions about team structure, current engineering challenges, how success is measured for EMs, and how the roadmap is set. Avoid questions with answers already on the company website.

Step 5: Practice spoken delivery. EM interviews are conversational. Record yourself answering a couple of questions out loud. Listen for filler words, buried results, and passive voice ('the team did' vs 'I decided'). Interviewers notice the difference.

Step 6: Know your compensation target. Engineering Manager salaries in India vary widely by level. Publicly reported ranges from Glassdoor and industry surveys suggest Manager roles are commonly cited around 35-60 LPA, Senior Manager roles around 55-90 LPA, and Director-level roles around 90-150+ LPA. Know your target band before the offer conversation so you are not caught off guard.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Describing team actions instead of personal actions. The most common EM interview mistake is saying 'we did' instead of 'I decided' or 'I initiated.' Interviewers are evaluating your judgment and ownership, not the team's output.

Skipping the result. Many candidates tell a detailed story and then trail off without stating the outcome. Always close with what changed, what shipped, what the customer said, or how the team improved.

Treating people questions as soft filler. Questions about feedback, retention, and hiring are not warm-up questions. At a robotics startup they are often the most important signal. Give these the same structured, specific treatment you give technical questions.

Overstating technical depth. You do not need to be a robotics engineer to be an EM at unboxrobotics, but overclaiming expertise you do not have will surface quickly. It is better to say 'I partnered with a firmware lead on this decision' than to imply you made firmware architecture calls alone.

Ignoring the startup context. Answers that assume large-team processes, dedicated QA pipelines, or long planning cycles may signal that you are not calibrated for a lean, fast-moving company. Show flexibility and comfort with ambiguity.

Arriving without questions to ask. Not having questions signals low interest or low preparation. Both hurt your chances.

How many rounds does the unboxrobotics Engineering Manager interview typically have?

Candidates report that the process typically includes an initial recruiter or HR screen, one or two technical and people-management interviews, and a final round with a senior leader or founder. Some candidates mention a case study or take-home problem at the manager level. The exact format can vary, so ask the recruiter to outline the stages at the start of the process.

Does unboxrobotics expect EMs to write code during the interview?

Candidates report that Engineering Manager interviews typically focus on system thinking, team management, and cross-functional coordination rather than live coding. Some technical discussions may test your ability to reason through architecture or debugging scenarios at a conceptual level. Being comfortable discussing firmware and software interactions is more important here than writing code on a whiteboard.

How important is robotics domain knowledge for an EM role here?

You do not need to be a robotics engineer, but familiarity with hardware-software co-development is genuinely valuable. Interviewers typically want to see that you can manage engineers across firmware, software, and field operations layers without getting lost in the handoffs.
