01 Overview

Overview

Nium is a global fintech company that powers cross-border payments, card issuance, and banking-as-a-service for businesses worldwide. Security Engineers here protect payment rails, cardholder data, and the APIs that move money across borders. The work sits at the intersection of software security, compliance, and incident response inside a heavily regulated industry.

As of mid-2026, knok jobradar tracked 39 open roles at Nium across India. Nationally, 628 Security Engineer positions were live at the same time, with Bangalore leading at 69 openings. Candidates typically report a multi-round process covering a recruiter screen, one or two technical rounds, a system design discussion, and a final conversation with a senior leader. Round count and order vary by level and team, so treat this as a general pattern rather than a fixed structure.

Nium's security team works closely with product and engineering, so interviewers look for someone who can communicate risk in business terms, not just technical jargon. Fintech-specific knowledge, especially around PCI-DSS, API security, and fraud vectors, comes up consistently across interview feedback.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format to structure your answers. Here are three examples tailored to Nium's domain.

Q: How would you detect and respond to a credential stuffing attack on a payment gateway?

*Situation:* At a previous company, our authentication service began showing unusual login patterns, with a sudden spike in failed attempts from a large number of distinct IP addresses.

*Task:* I was the on-call security engineer and responsible for investigating, containing, and documenting the incident.

*Action:* I pulled logs from our WAF, application servers, and authentication service. I correlated the IP patterns and confirmed the signature matched credential stuffing rather than brute force. I implemented rate limiting on the login endpoint, added step-up verification for suspicious sessions, and coordinated with the fraud team to flag accounts that had a successful login during the attack window. I also notified affected users to reset their passwords.

*Result:* The attack was contained within hours, affected accounts were secured before any fraudulent transactions were confirmed, and we used the incident to push through a long-pending MFA rollout for high-value accounts.

---

Q: Describe a time you found a critical vulnerability. What did you do?

*Situation:* During a routine code review at a fintech startup, I found that an internal API was returning full card numbers in its response payload, and those responses were being logged in plaintext.

*Task:* I had to assess scope, escalate appropriately, and work with the engineering team to fix it without disrupting live transactions.

*Action:* I documented the finding with evidence and classified it as critical given the PCI-DSS implications. I escalated to the CISO and engineering lead and proposed a two-step fix: an immediate mask on the logging side, then removing the sensitive field from the API response entirely. I stayed involved through both deployments to verify the fix was complete.

*Result:* Both fixes shipped quickly, the logging gap was closed before any confirmed data exposure, and the incident triggered a broader audit of our API response schemas. We updated the code review checklist to catch similar issues earlier.

---

Q: How do you balance security requirements with a tight product deadline?

*Situation:* A product team was launching a new payment feature and the go-live date was fixed due to a contractual commitment with a large client.

*Task:* My job was to complete a security review without blocking the launch.

*Action:* I prioritised the review by risk, focusing first on authentication, data handling, and external API calls. I worked directly with developers to fix the issues I rated high severity before launch. I documented the remaining findings as tracked items with owners and target dates, and I wrote a short risk-acceptance note for the CISO covering what was being deferred and why.

*Result:* The feature launched on time, the critical issues were resolved, and the remaining findings were addressed in the following sprint. The product team started involving security earlier in subsequent projects after seeing how the process worked.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

A few frameworks that work well for Nium-style security interviews:

Threat modelling questions: Structure your answer using STRIDE (Spoofing, Tampering, Repudiation, Information disclosure, Denial of service, Elevation of privilege). Walk through each category for the system in question, then explain which mitigations you would prioritise and why.

Incident response questions: Cover Prepare, Detect, Contain, Eradicate, Recover, and Learn as a natural flow. You do not need to name the framework explicitly, but hitting each phase shows methodical thinking. Interviewers pay attention to whether you mention communication and documentation alongside the technical steps.

Design and architecture questions: Lead with your threat model, then talk about controls in layers (network, application, data). For fintech specifically, bring in compliance requirements such as PCI-DSS and RBI guidelines alongside pure security reasoning. This shows you understand the regulatory context Nium operates in.

Behavioural questions: Use STAR cleanly. Keep the Situation brief, spend most of your time on Action (what you personally did, not the team), and make the Result concrete. If you do not have a specific number, describe the outcome in plain terms such as 'no confirmed data exposure', 'launched on time', or 'adopted across the engineering org'.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Candidates report that Nium security interviews test three things together: technical depth, judgment under pressure, and the ability to work with non-security stakeholders.

Technical depth means going beyond surface-level answers. If you mention PCI-DSS, be ready to discuss specific requirements. If you mention zero-trust, explain how you would implement it in a real environment, not just define the term.

Judgment means showing you can prioritise. Fintech companies deal with real financial risk, so interviewers want to see that you know which vulnerabilities to fix first and how to communicate residual risk to leadership without panic or vagueness.

Collaboration is a recurring theme in candidate feedback. Nium's engineering teams move fast, and the security function needs to be an enabler rather than a blocker. Examples where you worked with developers to fix issues rather than just flagging them tend to land well.

Fintech-specific knowledge is a clear differentiator. Familiarity with PCI-DSS scope, card data environments, fraud detection concepts, and API security for payment systems will set you apart from candidates with only a general security background.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Foundations and fintech context
Review PCI-DSS requirements, especially scope reduction, tokenisation, and logging rules. Read Nium's public blog, press releases, and any available case studies to understand their product lines across cross-border payments, card issuance, and banking-as-a-service. Map your past experience to their domain.

Week 2: Technical depth
Practise threat modelling on payment API scenarios out loud. Review common attack patterns targeting fintech: credential stuffing, API abuse, account takeover, and insider threats. Revisit your SIEM and log analysis experience with specific examples ready to share.

Week 3: System design and behavioural prep
Practise designing secure systems out loud, covering authentication, encryption at rest and in transit, secrets management, and audit logging. Write out STAR answers for your strongest five to seven experiences. Focus on incidents, vulnerability findings, and cross-team collaboration stories.

Before each round
Research the interviewers on LinkedIn if you have their names. Review Nium's recent news for product announcements or security-related coverage. Prepare two or three questions that show you have thought about their specific challenges, not just generic team culture questions.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Giving textbook answers without personal examples. Saying 'I would implement rate limiting' is far less convincing than describing a time you actually did it and what happened as a result.

Skipping the business context. Interviewers at fintech companies want to hear that you understand why security matters in payments, not just how to implement controls. Connect your answers to risk, compliance, and customer trust wherever you can.

Vague incident response answers. Saying 'I contained the incident and notified stakeholders' tells an interviewer very little. Be specific about what you investigated, what you found, what actions you took, and what changed afterward.

Treating compliance and security as the same thing. PCI-DSS compliance is a floor, not a ceiling. Showing that you push for security beyond the minimum signals maturity and earns respect from experienced interviewers.

Not asking good questions. Candidates who ask nothing, or ask only generic questions, miss a chance to show genuine interest. Ask about the team's current priorities, the biggest challenges they face, or how the security function is structured relative to engineering.
