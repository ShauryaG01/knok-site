01 Overview

Overview

Talenthackers is a staffing and recruitment firm that places iOS Engineers at product companies and tech clients across India. The iOS Engineer positions typically involve building native iOS applications in Swift, integrating with backend APIs, and collaborating with product and design teams at the end client.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These are the questions candidates report most frequently for iOS Engineer roles screened through staffing firms like Talenthackers. Expect a mix of conceptual, coding, and behavioural questions.

  1. Walk me through how ARC manages memory in Swift. How do retain cycles happen and how do you fix them?
  2. What is the difference between strong, weak, and unowned references? Give an example of when you would use each.
  3. How do you handle concurrency in iOS? Compare GCD, OperationQueue, and Swift async/await.
  4. Explain the MVVM pattern. Why might you choose it over MVC for a larger iOS project?
  5. How do you persist data locally in an iOS app? When would you pick UserDefaults, CoreData, or a third-party solution like Realm?
  6. How do you integrate REST APIs in iOS? How do you handle error states and retries?
  7. How do you profile and optimise an iOS app that is running slowly or consuming too much memory?
  8. What does your unit testing approach look like for iOS? Which frameworks do you use and why?
  9. Describe a production crash you investigated. What tools did you use and what was the root cause?
  10. How do you handle push notifications end to end, from APNs setup to handling a tap in the foreground?
  11. Walk me through the App Store submission process, including provisioning profiles and certificates.
  12. How do you keep up with Apple's annual SDK changes? Is there anything from recent iOS releases you have actively adopted?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format for behavioural and scenario questions. Here are three worked examples relevant to iOS Engineer interviews.

Q: How do you find and fix memory leaks in an iOS app?

*Situation:* An app I was working on was crashing on older iPhones after extended use by the same user in a single session.

*Task:* I was responsible for identifying the root cause and fixing it before a scheduled release date.

*Action:* I profiled the app using Xcode Instruments with the Leaks and Allocations templates while walking through the main user flows. I traced the leak to a delegate property declared as 'strong' instead of 'weak', which created a retain cycle between a view controller and a child component. I also audited all closure captures in the same module and added '[weak self]' wherever a closure could outlive the view controller.

*Result:* Memory footprint stabilised during the same user flows and crash reports linked to memory pressure dropped to zero in the following release.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you improved a networking layer in an iOS app.

*Situation:* I joined a fintech team where the existing URLSession wrapper had no error handling and silently failed on server errors, giving users a blank screen with no explanation.

*Task:* I was asked to refactor it so that both users and developers could understand what was going wrong.

*Action:* I rewrote the layer using Swift async/await for readability, mapped HTTP status codes to typed domain errors, and added exponential backoff for transient server failures. I also used a protocol-based design so the layer could be replaced with a mock in unit tests without touching any business logic.

*Result:* User-facing 'data not loading' complaints fell noticeably after the next release, and the team reported that writing new network-dependent tests became significantly faster.

---

Q: Describe a time you debugged a production crash.

*Situation:* After an app update, Crashlytics showed a spike in 'index out of range' crashes affecting a key screen for a large share of active users.

*Task:* I had to reproduce, diagnose, and ship a fix quickly before the issue was escalated to the client.

*Action:* I symbolicated the crash report using the dSYM file from the build archive and traced it to a table view data source being mutated on a background thread while the main thread was reading the same array. I wrapped the array mutation in a serial DispatchQueue to synchronise access and added a debug assertion to catch similar threading violations early in future development builds.

*Result:* A hotfix shipped the same day and that crash category disappeared entirely from subsequent Crashlytics reports.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Most iOS interview questions fall into three types. Match your structure to the question being asked.

For conceptual questions ('explain retain cycles', 'what is MVVM'), use a three-part structure: define the concept, explain why it matters in practice, then give a brief real example from your own work. This combination shows both theoretical understanding and hands-on experience, which is what interviewers are checking for.

For scenario questions ('how would you design a caching layer', 'what would you do if the app is slow'), think out loud. Name the trade-offs explicitly. For example: 'CoreData gives you powerful querying but adds significant setup complexity; for a simple key-value store I would start with UserDefaults.' Interviewers are evaluating your reasoning process, not just the final answer you land on.

For behavioural questions ('tell me about a time'), use STAR: one sentence of Situation, one sentence of Task describing your specific responsibility, a clear Action block using 'I' not 'we', and a concrete Result. Keep the whole answer to a couple of minutes. If the Result is hard to quantify, describe what changed qualitatively for the team or the users.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Talenthackers screens iOS Engineers both for technical fit and for how well a candidate will integrate into a client team. Candidates report that interviewers pay close attention to three qualities.

Depth over recall. Knowing that retain cycles exist is expected. Being able to profile one in Instruments, explain why ARC cannot resolve it automatically, and show the exact code fix is what moves you forward in the process. Prepare to go well beyond definitions.

Clear communication. Because Talenthackers places engineers into client teams that often include non-iOS colleagues, they want to see that you can explain technical decisions in plain language. Practice describing trade-offs without leaning on jargon or acronyms your audience may not know.

Ownership and initiative. Interviewers respond well to stories where you spotted a problem before being asked, proposed a better approach, or drove a change through to completion. Candidates who wait to be told what to do are harder to place confidently with clients.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Core iOS fundamentals. Revise ARC and memory management, concurrency models (GCD and Swift async/await), and the difference between UIKit and SwiftUI approaches. Write small code examples from scratch rather than just reading notes. Being able to type correct Swift under mild pressure is a skill in itself.

Week 2: Architecture and testing. Build a small sample project using MVVM, wire it to a real public API, and write unit tests for the ViewModel layer. Be ready to explain every design choice, not just describe the pattern at a high level. Interviewers probe the reasoning behind architecture decisions.

Week 3: Behavioural and process prep. Write out three to four STAR stories covering a bug you fixed, a collaboration challenge, and a technical decision you drove or influenced. Say them out loud until they sound natural rather than rehearsed. Also review the App Store submission flow end to end, including provisioning profiles, certificates, and TestFlight, since these come up consistently.

Before the interview. Read Apple's release notes for the current iOS version and note one or two features relevant to your past projects. Candidates report being asked about recent SDK changes as a signal of genuine curiosity. Also prepare at least two questions about the end client engagement, the domain, and the team structure, since asking nothing about the client makes a poor impression in a staffing-led process.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Giving textbook answers without examples. Saying 'weak references prevent retain cycles' is not enough on its own. Interviewers want to know you have actually debugged one. Anchor every concept to something you built or fixed.

Skipping trade-off reasoning. Choosing MVVM or CoreData is not automatically correct. If you cannot explain when you would choose differently, it signals surface-level knowledge. Always name the alternative and explain why you ruled it out for that specific context.

Ignoring the staffing context. Talenthackers is placing you with a client, not hiring you for an internal team. Candidates who ask nothing about the end client, the domain, or the project come across as passive. Prepare at least two questions about the client engagement before the interview.

Starting answers without structure. iOS technical interviews reward organised thinking. Taking a moment to collect your thoughts before speaking is better than starting several sentences that trail off without landing anywhere.

Underestimating App Store process knowledge. Provisioning profiles, certificates, and TestFlight are sometimes labelled 'devops' topics but come up consistently in iOS interviews. Walk through the full submission flow in your prep so you can answer these without hesitation.
