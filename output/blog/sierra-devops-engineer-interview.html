02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates at Sierra commonly report questions across these themes:

  1. Walk me through a CI/CD pipeline you built from scratch. What tools did you choose and why?
  2. How do you manage infrastructure as code at scale? What pitfalls have you hit with Terraform or Ansible?
  3. Describe a production outage you owned. What was your incident response process, and what did you change afterwards?
  4. How would you design a Kubernetes cluster for a multi-tenant SaaS product? What isolation mechanisms would you use?
  5. We run microservices across AWS. How would you set up centralised logging and alerting so teams can debug their own services?
  6. How do you handle secret management in a containerised environment? Walk me through your approach end to end.
  7. A deployment just went bad in production. How do you roll back safely without losing in-flight requests?
  8. Explain how you would implement a blue/green or canary deployment strategy. What are the trade-offs?
  9. How do you keep cloud costs under control while the engineering team is moving fast? Give a concrete example.
  10. Describe your experience with GitOps. How does it differ from traditional pipeline-push deployments?
  11. How do you ensure compliance and security scanning are built into the pipeline, not bolted on at the end?
  12. Tell me about a time you had to convince developers to change how they work for the sake of reliability or security.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a production outage you owned and what you changed afterwards.

*Situation:* Our primary database cluster failed over unexpectedly during peak traffic at my previous company, causing significant downtime for end users.

*Task:* I was the on-call DevOps engineer that night and had to coordinate the response, communicate with stakeholders, and get the service healthy as fast as possible.

*Action:* I pulled the runbook, confirmed the replica had promoted correctly in RDS, then worked with the backend team to restart application pods that had cached stale connection strings. I kept a live incident log and updated the product manager throughout. Once stable, I ran a blameless post-mortem, identified that our health-check thresholds were too loose, and automated a synthetic transaction test that would have caught the issue earlier.

*Result:* We reduced mean time to detect for similar database events substantially, and the synthetic test caught a near-miss several months later with zero customer impact.

---

Q: How do you manage infrastructure as code at scale?

*Situation:* At my last role, Terraform was spread across many repositories with no shared module standard, leading to config drift and broken plans every release cycle.

*Task:* I was asked to own the IaC strategy and bring consistency without slowing down multiple product squads.

*Action:* I introduced a private Terraform module registry, added linting and policy-as-code checks with Conftest, and set up Atlantis for pull-request based plan and apply. I ran lunch-and-learn sessions so developers could self-serve safely.

*Result:* Drift incidents dropped noticeably within two quarters, and the time to provision a new service environment shrank from days to a matter of hours. Teams reported feeling more confident making infrastructure changes because they got automated feedback before merge.

---

Q: How do you keep cloud costs under control while teams move fast?

*Situation:* After a rapid feature push, our AWS bill had grown sharply over two quarters with no clear owner for the overspend.

*Task:* Engineering leadership asked me to build a cost visibility and governance framework without adding friction to deployments.

*Action:* I set up AWS Cost Explorer with team-level tagging, created weekly Slack digests showing each squad their spend trend, and added a budget alert that paged the squad lead when spend exceeded a threshold. I also right-sized the most expensive EC2 and RDS instances using Compute Optimizer recommendations.

*Result:* Teams quickly identified and eliminated idle resources responsible for a meaningful share of the bill. Tagging compliance improved dramatically, which made cost attribution far more actionable for engineering leadership.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use STAR for every behavioural question. Situation and Task together should take no more than 30 seconds. Spend most of your time on Action (what you specifically did, not 'we') and Result (a concrete outcome, even if you can only say 'reduced incident frequency' rather than a specific number).

For technical design questions, use a four-step structure. First, clarify requirements and constraints (scale, SLA, team size). Second, sketch the high-level architecture. Third, walk through your tool choices and the trade-offs you considered. Fourth, explain what you would monitor and how you would know the design is working.

For 'why Sierra' or culture questions, connect a specific value or product area from Sierra's public communications to something you have genuinely cared about in past work. Avoid generic answers about 'innovation' or 'growth' with no personal hook.

Keep answers to two or three minutes. DevOps interviewers are busy practitioners. A concise, structured answer with one concrete example lands better than an exhaustive monologue.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates report, Sierra DevOps interviewers look for three things above everything else.

Ownership mindset. They want to see that you treat the system as yours, not someone else's problem. This shows up in how you talk about incidents (do you say 'I' or always 'the team'), and whether your examples end with systemic fixes rather than just 'we restarted the service.'

Depth over breadth. Knowing ten tools shallowly is less impressive than knowing three tools deeply. If you claim Kubernetes experience, expect questions on pod disruption budgets, resource quotas, and network policies, not just 'I deployed containers.'

Communication across teams. DevOps sits between engineering, product, and security. Interviewers probe whether you can translate technical trade-offs into language a non-technical stakeholder understands, and whether you can push back on bad practices respectfully.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Sharpen your core tools. Revisit Kubernetes fundamentals (scheduling, networking, RBAC), Terraform state management and module design, and your primary cloud provider's IAM and networking model. Do at least one hands-on lab or personal project if you have been away from these recently.

Week 2: Build your story bank. Write out five to six real examples from your career using STAR. Cover: an outage you resolved, a pipeline you built or improved, a cost or reliability win, a time you influenced a non-DevOps team, and a technical decision where you weighed competing trade-offs.

Week 3: Practice out loud. Record yourself answering two questions per day. Listen back for filler words, passive language ('we kind of did'), and answers that run beyond three minutes. Do at least one mock interview with a peer or a practice platform.

Before the interview: Research Sierra's current engineering blog posts or public tech talks if available. Know which cloud and container platform they use. Prepare two or three thoughtful questions about their on-call culture, deployment frequency, or observability maturity.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Claiming tool experience you cannot defend. If your resume says Terraform but you struggle to explain remote state or workspace strategy, that gap shows up immediately. Only list tools you can discuss at one level deeper than the documentation summary.

Describing team wins without your individual contribution. Saying 'we reduced deploy time' tells the interviewer nothing about you. Name what you specifically designed, wrote, or changed.

Skipping the 'why'. Candidates who say 'I chose Helm' without explaining what alternatives they considered and why Helm won for that context sound like they followed a tutorial, not made an engineering decision.

Ignoring the behavioural rounds. Many candidates prepare only for technical questions and then give thin answers to 'tell me about a conflict' or 'how do you handle pushback.' Sierra typically includes at least one behavioural discussion, and weak answers there can cancel a strong technical performance.

Not asking good questions. Ending an interview with no questions, or only generic ones, signals low interest. Ask about something specific: their incident response process, how they handle database migrations at scale, or what the on-call rotation looks like.

How many rounds does the Sierra DevOps interview typically have?

Candidates report two to four rounds, typically. The process commonly includes an initial HR or recruiter screen, one or two technical interviews covering DevOps tools and system design, and a final round that may include a culture or leadership discussion. Some candidates also report a short take-home or live coding exercise focused on scripting or infrastructure tasks.

Is Kubernetes knowledge mandatory for Sierra DevOps roles?

Based on what candidates report, Kubernetes is a commonly tested area and appears in most Sierra DevOps job descriptions. You do not always need to be a Kubernetes expert at entry level, but mid and senior candidates are typically expected to discuss cluster management, networking, and workload configuration in detail. If you are light on hands-on Kubernetes experience, invest time in a local cluster setup before your interview.

Does Sierra conduct live coding or take-home assignments?

Candidates report that Sierra sometimes includes a scripting or automation task, either live or as a short take-home. Common formats include writing a shell or Python script, a Terraform configuration for a given architecture, or debugging a broken pipeline. Prepare by practising small automation tasks under a time constraint so you are comfortable writing working code without looking everything up.

How important is cloud certification for getting hired at Sierra?

Certifications are helpful as a signal but are not a substitute for hands-on experience. Candidates with an AWS Solutions Architect or GCP Professional DevOps Engineer certification report that it helps at the resume shortlisting stage. However, interviewers will still probe practical depth, so a certification paired with real project experience is far stronger than a certification alone.
