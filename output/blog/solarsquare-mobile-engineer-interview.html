01 Overview

Overview

SolarSquare is one of India's fast-growing residential solar companies, helping homeowners go solar with end-to-end installation, monitoring, and after-sales support. As a Mobile Engineer there, you would typically work on the customer app (tracking installation progress, viewing live energy dashboards, managing payments) and internal tools used by field installation teams.

The company currently has 177 open roles listed, which signals active hiring across multiple functions. For Mobile Engineers in the broader market, 79 openings were tracked as of mid-2026, with Bangalore leading at 21 roles, followed by Delhi (3) and Pune (3).

The interview process typically runs 3-4 rounds: a recruiter or hiring manager screening call, one or two technical rounds covering coding and system design, and a final round focused on culture and product thinking. Candidates report that interviewers at SolarSquare care as much about real-world problem-solving and product empathy as they do about pure coding ability. Understanding the solar energy domain (how generation data works, why offline reliability matters for field teams) gives you a clear advantage going in.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These are the questions candidates most commonly report from SolarSquare Mobile Engineer interviews. Prepare detailed answers for all of them before your first round.

  1. Walk us through a complex mobile feature you built end-to-end. What tradeoffs did you make?
  2. How would you design a real-time energy monitoring dashboard for a homeowner app? What data-refresh strategy would you use?
  3. SolarSquare's field teams operate in areas with poor connectivity. How do you build offline-first functionality into a mobile app?
  4. How would you structure state management for an app that combines live solar generation data, billing history, and service requests?
  5. Tell us about a time you diagnosed and fixed a performance problem (ANRs, frame drops, high memory usage) in a production app.
  6. How would you build a push notification system that alerts users when their solar panel output drops below expected levels?
  7. Describe your approach to writing testable mobile code. How do you balance unit tests, integration tests, and end-to-end tests?
  8. Tell us about a time you worked with a backend or hardware team to define an API contract. How did you resolve disagreements?
  9. SolarSquare serves customers across India, many on mid-range or older devices. How do you ensure a good experience on constrained hardware?
  10. How would you implement a multi-step installation tracking flow where users can resume from exactly where they left off, even after closing the app?
  11. Describe your experience with maps or location features. How did you balance accuracy against battery consumption?
  12. How do you decide when to adopt a new platform API or move away from an older approach? Walk us through a recent example.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for all experience-based questions. Here are three worked examples.

Q: Tell us about a time you fixed a serious performance problem in a production app.

*Situation:* Our Android app was receiving poor reviews because users reported the home screen froze for several seconds after login.

*Task:* I was the only mobile engineer available that sprint, so the investigation and fix were entirely mine to own.

*Action:* I reproduced the issue with Android Profiler and found we were making several API calls sequentially on the main thread during startup. I refactored the startup sequence to run these calls in parallel using coroutines, moved heavy data parsing to a background dispatcher, and added a skeleton loading UI so the screen felt responsive immediately.

*Result:* Startup time dropped significantly and reviews mentioning freezes stopped appearing in that release cycle. The pattern became our standard for any new screen we built going forward.

---

Q: Describe a feature you built that required close collaboration with a backend team.

*Situation:* We needed to add a real-time generation chart to our solar monitoring app, but the backend team had not built a WebSocket endpoint before.

*Task:* My job was to define what the mobile client needed and help the backend engineer understand the data shape required for a smooth, responsive chart.

*Action:* I drafted a simple API spec document covering event types, payload fields, and error states, then held a couple of short sync calls with the backend engineer to walk through edge cases: device offline, inverter restart, and timezone handling. We agreed on a versioning field so the client could handle future payload changes without forcing an app update.

*Result:* The integration shipped in one sprint with no breaking changes. The chart became one of the highest-rated features in our in-app feedback.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you had to build something that worked reliably in poor network conditions.

*Situation:* Our installer app was being used in rural and semi-urban sites where 4G was unreliable. Field staff were losing half-filled forms when the connection dropped.

*Task:* I was asked to make the job-completion form work offline and sync automatically when connectivity returned.

*Action:* I introduced a local Room database as the source of truth, queued all form submissions as pending records, and used WorkManager with exponential backoff to retry uploads when the network came back. I also added a visible 'pending sync' indicator so field staff knew their data was saved locally even without a connection.

*Result:* Reported data-loss incidents dropped to zero in the following month, and field staff adoption of the app increased because they trusted it would not lose their work.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For system design questions (energy dashboard, notification system, offline sync): start with the user's goal, talk through the data flow, then address the specific constraint that matters at SolarSquare (real-time updates, offline reliability, low-end device support). Interviewers want to see you think through constraints before jumping to a solution.

For 'how have you handled X' questions: always use a concrete past example rather than a hypothetical. If you lack direct experience, say 'I haven't faced this exact scenario, but here is how I would approach it' and then give a structured walk-through.

For tradeoff questions: name the tradeoff clearly, for example 'we chose X over Y because of Z,' explain what you gave up, and say whether you would make the same call again. Interviewers value honesty over polished-sounding answers.

For domain-specific questions (solar monitoring, field tools): show that you understand why the problem matters to the business. If asked about offline sync, mention that a field engineer cannot delay an installation job because of a connectivity issue. Connecting your technical answer to a real business cost shows product thinking.

For debugging and performance questions: walk through your diagnostic process step by step (reproduce, instrument, isolate, fix, verify). Interviewers want to see a methodical approach, not just the final solution.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Product empathy, not just coding ability. SolarSquare is a product company serving homeowners and field staff. Interviewers want to see that you think about the end user, not just the API contract or the class hierarchy.

Clear ownership. Candidates who say 'I built X' and can explain every layer of it do far better than those who say 'our team built X.' Be ready to go deep on anything you claim credit for.

Domain awareness. You do not need a background in solar, but you should understand the basic product: customers generate solar energy, they want to see how much they produce and save, and field engineers install and service panels. Showing you have thought about these workflows signals genuine interest.

Comfort with Indian market constraints. Variable network quality, a wide range of device tiers, and multilingual users come up repeatedly. Interviewers want to hear that you have shipped features under these constraints or have a clear plan for handling them.

Conversational engagement. Candidates report that interviewers interrupt with clarifying questions often. This is a positive sign, not a challenge. Treat it as a dialogue and adjust your answer based on what they ask.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Core technical review

Revise the fundamentals your target platform (Android or iOS) expects: lifecycle management, the threading model, memory handling, and the navigation pattern the platform recommends. Do at least one mock system design session focused on a real-time data dashboard, covering data freshness, local caching, and failure handling.

Week 2: Domain and product prep

Download the SolarSquare app and use it for a few days. Note what works well, what feels slow, and what you would improve. Read publicly available coverage of the company to understand their growth stage and customer base. Prepare answers for all 12 questions listed in the section above.

Before each round

Review your STAR stories. Aim for 5-6 ready-to-use examples covering performance, cross-team collaboration, offline reliability, a product decision, and a time things went wrong. Candidates report that SolarSquare interviewers often follow up with 'what would you do differently?' so prepare that angle for each story too.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Giving generic answers. Saying 'I care about code quality' with no example behind it is the fastest way to lose an interviewer's attention. Attach every claim to a specific story.

Ignoring the product context. Candidates who treat this as a generic mobile role miss easy points. Reference the company's actual product when answering design questions.

Over-engineering design answers. SolarSquare is a growth-stage startup. Proposing a globally distributed microservices architecture for a simple notification alert will raise eyebrows. Match your design to the company's actual scale and team size.

Rushing past reliability work. Offline sync, retry logic, and graceful degradation are core to SolarSquare's field tools. Candidates who have done this work but treat it as minor in interviews leave value on the table.

Not asking questions. Interviewers notice when a candidate has nothing to ask at the end. Prepare a couple of genuine questions about the team's current technical challenges, the app's biggest pain points, or how mobile and backend engineers collaborate day to day.
