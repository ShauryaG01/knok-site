02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

The following questions reflect what candidates publicly report in Software Engineer interviews at similar-profile companies:

  1. Walk me through a project you built end-to-end. What technical decisions did you personally own?
  2. How do you approach debugging a production issue you have never encountered before?
  3. Design a URL shortener. Walk me through your thought process from requirements to architecture.
  4. What data structures would you use to build a real-time leaderboard, and why?
  5. Tell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate on a technical approach. What happened?
  6. How do you make sure your code is easy for the next engineer to read and maintain?
  7. Explain the difference between a process and a thread. When would you prefer one over the other?
  8. How have you handled pressure to deliver quickly without cutting corners on quality?
  9. Describe a bug that took you a long time to find. What made it so hard to track down?
  10. What is your experience with REST APIs? How would you version one without breaking existing clients?
  11. How do you decide which new technology is worth learning versus which one to skip?
  12. What does a good code review look like to you, both as the author and as the reviewer?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk me through a project you built end-to-end.

*Situation:* My team was losing time manually checking Slack threads to track deployment statuses across several microservices.
*Task:* I was asked to own the solution from initial design through to delivery.
*Action:* I built a lightweight dashboard in Node.js and React that polled our CI/CD webhooks and displayed real-time status per service. I documented the API contract first, got two teammates to review it, then built in short sprints with a mid-point demo to keep the team aligned.
*Result:* Deployment-related Slack noise dropped noticeably. The team adopted it within a week and it became part of our standard runbook.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate on a technical approach.

*Situation:* A senior colleague wanted to rewrite a legacy module in a new framework mid-sprint, just before a release.
*Task:* I needed to raise my concern clearly without stalling the team or creating conflict.
*Action:* I asked for a short slot to document the risk: timeline impact, untested edge cases, and the cost of rollback if something broke. I shared the write-up before the discussion so everyone could read it calmly rather than reacting on the spot.
*Result:* The team agreed to schedule the rewrite for a later sprint after the release. The release shipped on time and the rewrite went smoothly with proper test coverage in place.

---

Q: Describe a bug that took you a long time to find.

*Situation:* A payment service was occasionally processing duplicate transactions, but only under high load.
*Task:* I was the on-call engineer and needed to find and fix the root cause before the next business day.
*Action:* I added detailed trace logging, replayed load test scenarios, and found that a retry mechanism was firing twice when the network timed out just after a write had already succeeded. I added idempotency keys to prevent duplicate writes.
*Result:* Duplicate transactions stopped completely. I wrote a postmortem and a checklist for testing retry logic, which the team now uses as a standard step for any new external integration.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use STAR for any behavioural question. STAR stands for Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep your Situation brief (one or two sentences), spend most of your time on Action (specifically what you did, not what the team did), and always close with a concrete Result. If you have no metric, describe the qualitative change clearly.

Use 'Clarify, then Design' for system design questions. Before drawing any architecture boxes, ask two or three clarifying questions: expected scale, read-vs-write ratio, latency requirements. Candidates report that interviewers value this habit because it mirrors how engineers actually scope real work.

Use 'Problem, Root Cause, Fix, Prevention' for debugging questions. This structure shows you think beyond the immediate patch. Mention any monitoring or alerting you added so the same failure cannot quietly recur.

For 'how do you stay updated' questions, name two or three specific sources (newsletters, open-source projects, engineering blogs from product companies) and explain how you decide whether something is production-ready. Vague answers like 'I read tech blogs' do not stand out. Specificity signals genuine curiosity.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on patterns candidates report from Software Engineer interviews at similar companies, evaluators are typically looking for the following:

Communication above all. Can you explain a technical decision to someone who was not in the room? Candidates who think aloud and narrate their reasoning are easier to assess and easier to endorse internally.

Clear ownership. Use 'I designed' or 'I decided' rather than 'we built'. Interviewers want to understand your specific contribution to a project, not the team's collective output.

Practical judgment, not just textbook knowledge. Knowing algorithm complexity matters, but knowing when to use a simple approach over a clever one matters more at mid and senior levels.

Comfort with ambiguity. Engineers who ask good clarifying questions and make explicit, reasoned assumptions are valued over those who freeze when a problem is underspecified.

Collaboration signals. How you talk about past teammates, how you respond when corrected, and whether you show genuine curiosity about the team's work all factor into a hiring decision, particularly in final rounds.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Reframe your experience
Rewrite your top three resume projects as STAR stories. Practice explaining each one in under three minutes. Record yourself and watch it back at least once. Most candidates skip this step entirely, and it shows in the actual interview.

Week 2: Technical fundamentals
Solve one or two coding problems daily, focusing on arrays, strings, hashmaps, and trees. These categories cover most of what candidates report being asked at this level. For mid and senior roles, add one system design session each day using the 'Clarify, then Design' approach from this guide.

Week 3: Company and role research
Review San Hire's LinkedIn page and any publicly available job descriptions. Prepare two genuine questions about the team's current technical challenges, not just compensation. Interviewers notice when a candidate has actually done their homework.

Week 4: Simulate real conditions
Do at least two mock interviews with a peer or via a practice platform. Focus on narrating your thinking out loud, not just arriving at the answer silently. On the day itself, keep your best STAR stories written on paper nearby for quick reference during phone or video calls.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping the clarifying question. Jumping straight into a solution without confirming scope is the most commonly reported reason candidates lose points in design rounds. Ask one or two quick questions before you start building.

Using 'we' for everything. Interviewers cannot evaluate 'we built the API.' Replace it with something specific: 'I designed the endpoint contract and my teammate handled the database schema.'

Ending your STAR answer at Action. Stories that stop at 'and then I fixed it' leave the interviewer guessing. Always close with what changed: the release shipped, complaints dropped, the team adopted the approach.

Over-preparing for coding, under-preparing for behaviour. Many candidates spend weeks on algorithmic problems and show up unprepared for questions about conflict, failure, or collaboration. Budget equal time for both categories.

Saying you have no questions at the end. Prepare two genuine questions about the team or the tech stack. It takes five minutes and signals real interest in the role.

Framing past employers negatively. Even if a company had real problems, interviewers read it as a warning sign about you. Reframe every difficult past experience as a lesson you took away or a challenge you navigated constructively.

How many rounds does a San Hire Software Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically runs 2-3 rounds, though this can vary by seniority and the specific team involved. A common pattern includes an initial screening call, a technical round covering coding or system design, and a final discussion round. Always confirm the exact format with the recruiter after your first call so you can prepare accordingly.

What salary can I expect for a Software Engineer role at San Hire?

Salary data specific to San Hire is not available in sufficient volume to cite a firm number. Across the broader market, industry surveys show entry-level Software Engineers (0-2 years) at 6-12 LPA, mid-level (3-5 years) at 15-25 LPA, and senior engineers (6-9 years) at 28-45 LPA. Use these as a reference point and negotiate based on your actual offer and the scope of the role you are applying for.

Does San Hire focus more on algorithms or practical engineering skills in interviews?

Based on what candidates report from similar-profile companies, interviews tend to lean toward practical problem-solving and project discussion rather than pure competitive-programming puzzles. That said, basic knowledge of data structures (arrays, hashmaps, trees) is expected at every level. Prepare for both and let the interviewer's questions guide the depth of your technical answers.

How should I approach system design if I am applying at an entry level?

Entry-level candidates are typically not expected to produce a production-ready system design. What matters is showing that you can break a problem into components, ask sensible clarifying questions, and explain your tradeoffs out loud. Practice designing one simple system (a URL shortener, a basic notification service) using the 'Clarify, then Design' approach described in this guide.
