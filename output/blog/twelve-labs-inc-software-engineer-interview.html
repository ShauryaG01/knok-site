01 Overview

Overview

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR method for all behavioural and project questions. Below are three worked examples.

---

Q: Tell me about a distributed system you built or significantly improved.

*Situation:* At my previous company, our video transcoding service processed uploads sequentially. Each job queued behind the last, and the growing backlog caused users to abandon the product shortly after uploading content.

*Task:* I was asked to redesign the pipeline to reduce end-to-end processing time without rebuilding the entire service from scratch.

*Action:* I introduced a message queue to decouple ingestion from processing, spun up multiple worker pods in Kubernetes, and added a priority lane for shorter clips. I also set up dead-letter queues so failed jobs would surface for inspection instead of disappearing silently.

*Result:* Average processing time dropped materially (an internally tracked metric, not a public figure), silent failures fell to near zero, and the team adopted the same pattern for two other pipelines that same quarter.

---

Q: Describe a technically ambiguous problem you solved without a clear spec.

*Situation:* We were asked to build a 'relevance scoring' layer for search results, but no one had defined what 'relevance' meant for our specific content type.

*Task:* My job was to propose and prototype a definition that the product and ML teams could align on before we built anything permanent.

*Action:* I ran a two-day internal annotation exercise where three team members rated a sample of search results. I used that signal to draft a scoring rubric, then built a small evaluation harness to score two candidate ranking approaches against it.

*Result:* The team aligned on an approach in one week instead of debating for a month. The rubric became the baseline for all future model evaluations on that product.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you caught a serious bug before it reached production.

*Situation:* During a code review, I noticed our API was serialising user IDs as integers, but our database used string UUIDs. On small test data this problem was invisible.

*Task:* I needed to assess the blast radius and fix the issue without blocking the team's release.

*Action:* I wrote a script to verify ID consistency across tables, patched the serialiser, added a contract test to the CI pipeline to catch type mismatches automatically going forward, and updated the API documentation.

*Result:* The fix merged cleanly and the contract test later caught two similar type-mismatch issues in other services, as noted in team retro discussions.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the backbone for any story-based question. Keep each component tight: one to two sentences for Situation and Task, three to four sentences for Action (the technical substance), and one concrete Result. Avoid vague outcomes like 'things improved' and instead tie results to something observable or measurable from your own work context.

For system design questions, use a layered approach. Start with clarifying questions about scale, latency requirements, and consistency needs. Then sketch a high-level architecture verbally: client, API layer, processing layer, storage, and any async queues. Next, go deeper on the one or two components the interviewer seems most interested in. Finish by explicitly naming trade-offs, since engineers at a product-focused AI company are expected to reason about the cost of design decisions, not just make them.

For 'what do you know about X' questions (like vector embeddings or how transformers work), lead with a plain-language definition, give a concrete example from Twelve Labs' product domain, and then honestly acknowledge the edges of your knowledge. Saying 'I have not worked with this specific library but I understand the concept and here is how I would ramp up' reads better than overclaiming expertise you do not have.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Twelve Labs is a small, product-focused AI company. Based on what candidates report, interviewers are typically looking for a few specific qualities.

Genuine curiosity about video and AI. This is not a role where you can treat the model as a black box. Interviewers want to see that you have thought about how models work, what their failure modes are, and how you would build systems around them that handle errors gracefully.

Comfort with ambiguity. Early-stage startups move fast and change direction. Candidates who have navigated unclear requirements or rebuilt systems mid-flight tend to stand out over those who have only worked in well-specified environments.

End-to-end ownership. Twelve Labs engineers are expected to care about the full stack, from data ingestion to API response. Showing that you have shipped things end to end, not just owned one isolated layer, signals the right mindset.

Clear written and spoken communication. With a distributed team, explaining a technical decision clearly in writing is as important as coding ability. Candidates report that even how you write up a take-home solution is part of the evaluation.

Collaborative instinct. Culture conversations often centre on how you handle disagreement, give feedback, and support teammates in a high-ownership, low-process environment.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Coding Fundamentals
Revise core data structures and algorithms with a focus on arrays, hash maps, graphs, and heaps. These come up most often in coding rounds at AI startups. Practice consistently, aiming for a few problems each day on a platform of your choice.

Week 2: System Design for Scale
Study how to design scalable data pipelines, API gateways, and message queue systems. For Twelve Labs specifically, spend time understanding vector databases, how embeddings are stored and searched, and how you would serve low-latency queries over large indexes. Read publicly available engineering write-ups on large-scale media processing.

Week 3: AI and Multimodal Concepts
You do not need to be an ML researcher, but you should be able to explain what embeddings are, how similarity search works, and what a transformer does at a conceptual level. Spend time with Twelve Labs' public documentation and any technical blog posts they have published to understand how their product is actually built.

Week 4: Behavioural and Communication Practice
Prepare six to eight STAR stories from your past experience. Cover: a technically ambiguous project, a hard debugging story, a disagreement with a teammate, a system you designed end to end, and a time you improved a slow or broken process. Practice saying them out loud, not just writing them down.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping clarifying questions in system design. Jumping straight into a solution before confirming scale and constraints is one of the most common ways candidates lose points. Always ask about expected load, latency targets, and data volume before drawing any architecture.

Treating AI as a black box. At a video AI company, saying 'we just call the model and use the output' is not enough. Be ready to discuss how you would validate model outputs, handle edge cases, and build systems that degrade gracefully when the model produces bad results.

Vague results in STAR answers. Saying 'the project went well' or 'users were happier' without any anchor makes it hard for interviewers to assess your actual impact. Reference team retros, internally tracked signals, or observable outcomes from your own experience.

Overclaiming on AI or ML experience. If you have not worked directly with multimodal models, say so clearly and pivot to what you do know. Interviewers at small AI companies typically have deep expertise and will probe hard if something sounds rehearsed or exaggerated.

Not researching the product before the interview. Candidates who have not used or read about Twelve Labs' API before their interview stand out in a negative way. Spend time with their public documentation and be ready to mention one specific feature or design decision that genuinely interested you.

Underestimating async communication. Since the engineering team is distributed, how clearly you explain your thinking in writing, even in a follow-up email or take-home submission, is part of the evaluation. Write as if your reader cannot ask you a follow-up question.
