02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up frequently in UI/UX Designer interviews at product companies like Berg Group. Prepare a specific, story-based answer for each one.

  1. Walk us through a project in your portfolio where you identified and solved a complex user problem from scratch.
  2. How do you approach user research when time and budget are limited?
  3. Describe a time you had to convince stakeholders to change a design decision. How did you frame the argument?
  4. How do you balance business requirements with user needs when they pull in opposite directions?
  5. Tell us about a design that failed or did not perform as expected. What went wrong and what did you change?
  6. How do you hand off designs to developers and ensure the final product stays close to your intent?
  7. What design tools do you rely on most, and how do you decide which tool to use for a given project?
  8. Describe your process for running usability tests. How do you turn findings into actionable changes?
  9. How do you stay current with accessibility standards and inclusive design practices?
  10. Tell us about a time you worked with a product manager whose priorities conflicted with your design goals.
  11. If you had two weeks to redesign a core feature in one of our products, how would you approach it?
  12. Describe a situation where you had to design for multiple platforms or screen sizes at the same time.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time you had to convince stakeholders to change a design decision.

*Situation:* At my previous role, the product team had signed off on a navigation layout that, after moderated usability sessions, visibly confused users trying to complete key tasks.

*Task:* I needed to present my findings and propose a revised structure without making the team feel their work had been dismissed.

*Action:* I compiled short clips from three usability sessions showing specific moments of confusion and mapped those moments to drop-off points in existing analytics. I then presented two alternative layouts, each with a clear rationale tied to user goals. I framed the conversation around our shared objective, not around who was right.

*Result:* The team agreed to test the revised layout. Task completion improved noticeably in the follow-up round, and the PM began looping me into sprint decisions earlier.

---

Q: Tell us about a design that did not perform the way you expected.

*Situation:* I redesigned the onboarding flow for a mobile app, confident that reducing the number of steps would lift completion rates.

*Task:* Post-launch, completion rates dropped compared to the original. I had to diagnose the problem quickly and propose a fix.

*Action:* I ran a fresh set of user interviews and discovered that removing contextual tooltips had left new users uncertain about what the app actually did for them. I restored a lightweight contextual layer and ran an A/B test against the stripped version.

*Result:* Completion rates recovered and exceeded the earlier baseline. More importantly, I built a practice of validating simplification assumptions with a small cohort before committing to a full rollout.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you worked with a PM whose priorities differed from yours.

*Situation:* During a feature sprint, the PM wanted to ship a form with many required fields to capture maximum data upfront. My research showed that users dropped off on long mobile forms.

*Task:* I needed to find a middle ground that served the data collection goal without hurting completion.

*Action:* I proposed progressive disclosure: show only the essential fields first, then prompt for optional information after the primary action was complete. I referenced industry surveys showing that shorter initial forms tend to produce higher completion, and mocked up both versions so the PM could see the experience difference directly.

*Result:* The PM agreed to the phased approach. Completion improved after launch, and the PM started involving me in scoping conversations earlier, before feature specs were finalised.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Three frameworks help UI/UX candidates structure clear answers under pressure.

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the baseline for every behavioural question. Keep Situation and Task to two or three sentences each. Spend most of your time on Action and Result. Quantify results where you honestly can, but do not invent numbers. If you do not have metrics, explain how you measured or would measure impact.

Design Thinking Walkthrough is the right frame when interviewers ask about your process. Walk through: empathise (research and discovery), define (framing the problem), ideate (exploring solutions), prototype (low to high fidelity), and test (usability evaluation). Always tie each stage back to a specific decision you made in a real project.

Trade-off Framing works well for questions about balancing business goals and user needs. Name the tension explicitly, explain what data or principles informed your choice, and describe what happened. Interviewers want evidence that you can reason through constraints, not just design in ideal conditions.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Candidates who do well in Berg Group UI/UX interviews typically demonstrate a consistent set of qualities.

Portfolio storytelling over polished pixels. Interviewers want to understand your reasoning, not just admire your final screens. Be ready to explain why you made specific decisions, what alternatives you considered, and what you would do differently with more time or data.

Comfort with ambiguity. Design roles at product companies often begin with a loosely defined problem. Show that you can scope a brief yourself, identify what you need to learn first, and move forward without a complete specification.

Collaboration track record. Candidates report that interviewers pay close attention to how you describe working with developers, PMs, and business stakeholders. Avoid language that positions design as superior to other functions. Show that you influence through evidence and conversation, not by overruling.

Accessibility and cross-platform awareness. Expect at least one question about designing for users with different abilities or for multiple device types. Being able to reference WCAG principles and speak to mobile-first thinking is a clear differentiator.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused two-week plan covers the key areas without burning you out before interview day.

Week one: portfolio and core skills

Curate three to five case studies you can walk through verbally in about five minutes each. For each, prepare to explain the problem, your research approach, the key design decisions and why you made them, and the measurable or observable outcome. Be ready to discuss what you would do differently now, not just what went well.

Review your primary design tool in depth. Berg Group roles typically involve collaborative design files, so fluency in shared libraries, auto-layout, and handoff workflows matters. If you use Figma, make sure you are comfortable with the developer mode and component documentation features.

Refresh your knowledge of WCAG accessibility principles at a practical level. You do not need to memorise every criterion. Focus on contrast, touch target sizing, focus states, and how you have applied these in real work.

Week two: company research and mock interviews

Explore Berg Group's publicly visible products or marketing. Form a specific opinion on one feature you would improve and one design choice you respect. Interviewers notice when a candidate has clearly done this homework.

Do at least two mock interviews aloud, either with a peer or recorded for self-review. Look for vague answers, overuse of 'we' without specifying your personal contribution, and moments where you led with aesthetics rather than user outcomes.

Prepare three to five questions to ask the interviewer. Strong ones focus on team structure, how design decisions get escalated or challenged, what a successful first few months looks like, and how design and engineering collaborate day to day.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Claiming sole credit for collaborative work, or hiding your contribution behind 'we.' Interviewers listen for both. Be precise: say 'I led the research and handed wireframes to the visual designer' rather than claiming the full project or describing everything as a team effort.

Leading with aesthetics instead of outcomes. Saying 'I made it look cleaner' is a weak answer. Ground every design decision in a user need, a business constraint, or something you learned from research or testing.

Turning a failure question into a humble-brag. Interviewers see through answers like 'my biggest weakness is that I care too much.' Pick a real failure, explain specifically what went wrong, and describe what changed in your practice as a result.

Bringing a portfolio but not knowing the impact. If you mention that a redesign improved an outcome, be ready to explain where that data came from and what the baseline was. If you genuinely do not have metrics, say so and explain how you would measure it in a future project.

Rushing the design exercise brief. Candidates report that a common reason for early elimination is ignoring stated constraints in take-home or live exercises. Read the brief carefully, acknowledge any trade-offs you are making deliberately, and show your reasoning at every step.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many interview rounds does Berg Group typically have for UI/UX Designer roles?

Candidates report a process that typically includes an initial screening call, a portfolio review or design exercise, and one or two rounds of conversations with the team. The exact number varies by seniority and which team you are joining. Confirm the structure with your recruiter at the start so you can plan your preparation accordingly.

Is there a take-home design assignment or live design exercise?

Candidates for UI/UX roles commonly report some form of design task, either a take-home exercise or a live session in Figma or on a whiteboard. Typically you will be given a product or feature problem and asked to walk through your solution. Focus on showing your reasoning process, not just a polished final design, since interviewers weight thinking over output.

What tools should I know before the interview?

Figma is the most commonly cited design tool in Indian product companies and is typically expected at this level. Familiarity with collaborative design workflows, component libraries, and developer handoff will help. Knowing your primary tool deeply is more valuable than listing many tools you use superficially.

What should I focus on most if I only have a few days to prepare?

Prioritise your portfolio walkthroughs above everything else. Pick your two or three strongest case studies and practise explaining the problem, your decisions, and the outcome clearly in under five minutes each.
