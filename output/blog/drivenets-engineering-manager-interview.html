01 Overview

Overview

DriveNets builds software-defined, disaggregated networking software for major telecom and cloud providers worldwide. The Engineering Manager role sits at the intersection of deep technical ownership and people leadership: you manage engineers working on high-performance distributed systems, own delivery for carrier-grade software, and coordinate across product, QA, and infrastructure teams. With 41 open Engineering Manager roles at DriveNets currently, the company is in a significant hiring push for engineering leadership.

Candidates typically experience a multi-round process covering behavioral leadership, technical judgment, and cross-functional collaboration. The interview is known to be rigorous, with strong emphasis on how you handle ambiguity and drive delivery in a fast-moving networking product company. Based on knok jobradar data for this role category, Engineering Manager compensation ranges from 35-60 LPA at the Manager level and 55-90 LPA at the Senior Manager level.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These are the questions candidates most commonly report from DriveNets Engineering Manager interviews. The focus is on technical credibility alongside leadership maturity.

  1. DriveNets builds large-scale distributed networking software. Describe a time you led a team working on complex, low-level or performance-sensitive systems. How did you keep quality high under pressure?
  2. How do you balance technical debt against new feature delivery when your product is carrier-grade and reliability is non-negotiable?
  3. How have you hired or grown engineers in a niche technical domain like networking protocols, distributed systems, or systems programming?
  4. Describe a situation where your team was blocked by a dependency on another team or an external vendor. How did you resolve it?
  5. DriveNets operates in a rapidly evolving market. How do you keep your engineers current with industry shifts like network disaggregation or software-defined networking trends?
  6. Walk us through how you set goals for your team and measure progress across a quarter. What does a good quarter look like?
  7. Tell us about a time a key project missed its deadline. What went wrong and what did you change afterward?
  8. How do you handle disagreement between senior engineers on an architectural or technical decision?
  9. How do you work with product managers and customers to translate vague or shifting requirements into clear engineering work?
  10. Describe your approach to performance management. How do you support a struggling engineer without letting it affect the rest of the team?
  11. How do you create a culture of reliability and ownership in a team building software that operators depend on around the clock?
  12. DriveNets has a fast-paced, startup-like culture. How do you keep your team motivated through uncertainty or shifting priorities?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time you led a team working on complex, low-level systems.

*Situation:* My team was responsible for a high-performance packet processing module in a distributed network platform. We were integrating a new forwarding plane component with strict latency requirements, and two engineers on the team were new to systems-level programming.

*Task:* I needed to deliver the integration on schedule without compromising reliability, and bring the junior engineers up to speed without slowing the senior ones down.

*Action:* I paired each new engineer with a senior architect for the first sprint, introduced mandatory design reviews before any code was written, and ran weekly deep-dive sessions where the team walked through tricky areas together. I also worked with the product manager to protect the team from scope changes during the critical integration phase.

*Result:* We delivered on schedule with zero critical defects in the first month of production. Both junior engineers became confident contributors, and we documented the integration patterns for use on future modules.

---

Q: Tell us about a time a project missed its deadline.

*Situation:* A major firmware integration project slipped by three weeks because a third-party vendor delivered incomplete API documentation late in the development cycle.

*Task:* I had to manage the impact, communicate transparently with leadership, recover the timeline where possible, and keep the team motivated through the uncertainty.

*Action:* I escalated to the vendor relationship owner immediately and negotiated a parallel track where we built against documented assumptions while waiting for final specs. I restructured the sprint plan to front-load testable deliverables so stakeholders could see progress, and I held a mid-project retrospective rather than waiting until the end.

*Result:* We recovered two of the three weeks and shipped with one week of delay. The retrospective produced a vendor dependency checklist that the team now runs on every external integration.

---

Q: How do you handle disagreement between senior engineers on an architectural decision?

*Situation:* Two senior engineers on my team disagreed strongly on whether to use a message-queue architecture or direct RPC calls for inter-service communication in a new module.

*Task:* I needed to facilitate a decision without picking sides and ensure both engineers remained engaged and respected after the outcome.

*Action:* I asked each engineer to write a one-page brief covering trade-offs, failure modes, and operational complexity. I then ran a structured discussion where we evaluated each option against agreed criteria: latency, operational overhead, and long-term maintainability. I also invited a neutral architect from another team to offer an outside view.

*Result:* The team converged on a hybrid approach that neither engineer had originally proposed. Both felt heard, and the decision had clear documented reasoning that we referenced months later when extending the system.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioral questions: Structure every leadership story as Situation (context and stakes), Task (your specific responsibility), Action (the steps you took, focusing on your own choices), Result (outcome and what you learned). Keep each section to 2-3 sentences and aim for 2-3 minutes per answer total.

Technical judgment questions: When asked about a trade-off or architectural decision, use a three-part structure: first, name the competing priorities; second, describe how you gathered input from engineers, product, and data; third, explain how you made and communicated the final call. Avoid vague answers like 'I bring the team together' without specifics on what that looked like in practice.

Ambiguity questions: Show that you take action rather than wait for clarity. Describe how you break the problem down, identify the first decision that unblocks the team, and communicate your reasoning to stakeholders. DriveNets interviewers typically want to see bias toward action over lengthy alignment processes.

Growth and hiring questions: Lead with outcomes. Instead of describing your process in the abstract, anchor your answer to a specific engineer you hired, developed, or helped grow into a more complex role. Concrete evidence of team-building in niche technical domains lands far better than general process descriptions.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

DriveNets interviewers are typically senior engineers and directors with deep technical backgrounds. They are not just evaluating whether you can manage people: they want to see genuine technical credibility so you can earn the respect of engineers working on complex, high-performance networking software.

Technical depth: You do not need to be a networking expert, but you should understand distributed systems, software reliability, and the trade-offs involved in building carrier-grade software. Be ready to discuss architecture decisions you have personally owned and what you would do differently in hindsight.

Ownership and accountability: DriveNets values a 'build it, own it' culture. Candidates who describe taking personal ownership of delivery, quality, and team health, rather than delegating accountability upward, tend to resonate well with interviewers.

People development: The company is growing rapidly and needs managers who can hire well and grow engineers in niche technical domains. Come prepared with specific examples of engineers you have mentored, promoted, or successfully onboarded into complex technical work.

Cross-functional credibility: Engineering Managers at DriveNets work closely with product, QA, and sales engineering. Candidates who demonstrate smooth cross-team delivery and the ability to influence without direct authority are valued highly.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Company and product context. Read DriveNets public materials on network disaggregation and their core product. Understand what a Distributed Disaggregated Chassis does and why telecom operators care about it. You do not need to be a networking engineer, but you should speak intelligently to why the problem DriveNets solves is technically hard and competitively relevant.

Week 2: Leadership story bank. Write out 8-10 STAR stories covering missed deadlines, technical conflict resolution, team building in niche domains, cross-functional alignment, and performance management. Candidates report that behavioral questions are weighted heavily across all rounds.

Week 3: Technical leadership scenarios. Practice talking through architectural trade-offs at a high level. Review concepts like distributed system consistency, reliability engineering, and how you set technical direction for a team. Be ready to walk an interviewer through a past technical decision in detail, including what you would change in hindsight.

Week 4: Mock interviews and calibration. Run mock interviews with a peer who can give honest feedback. Time your STAR answers, aiming for 2-3 minutes each. Review the job description line by line and map a concrete story to each key responsibility listed.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping the technical layer. Some candidates prepare only behavioral stories and are caught off-guard when asked about system design trade-offs or technical decisions they have personally owned. DriveNets expects Engineering Managers to be technically credible, not just process coordinators.

Vague leadership claims. Saying 'I create a culture of collaboration' without a concrete story will not land. Every leadership claim needs a specific example with a measurable result attached.

Underestimating cross-team complexity. Candidates sometimes focus only on their direct team and miss questions about managing external dependencies, influencing product decisions, or working with vendors. Come prepared with cross-functional examples that show you can deliver without full control over all the pieces.

Not asking good questions. Interviewers at this level typically expect candidates to ask thoughtful questions about engineering challenges, team structure, and product direction. Generic or absent questions signal low engagement with the role.

Over-indexing on process. DriveNets is not a pure process company. Candidates who lead with Agile ceremony descriptions and sprint metrics, and underweight technical judgment and delivery ownership, can come across as mismatched for the culture.
