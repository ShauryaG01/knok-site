01 Overview

Overview

Glance is a lock-screen content discovery platform built by InMobi. It delivers short videos, news, games, and live content directly to the lock screen of Android phones, reaching a massive audience across India and parts of Southeast Asia. As of mid-2026, Glance had 39 open roles listed on knok jobradar, pointing to active hiring across product and design teams.

A UI/UX Designer at Glance typically works on mobile-first experiences where content must grab attention before the user even unlocks their phone. The role blends interaction design, visual craft, and data-informed iteration. Because Glance serves vernacular audiences across tier-2 and tier-3 cities, designers are expected to think beyond assumptions built around metro power users.

Candidates report the interview process typically includes a portfolio review call, one or two design exercises or take-home case studies, a cross-functional panel with product and engineering, and a final leadership conversation. Rounds and their sequence can vary, so confirm the structure with your recruiter before the first call.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Here are three example answers built around questions Glance interviewers commonly ask.

Q: Walk us through a project where you designed for users with limited digital literacy.

*Situation:* At my previous company, I was redesigning the onboarding flow for a regional language app targeting first-time smartphone users in smaller towns.

*Task:* I owned the end-to-end onboarding experience, and early research showed many users had never downloaded an app independently before.

*Action:* I replaced text-heavy instruction screens with icon-based steps, conducted paper prototyping sessions with participants in two towns, and iterated on what users could navigate without any assistance.

*Result:* Task completion in usability tests improved noticeably, and engineering flagged it as one of the smoothest handoffs they had seen. The pattern was later adopted for two other onboarding flows in the product.

---

Q: Tell me about a data point that completely changed your design direction.

*Situation:* We were building a content recommendations widget and I had designed a horizontal scroll carousel as the default interaction.

*Task:* I owned the interaction design and assumed users would swipe through options to explore.

*Action:* Session recordings showed most users never scrolled past the first card. I brought this to the PM, ran a quick A/B test with a vertical list format, and presented the findings in the next design review.

*Result:* The vertical list outperformed the carousel on click-through, and the pattern became the default across the product for similar widgets.

---

Q: Describe a time your design was challenged by engineering constraints.

*Situation:* I had designed a parallax scroll animation for a content card that the team loved in review sessions.

*Task:* Engineering confirmed the animation caused visible frame drops on entry-level devices, which made up a large share of our user base.

*Action:* I worked with the front-end lead to understand what was technically feasible, simplified the animation to a clean fade-in, and reworked the visual hierarchy so the static version felt like a deliberate choice rather than a compromise.

*Result:* The card shipped on schedule, performed well in a phased rollout, and the approach became a reference point for how the team handled animation trade-offs going forward.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Having a clear structure helps you give confident, focused answers without rambling.

For design process questions, use a four-part flow: (1) how you framed the problem, (2) what you learned from research or data, (3) how you made key design decisions, (4) how you measured or validated the outcome. Glance interviewers look for evidence that you tie design decisions to user behaviour, not just visual instinct.

For 'how would you redesign X' questions, start with constraints before solutions. State what you would want to know (user goals, current drop-off points, device profile), sketch a few directions, then pick one and defend it with reasoning. Showing your thinking process matters more than arriving at a single 'correct' answer.

For behavioural questions, the STAR format works well: Situation (one or two sentences of context), Task (what you were responsible for), Action (the specific steps you took, not 'we'), Result (a concrete outcome, even a qualitative one). Keep the Situation short and spend most of your time on Action and Result.

For metric and success criteria questions, anchor on user behaviour first (did users do the thing the feature was meant to enable?), then business outcome. Name specific metrics like session length on a card, swipe-back rate, or content completion rate. Avoid vague answers like 'user satisfaction improved.'

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Glance interviewers are typically looking for a few things beyond a clean portfolio.

Mobile-first instinct. Can you explain why a design decision works differently on a phone screen versus a desktop? Glance is a mobile-only product and reviewers notice quickly when candidates default to web-centric thinking.

Comfort designing for India's real users. This means thinking about low-bandwidth conditions, vernacular text that expands layout, and users who have varying levels of experience with touch interfaces. Candidates who treat metro urban users as the default stand out for the wrong reasons.

Data literacy. You do not need to be an analyst, but you should be able to talk about how you used data or qualitative research to make or revise a decision. Candidates who can describe a specific metric they tracked, even from a small user test, tend to stand out.

Collaboration without ego. Glance teams are cross-functional, and interviewers probe for how you handle pushback from engineering or product. They want to see that you can hold a design position with clear reasoning while staying open to constraints.

Portfolio depth, not breadth. Two or three well-explained case studies with clear problem framing and measured outcomes will land better than ten projects with surface-level walkthroughs.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused one-to-two week plan for a Glance UI/UX Designer interview.

Days 1-2: Understand the product deeply. Use Glance on an Android device if possible. Note what content types appear, how cards are laid out, how transitions feel, and where you see friction. Write down three things you would change and why. This feeds directly into redesign questions.

Days 3-4: Prepare your portfolio stories. Pick two or three projects that show mobile design, research-driven decisions, or work for a diverse or non-metro Indian audience. Structure each one using the four-part flow: problem framing, research, decisions, outcome. Practice saying each story out loud in under five minutes.

Days 5-6: Sharpen your STAR answers. Write out answers for at least six behavioural questions from the list above. Focus on specifics: what you did (not 'the team'), what happened, and what you learned. Avoid answers that stay too abstract.

Days 7-8: Practice a design exercise. Pick a real Glance feature and redesign one part of it. Timebox yourself to a fixed window of about an hour. Then walk through your decisions as if presenting to the team, explaining trade-offs out loud, including what you chose not to do and why.

Day 9: Prepare your questions. Glance interviewers appreciate candidates who ask about design process, how research is conducted, or how the team measures design quality. Avoid asking only about compensation in early rounds.

Day 10: Light review and rest. Re-read your portfolio notes, confirm the interview format with your recruiter, and get a good night's sleep.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

These are the most common ways candidates underperform in Glance design interviews, based on what interviewers and candidates have shared.

Jumping to visuals before understanding the problem. When asked to redesign something, many candidates go straight to layout ideas. Interviewers want to hear you frame the problem first: who is the user, what are they trying to do, what is currently failing for them?

Speaking in 'we' throughout the portfolio. If an interviewer cannot tell what you personally did on a project, they cannot evaluate your skills. Use 'I' for your contributions and give clear credit elsewhere when needed.

Ignoring device and network constraints. Describing a design without acknowledging that many Glance users are on mid-range or entry-level devices with variable data speeds signals a gap in mobile thinking. Always tie design choices to real-world conditions.

Weak or missing outcomes. Ending a case study with 'the project launched' is not enough. Even a qualitative outcome, like 'user interviews showed people found it easier to navigate', is better than no result at all. Prepare at least one specific thing that happened after your design shipped.

Over-polished slides, under-explained decisions. Glance interviewers care more about your reasoning than your Figma output. A simple wireframe you can explain well will land better than a pixel-perfect mockup with no rationale behind it.

Not asking clarifying questions during a design exercise. Jumping in without clarifying scope, constraints, or user context looks like overconfidence. Ask two or three grounding questions before you start designing.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Glance UI/UX Designer interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically involves three to four rounds: a portfolio review with a design lead, one or two case study or design exercise rounds, and a final round with a senior leader or cross-functional panel. The exact structure can vary by team and level, so ask your recruiter to confirm what to expect before your first call.

Does Glance give a take-home design exercise?

Many candidates report receiving a take-home case study or a timed design problem as part of the process, though this is not universal. The brief typically asks you to redesign a feature or solve a user problem relevant to content discovery or mobile engagement. Treat it as a chance to show your thinking process, not just a polished final output.

What should my portfolio include for a Glance interview?

Prioritise two or three mobile-focused case studies that show how you moved from a user problem to a measured outcome. Projects that involve diverse Indian users, low-end device constraints, or multilingual UI will resonate well with the Glance context. Each case study should clearly state the problem, your specific contribution, the decisions you made, and what happened after launch.

What salary can I expect for this role at Glance?

Glance does not publish standard pay bands publicly. Based on Glassdoor and levels.fyi data, mid-level UI/UX Designer salaries at mid-to-large Indian tech companies are commonly cited in a range of 12-25 LPA, varying by experience and the specific level you are hired at. Research comparable roles on Glassdoor before entering any compensation conversation, and be ready to share your current or expected range.

How important is Figma proficiency for this role?

Glance design teams, like most modern product teams, typically use Figma for day-to-day work. You should be comfortable building components, creating interactive prototypes, and collaborating within a shared design system. That said, interviewers are more focused on your thinking and decision-making than on your Figma speed, so do not let tool proficiency overshadow your ability to explain your design choices.
