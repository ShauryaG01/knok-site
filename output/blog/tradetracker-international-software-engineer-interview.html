02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates who have interviewed at companies in the affiliate marketing and ad-tech space report questions across three areas: backend engineering, data system design, and behavioural fit for remote-friendly, cross-functional teams.

  1. Walk us through a backend service you built from scratch. What technology choices did you make and why?
  2. How would you design a click-tracking system that handles a large spike in traffic without dropping events?
  3. Describe a time you improved the performance of a slow API or database query. What was your approach?
  4. How do you handle a situation where a business deadline conflicts with doing the engineering properly?
  5. Tell us about a production incident you helped debug. How did you find the root cause?
  6. Have you worked with event-driven architectures or message queues? Give a concrete example from your work.
  7. How do you approach code reviews? What do you look for, and how do you give feedback to teammates?
  8. Describe a time you had to pick up an unfamiliar technology quickly. How did you get up to speed?
  9. How would you design a reporting dashboard that aggregates conversion data across many advertisers in near real time?
  10. Tell us about a project where you worked closely with a non-technical stakeholder. How did you manage expectations?
  11. How do you decide when to refactor legacy code versus leaving it as is and shipping the feature?
  12. What is your experience with containerisation or cloud deployments? Walk us through something you set up yourself.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

The three answers below use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result). Adapt each to your own experience before the interview.

Q: Describe a time you improved the performance of a slow API.

*Situation:* At my previous company, our advertiser reporting API was timing out regularly during peak hours, causing dashboard failures that account managers were escalating daily.

*Task:* I was asked to investigate and fix the problem within two weeks, without a major architectural overhaul since a full rewrite had not been approved.

*Action:* I profiled the endpoint and found it was running several unindexed queries against a large events table. I added composite indexes on the most-queried columns, introduced a caching layer for frequently requested date ranges, and rewrote one heavy query to use a pre-aggregated summary table already being populated by a nightly batch job.

*Result:* API response time dropped from several seconds to well under a second for typical requests. Dashboard load failures stopped appearing in our alerting channel, and the account team reported a noticeably smoother experience.

---

Q: Tell us about a production incident you helped debug.

*Situation:* A publisher integration started silently dropping conversion events after a routine deployment. We noticed only when a publisher flagged lower-than-expected payouts two days later.

*Task:* I was part of the on-call rotation and took ownership of the investigation.

*Action:* I compared logs from before and after the deployment and found that a config change had altered an expected field name in the incoming webhook payload, causing the parser to discard events silently instead of raising an error. I rolled back the config change, added a validation step that logs a warning on unexpected payload shapes, and created a task to add an automated integration test for this path.

*Result:* Event ingestion recovered immediately after the rollback. The publisher was compensated based on historical averages for the affected window. The validation step we added caught a similar payload drift three months later before it caused any data loss.

---

Q: Describe a project where you worked closely with a non-technical stakeholder.

*Situation:* Our product manager wanted a 'top publishers' feature on the advertiser dashboard. The brief described the desired output but did not specify ranking logic, refresh frequency, or how to handle ties.

*Task:* I had to bridge the gap between what the PM wanted to display and what our data and system could actually support, without delaying the release.

*Action:* I set up a short working session with the PM and walked through two or three ranking options using sample outputs. I explained the trade-offs in plain language (accuracy versus refresh speed, for example), and we agreed on a daily pre-computed ranking with a clear tie-breaking rule. I documented the agreed logic before writing a single line of code.

*Result:* The feature shipped on schedule with no post-launch confusion about ranking behaviour. The PM used the same documentation approach when briefing two subsequent features.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions. Every 'tell me about a time' question maps to: Situation (context in one or two sentences), Task (your specific responsibility), Action (what you personally did, not the team), and Result (a concrete outcome). Keep the Situation brief and spend most of your time on Action and Result.

Structured approach for system design. Candidates typically do well by covering these steps in order: clarify requirements and scale assumptions, sketch the high-level components, discuss the data model, identify bottlenecks, then propose solutions. TradeTracker's domain involves high write throughput and eventual consistency trade-offs, so be ready to discuss queuing strategies and data aggregation approaches specifically.

Narrate your mental model for debugging questions. Interviewers want to see how you think, not just the correct answer. Describe what signals you look at first (logs, metrics, recent deployments), how you form a hypothesis, and how you confirm or rule it out. Avoid jumping straight to the fix.

Show business context for trade-off questions. Questions like 'refactor now vs ship fast' are not purely technical. Show that you weigh engineering quality against delivery risk and team bandwidth. Interviewers at product companies value engineers who understand the business reason behind a trade-off, not just the technical one.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates report from interviews in the ad-tech and performance marketing space, interviewers typically look for several qualities beyond raw coding ability.

Comfort with data-heavy backend systems. The core product processes large volumes of events: clicks, conversions, and impressions. Engineers who have worked with event pipelines, message queues, or aggregation systems stand out over those with only CRUD application experience.

Ownership and follow-through. Multiple interview reports mention questions about production incidents handled end-to-end. Interviewers want to see that you fixed the immediate problem, addressed the root cause, and improved the system to prevent recurrence.

Clear communication across functions. TradeTracker works with publishers and advertisers who are not engineers. Candidates who can explain technical trade-offs in plain language and collaborate with non-technical stakeholders without becoming a bottleneck tend to do well.

Pragmatism over perfectionism. The company operates across many markets and needs engineers who ship. Candidates who show they can balance code quality with delivery speed, and who have a clear framework for making that call, come across as strong.

Curiosity about the domain. Affiliate marketing and performance tracking are niche areas. Interviewers notice when a candidate has made the effort to understand how the product works before walking into the interview.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

One week before the interview:

Read TradeTracker's public product pages to understand how their affiliate network works: how publishers earn commissions, how click events are tracked, and how conversions are attributed back to publishers. You do not need expert-level knowledge, but being able to reference how the product works signals genuine interest.

Revise system design fundamentals with a focus on high-write-throughput architectures: message queues, caching strategies, database indexing, and eventual consistency trade-offs. These topics map directly to TradeTracker's technical domain.

Prepare three to five STAR stories from your own experience. Cover at least: a performance improvement, a production incident, and a cross-functional collaboration. Have concrete, observable outcomes ready for each.

Two to three days before the interview:

Practice one or two live coding problems in your preferred language, focusing on clean and readable code rather than clever tricks. Review data structures commonly used in event processing: hash maps, priority queues, and sliding window approaches.

Prepare two or three genuine questions to ask the interviewer. Good options include questions about the team's current technical challenges, how on-call responsibilities are structured, and how the team handles technical debt.

Day of the interview:

Have your coding environment ready if it is a live round. For system design, have a blank canvas or diagramming tool open. Speak your reasoning out loud throughout, even when you are still thinking. Interviewers give partial credit for a sound process even when the final answer is incomplete.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Starting to code before clarifying the problem. Candidates often lose credit by diving into implementation before confirming scope and edge cases. Taking a brief pause to restate the problem and check your assumptions is always worth it.

Vague STAR answers. Saying 'we improved the system' without any observable detail leaves interviewers unconvinced. If you cannot share exact metrics, describe the before and after in concrete terms: 'the page used to time out under load; after the fix it was stable through our peak hours.'

Ignoring the domain. Treating TradeTracker as a generic software company and making no reference to affiliate marketing or tracking systems is a missed opportunity. Even a brief and accurate reference to their business model shows preparation.

Talking about 'we' instead of 'I'. Behavioural questions ask what you did. If every answer is about the team, interviewers cannot evaluate your individual contribution. Be specific about your role, your decisions, and your actions.

Arriving with no questions. Candidates who have nothing to ask at the end come across as disengaged. Prepare at least two genuine questions about the role, the engineering challenges, or the team's working style.

Overcomplicating system design. A common mistake is proposing a highly complex architecture before meeting the basic requirements. Start with the simplest design that works, confirm the interviewer is satisfied, and then layer in complexity where they agree it is needed.
