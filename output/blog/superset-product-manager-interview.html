01 Overview

Overview

Superset is a curated hiring platform built for the Indian market, connecting fresh graduates and early-career professionals with funded startups and high-growth companies. The internal Product team owns a two-sided marketplace covering candidate discovery, employer dashboards, assessment tooling, and matching logic. A PM at Superset works at the intersection of job-seeker experience and employer outcomes, balancing the needs of both sides while moving core marketplace metrics.

As of mid-2026, Superset has 9 open Product Manager roles. The broader market tracked by knok jobradar shows 2,009 active PM openings across India, with Bangalore leading at 271 roles and Delhi at 177. Demand for PMs remains strong at product-led companies.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a product decision you made that was driven primarily by data.

*Situation:* At my previous company, our mobile app had a significant drop-off at the profile completion step. Analytics showed a large share of users who installed the app never completed their profiles, yet profile completion was required before they could apply to any role.

*Task:* My task was to understand why this drop-off was happening and propose a change that would improve completion rates without reducing the quality of information collected.

*Action:* I pulled funnel data and ran a cohort analysis to identify which fields caused the most abandonment. I followed this with five user interviews to add qualitative context. Both sources pointed to the same problem: a long free-text 'career summary' field that most users left blank or rushed through. I proposed replacing it with a structured prompt-based input built around three focused questions. I wrote the product spec, worked with design on the revised flow, and aligned engineering on a two-sprint delivery. We A/B tested the new flow against the original before rolling out fully.

*Result:* The cohort using the new flow showed a meaningfully higher profile completion rate over the test period. Downstream application quality also improved, which hiring managers flagged in their feedback. The experiment became a template for how the team approached form redesign going forward.

---

Q: A feature you shipped is tracking below its success metric. How do you decide whether to iterate, pivot, or remove it?

*Situation:* I had shipped a 'Saved Searches' feature for job seekers on a hiring platform. Three months after launch, active usage was well below the target set at launch.

*Task:* I needed to make a clear recommendation: invest more in iterating, change the feature direction, or deprecate it entirely.

*Action:* I separated the metrics before drawing any conclusion. Active usage was low, but among users who had found the feature, re-engagement and retention signals were strong. This told me the core value was real but the discovery path was broken. I traced the entry points and found the feature was buried two taps deep in a settings menu instead of being surfaced on the job search results page, where the use case was most relevant. I checked support tickets and found several users who had stumbled onto it by accident and found it very useful. I brought this framing to the team: this was a placement failure, not a product failure. We agreed on a one-sprint fix to move the entry point and add a contextual prompt on the search page, with a short reassessment window.

*Result:* Adoption improved visibly within the first two weeks after repositioning. The feature was removed from the deprecation list. The experience reinforced a useful rule: low adoption is not always a signal to kill a feature. Sometimes it is a signal to fix the path to it.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you had to resolve a conflict between two senior stakeholders over roadmap priorities.

*Situation:* The head of sales wanted a bulk-invite feature for enterprise clients because it kept coming up in sales conversations. The growth lead wanted focus on improving the candidate recommendation engine, arguing that early data pointed to it as the bigger retention lever.

*Task:* I had to make a prioritisation call with limited engineering capacity and avoid damaging the working relationship between two teams that both had legitimate business cases.

*Action:* I set up individual conversations with each stakeholder before any group meeting, focusing on their underlying goal rather than the specific feature request. Sales needed to close two large accounts before end of quarter. Growth needed to show improved early retention in their quarterly review. I mapped both requests against our north-star metric, estimated rough impact versus effort for each using available analytics, and found a sequencing that avoided a full trade-off. A lightweight bulk-invite version could ship in one sprint and cover the immediate sales need. The recommendation engine work would follow in the next two sprints with full engineering focus. I documented this reasoning and shared it with both stakeholders before the planning session so there were no surprises.

*Result:* Both teams agreed to the plan. Sales closed one of the target accounts using the lightweight feature. Growth got their work prioritised with clear engineering commitment. The key was showing each stakeholder that the sequencing was driven by business timing and data, not by whose request was louder.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Four frameworks come up most often in Superset PM interviews. Knowing when to reach for each one will help you structure answers clearly without sounding mechanical.

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result): Use this for every behavioural question. Interviewers at product-led companies want specifics. Vague answers about 'collaborating with cross-functional teams' do not land. Lead with the context, state your specific role and the decision you faced, describe exactly what you did, then close with the measurable or observable outcome.

Users, Goals, Metrics opener: When given a product design or improvement question, start by stating who the users are (job seekers vs. employers at Superset), what each group is trying to achieve, and what success looks like in concrete terms. This demonstrates product thinking before you jump to solutions and signals that you understand the two-sided marketplace dynamic.

Impact vs. Effort prioritisation: For roadmap and trade-off questions, place options on an impact-effort grid and explain your assumptions out loud. Superset interviewers will push back on your assumptions, so treat this as a dialogue rather than a presentation. The ability to defend and adjust your assumptions calmly is part of what they are testing.

Root cause decomposition: For diagnostic questions ('hire rates are down', 'candidates are not converting'), break the problem into a funnel or a tree before proposing anything. For example: employer hire rate = (jobs posted) times (candidates surfaced) times (shortlist rate) times (interview-to-offer rate) times (offer acceptance rate). Isolate which stage is broken before suggesting a fix. Jumping to solutions without this step is one of the most common mistakes candidates make.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Candidates who have interviewed at Superset and similar hiring-tech companies typically report that interviewers look for a specific set of signals.

Two-sided marketplace thinking. Can you hold the job seeker's problem and the employer's problem in your head simultaneously? Solutions that help one side at the expense of the other are a red flag. Show that you understand where the two sides have aligned interests and where they diverge.

Comfort with ambiguity alongside data. Superset operates in a fast-moving space with imperfect information. Interviewers want to see that you can make a reasonable decision with the data available, while being honest about what you do not yet know. Candidates who wait for perfect data, and those who ignore data entirely, both raise concerns.

Structured communication. PMs at a hiring platform work across engineering, sales, growth, and operations teams. Interviewers watch for whether you can explain a complex trade-off clearly, in plain language, to someone who does not share your context. Favour concise logical structure over jargon.

Genuine product curiosity. Candidates who have actually used Superset, explored the job seeker flow, and noticed something concrete they would change tend to stand out. Generic statements about 'caring about users' without a specific observation from the product itself are unconvincing.

Ownership language. Interviewers typically probe for whether you treat your work as something handed to you or as a problem you personally own until it is resolved. Use 'I decided', 'I pushed back on that approach', 'I owned the outcome' rather than 'the team worked on it' or 'we tried to address it'. This is professional precision about your contribution, not arrogance.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused two-week approach that candidates report finding useful for Superset PM interviews.

Week 1: know the product and the space. Use Superset as a job seeker and explore the employer-side experience if possible. Map out the core flows: sign-up, profile creation, job discovery, application submission, and employer candidate review. Note three specific things you would change and be ready to explain why. Read about two-sided marketplace fundamentals, focusing on liquidity, matching quality, and disintermediation risk. Look at how competitors like Unstop and Cutshort position themselves and where Superset is distinctly different.

Week 2: practise structured answers. Work through all 12 questions in this guide out loud, not just in your head. Use STAR for every behavioural question. Time yourself and aim for two to three minutes per answer. For product design and metrics questions, practise drawing a funnel or framework on paper before verbalising it. Get at least one mock interview with someone who will give honest feedback on structure, specificity, and clarity.

Day before the interview. Review Superset's recent product updates or any publicly available announcements about new features. Prepare two or three questions to ask the interviewer about their roadmap priorities, team structure, or the biggest challenge they are currently working through. Asking sharp questions signals product maturity and genuine interest.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating both sides of the marketplace as one user. Job seekers and employers have different needs, and at several points those needs are in direct tension. Candidates who describe a solution without acknowledging this tension come across as having thought about only half the product. Always ask: how does this change affect each side?

Jumping to solutions before diagnosing the problem. In case questions, many candidates skip the funnel breakdown and go straight to feature ideas. Interviewers flag this as weak analytical thinking. Always spend at least a minute framing the problem space and identifying which part of the funnel is broken before proposing anything.

Giving generic STAR answers. 'I collaborated with cross-functional teams to deliver a feature on time' is not a STAR answer. It is a job description. Name the specific decision you made, the specific data point that drove it, the specific pushback you navigated, and the specific outcome.

Not having used the product. A surprising number of candidates say they are excited about Superset without having actually used it. If you cannot describe the sign-up flow or name one concrete thing you would improve, you have not done the baseline preparation.

Skipping the measurement step in design questions. Every product question should close with: 'Here is how I would know whether this worked.' If you do not close the loop on metrics, interviewers assume you ship and move on without learning.

Underselling individual ownership. Many candidates default to 'we' out of modesty. In a PM interview, use 'I' when describing decisions and actions you personally took. Clarity about your specific contribution is exactly what interviewers are listening for.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many interview rounds does Superset typically have for PM roles?

Candidates report that the process typically runs across three to four rounds, though the exact count varies by seniority level and the specific team hiring. Rounds commonly include a product thinking discussion, a case study or take-home assignment, a behavioural or stakeholder round, and a culture or leadership conversation. Confirm the specific format with your recruiter once you enter the process, as round names and sequences are not standardised across all roles.

What salary can I expect for a PM role at Superset?

Superset does not publicly report detailed compensation bands at scale, so use industry benchmarks as your reference. Mid-level PM roles with 3-6 years of experience are commonly cited in the 24-40 LPA range, while senior PM roles are publicly reported at 40-60 LPA for the Indian market. Your specific offer will depend on the seniority of the role, your prior experience, and the equity component, so negotiate with the full picture in mind.

Does Superset give a take-home assignment during the PM interview process?

Some candidates report receiving a take-home product case or analysis task, particularly for mid to senior-level roles. Typically it involves designing a feature or working through a product problem within a set time limit. Treat it like a real deliverable: be structured, show your metrics thinking, and state your assumptions explicitly rather than pretending to have complete information. Interviewers often probe the assumptions you made as much as the solution itself.

How should I approach a two-sided marketplace case question at Superset?

Start by identifying the two users clearly: job seekers and employers, each with different goals and measures of success. Map the core marketplace funnel before proposing any solution, covering supply of candidates, demand from employers, match quality, and the conversion steps in between. Any solution you propose should either improve both sides or clearly articulate the trade-off you are accepting. Practise talking through a funnel breakdown out loud so it feels natural in the interview room.

Is prior experience in hiring tech required to apply for a PM role at Superset?

Not necessarily. Candidates from consumer product, SaaS, and general marketplace backgrounds outside of HR tech have joined PM teams at companies like Superset. What matters more is showing that you understand two-sided marketplace dynamics and that you have done genuine preparation on Superset's specific product. Being able to speak concretely about the platform, rather than about hiring tech in general, often signals readiness more clearly than a matching industry background.

How do I show genuine product interest without just saying 'I love the product'?

The most effective signal is being specific about something you actually observed while using Superset. Describe a friction point in the job seeker flow, a gap in the employer experience, or a comparison to a competitor that reveals a concrete opportunity. Noting that search filters do not surface roles by company funding stage is far more memorable than a general statement about loving the product. Specificity is what distinguishes a prepared candidate from an enthusiastic one.
