03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Design a real-time event pipeline that handles a large volume of events per day.

*Situation:* At my previous company, our ad-event ingestion pipeline was dropping events during traffic spikes because it relied on a single Kafka cluster with no backpressure handling.

*Task:* I was asked to redesign the pipeline to be fault-tolerant and horizontally scalable, with a target of zero event loss.

*Action:* I moved ingestion to a multi-AZ Kafka setup on AWS MSK, added a Kinesis buffer as an overflow layer, and wrote consumer services in Go that published to S3 with idempotent writes. I added dead-letter queues for failed events and built alerting in CloudWatch to surface consumer lag early.

*Result:* Event loss dropped to zero over the following quarter. The pipeline handled a significant traffic surge during a major campaign period without any manual intervention.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you reduced cloud costs significantly.

*Situation:* Our team noticed that AWS bills had grown sharply over several months without a matching growth in product usage.

*Task:* I was asked to audit spending and propose cuts that would not affect production reliability.

*Action:* I used AWS Cost Explorer and Trusted Advisor to identify over-provisioned EC2 instances, unused EBS volumes, and data transfer charges from cross-region calls. I rightsized a large portion of our instances, moved infrequently accessed S3 data to Glacier, and rerouted cross-region traffic through VPC peering.

*Result:* Monthly AWS spend dropped by a meaningful amount, which I reported to the engineering manager. The changes were applied without any production incidents.

---

Q: Describe a time you improved reliability for a critical service.

*Situation:* A core attribution service had a strict uptime SLA, but we were seeing multiple unplanned outages each month due to dependency failures.

*Task:* My job was to identify root causes and implement changes that would make the service self-healing.

*Action:* I added circuit breakers using a service mesh, introduced health-check-based auto-scaling in EKS, and set up synthetic monitoring to catch degradation before customers noticed. I also ran chaos engineering drills to validate the fixes.

*Result:* Unplanned outages fell to zero over the following two months. The SLA was consistently met, and the on-call burden dropped noticeably for the team.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task short (two to three sentences). Spend most of your time on Action, since that is where your technical depth shows. Quantify the Result where you can, but be honest if exact numbers are not available.

C4 or box-and-line for system design: When asked to design a pipeline or architecture, start by clarifying scale requirements (events per second, latency targets, durability needs). Then sketch the layers: ingestion, processing, storage, and serving. Name specific AWS services and explain your choices. Discuss tradeoffs explicitly, for example why you chose Kafka over SQS for a particular use case.

PREP for open-ended questions: Point, Reason, Example, Point again. Useful when asked 'how do you approach X'. Lead with your answer, explain why, give a real example, then restate your point briefly.

Debugging narrative: When asked how you debug a spike or incident, follow a structured path. Observe (what metrics or logs surfaced the issue). Hypothesise (what could cause this pattern). Isolate (narrow to a service or component). Fix (apply the smallest change that addresses the root cause). Prevent (what monitoring or code change would catch this earlier). Interviewers want to see methodical thinking, not guesswork.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

AppsFlyer runs infrastructure at a scale that few engineering teams in India experience day to day. Interviewers are typically looking for engineers who have dealt with real production complexity, not just theoretical knowledge.

Scale instinct: Can you reason about what happens at a very high event volume? Do you think about throughput, backpressure, and partial failures without being prompted?

AWS depth: Candidates report that interviewers probe beyond surface-level knowledge. Knowing that a service exists is not enough. Expect follow-up questions on why you chose one service over another and what the tradeoffs are.

Ownership mindset: AppsFlyer values engineers who treat infrastructure as a product. Show that you have cared about reliability, cost, and developer experience, not just uptime metrics.

Security awareness: IAM policies, secrets management, and network segmentation come up regularly. Demonstrate that security is a default concern, not an afterthought.

Communication: System design rounds are partly about how clearly you can explain complex architecture decisions to a mixed technical audience. Think out loud and invite feedback.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know the product and the platform

Read AppsFlyer's engineering blog (search for 'AppsFlyer tech blog' to find it). Focus on posts about their data infrastructure, attribution pipeline, and any cloud migrations they have documented publicly. Understand what their mobile attribution product does, what their analytics suite covers, and how their anti-fraud product works at a high level. This context will help you frame your answers around their actual problems.

Week 2: Deepen AWS and distributed systems knowledge

Review core AWS services relevant to data pipelines: Kinesis, MSK (managed Kafka), ECS, EKS, Lambda, S3, RDS, and Aurora. Practise explaining tradeoffs between them out loud. Study distributed systems concepts: exactly-once delivery, idempotency, consumer group lag, and partition tolerance.

Week 3: System design practice

Practise designing two to three large-scale systems out loud or on paper. Good scenarios: a real-time clickstream pipeline, a multi-tenant analytics store, and a zero-downtime deployment system. Time yourself and focus on clarifying requirements before drawing any boxes.

Week 4: Behavioural stories and mock interviews

Write out STAR stories for five to six situations: a reliability improvement, a cost reduction, a difficult technical decision, a disagreement with a teammate, and a time you influenced a team without formal authority. Practise them until they feel natural rather than memorised.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping the scale conversation: Candidates sometimes jump into architecture without asking about throughput, latency targets, or durability requirements. This signals a gap in experience. Always clarify scale before designing.

Name-dropping services without tradeoffs: Saying 'I would use Kafka' is incomplete. Interviewers want to know why Kafka and not Kinesis or RabbitMQ for this specific scenario.

Vague STAR answers: Saying 'we improved performance' without specifics is unconvincing. Even if you cannot share exact company figures, you can say 'latency dropped significantly' or 'the team rated the improvement as high-impact in the retrospective.'

Ignoring security and cost: Some candidates design for performance and reliability but forget IAM policies, encryption at rest, or cost implications. AppsFlyer interviewers notice this gap.

Over-preparing for coding, under-preparing for system design: Candidates report that system design and cloud architecture carry more weight than LeetCode-style coding at this level. Balance your preparation accordingly.

Not asking questions: Interviewers at companies like AppsFlyer expect senior engineers to treat the interview as a two-way conversation. Prepare two to three thoughtful questions about their infrastructure challenges or engineering culture.
