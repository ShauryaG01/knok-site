01 Overview

Overview

Navi Technologies is one of India's newer fintech companies, known for instant personal loans, health insurance, and mutual fund investments delivered entirely through its app. Because the product is fully digital, the UI/UX Designer role carries real weight: you shape how users borrow money, file claims, or invest, often for the very first time. The design team works closely with product managers and engineers to keep flows intuitive for users who may be new to financial apps, while meeting RBI compliance requirements.

As of July 2026, knok jobradar tracked 29 open UI/UX Designer roles across India, and Navi itself has 60 open positions, signalling active growth. Most openings are in Delhi (8) and Bangalore (6). Candidates report the process typically includes a portfolio review, a take-home design assignment, and one or two functional interview rounds, though the exact structure can vary by team and seniority level.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Here are the questions candidates report most often in Navi UI/UX Designer interviews.

  1. Walk us through a project in your portfolio where you improved a transactional or financial flow.
  2. How do you design for users who may be first-time smartphone users or have low digital literacy?
  3. The Navi app needs to show a loan approval in seconds. How would you design that moment to delight the user while keeping them informed?
  4. How do you balance visual simplicity with the legal disclosures a financial product must show?
  5. Describe your process for creating or contributing to a design system.
  6. How do you approach user research when timelines are tight and you cannot run full usability studies?
  7. Tell us about a time you disagreed with a product manager on a design decision. How did you resolve it?
  8. How would you redesign the Navi loan application flow to reduce user drop-offs?
  9. What accessibility considerations do you build into your designs by default?
  10. How do you measure whether a design is working after it ships?
  11. Navi operates in a regulated space. How do you handle design constraints from compliance or legal teams?
  12. Walk us through how you would run a discovery sprint for a new insurance product feature.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through a project where you improved a transactional or financial flow.

*Situation:* At a previous role, the KYC completion screen had a high drop-off rate that was blocking users from accessing the app's core features.
*Task:* I was asked to redesign that flow within a two-week sprint.
*Action:* I started by reviewing session recordings to find exactly where users stopped. I then spoke with five users to understand their hesitation. Based on that research, I simplified the visible fields, added a progress indicator, and rewrote error messages in plain language. I tested two prototypes before the final handoff to engineering.
*Result:* The redesigned flow shipped and the team saw a measurable improvement in completion. The chunking pattern I introduced became a reusable component in our design system.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you disagreed with a product manager on a design decision.

*Situation:* A PM wanted to place four promotional banners on the home screen right after login to cross-sell products.
*Task:* I believed the banner stack would overwhelm users and hurt core task completion, so I needed to make that case with evidence, not just opinion.
*Action:* I proposed a quick test with five existing users. I built both versions as a clickable prototype and let users narrate their experience aloud. The sessions showed that users in the banner-heavy version missed the primary action button. I shared a short document with the findings and proposed a single contextual recommendation instead.
*Result:* The PM agreed to the single-recommendation approach. Post-launch feedback from customer support showed fewer confused user queries, which the team took as a clear positive signal.

---

Q: How do you design for users who may be first-time smartphone users or have low digital literacy?

*Situation:* While working on a lending product, user testing showed that a segment of applicants struggled with form inputs and did not understand what 'NACH mandate' meant.
*Task:* I needed to make the loan disbursement and repayment setup understandable without removing any required information.
*Action:* I introduced step-by-step tooltips with plain-language explanations, replaced jargon with everyday words, and added illustrative icons next to each input. I worked with the copy team to write in a conversational tone, and we tested comprehension with users from tier-2 cities before launch.
*Result:* Comprehension scores in follow-up tests improved, and users reported feeling more confident completing the form. The approach shaped how the team wrote onboarding copy for subsequent features.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Most Navi design questions fall into three types, and each rewards a slightly different structure.

Portfolio walkthroughs work best when you lead with the problem, not the solution. State what the user was struggling with, then explain your process before showing the final screens. Interviewers want to see how you think, not just what you shipped.

Hypothetical redesign questions (such as 'How would you improve our loan flow?') call for a discovery-first approach. Start by asking clarifying questions: Who is the user? What does success look like? What constraints exist? Then outline a process rather than jumping to solutions. Candidates report that interviewers at fintech companies value structured thinking over visual flair.

Behavioural questions about conflict, feedback, or failure respond well to the STAR structure: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep the Situation short (two sentences), spend most time on Action, and close with a concrete Result. Avoid vague outcomes like 'the team was happy.' Tie the result to user behaviour, a process change, or feedback you received.

Compliance and constraint questions are common in fintech. When asked how you handle legal or regulatory limits, acknowledge the constraint first, then explain how you work within it creatively rather than treating it as a blocker.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Navi's design interviewers typically look for a few specific qualities beyond raw craft skill.

User empathy grounded in research. Navi serves users across income levels and digital literacy levels. Interviewers want to see that you have actually spoken to users, not just assumed what they want.

Comfort with constraints. Fintech design is full of non-negotiables: regulatory disclosures, identity verification steps, repayment schedules. Candidates who treat these as creative challenges rather than obstacles stand out.

Systems thinking. Navi has multiple products (loans, insurance, investments) that share a design language. Interviewers look for designers who think in components and patterns, not just individual screens.

Clear communication. You will present your work to non-designers, including engineers and product managers. How well you explain your decisions matters as much as the decisions themselves.

Ownership and speed. Candidates report that Navi moves quickly. Interviewers value people who can make decisions with imperfect information and iterate, rather than waiting for perfect research before acting.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused two-week plan covers most of what Navi interviewers look for.

Week 1: Audit your portfolio and research Navi

Pick three to four projects that show your design process end to end, not just final screens. At least one should involve a complex flow such as onboarding, a multi-step form, or a payment journey. Download the Navi app and use it. Note what works, what feels clunky, and what you would change. Read recent coverage of Navi's product launches to understand their direction.

Week 2: Practice and assignment prep

Practice presenting each portfolio project out loud in under ten minutes. Record yourself once to catch filler words. If the role involves a take-home assignment (candidates report this is common at Navi), treat it like a real sprint: start with a clear problem statement, show your research approach even if brief, and annotate your final screens to explain each decision.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Showing only final screens, not the process. Navi interviewers want to know how you think. A polished Figma file with no explanation of why you made each decision will not land well. Always narrate your reasoning.

Ignoring the fintech context. Candidates who present consumer app portfolios without connecting them to financial products, trust, or compliance miss an opportunity. If your portfolio is not from fintech, bridge the gap explicitly during your presentation.

Being vague about outcomes. 'The project went well' is not a result. Tie your outcomes to something observable, even qualitative: user quotes, a drop in support tickets, or a change in team process.

Over-designing the take-home assignment. Candidates report spending too many hours on pixel-perfect visuals when interviewers want to see structured thinking. Annotate your decisions. A clear rationale beats a polished mockup with no explanation.

Not asking clarifying questions. When given a hypothetical scenario, jumping straight to solutions without clarifying the user, the goal, or the constraints signals shallow thinking. Interviewers want to see a discovery mindset.

Underselling collaboration. Design at Navi is cross-functional. If you only talk about your individual contributions without mentioning how you worked with PMs, engineers, or researchers, you miss part of what they are evaluating.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Navi UI/UX Designer interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically involves three to four rounds, often including a portfolio presentation, a take-home design assignment, and one or two functional discussions. The exact number can vary by team and seniority. It is a good idea to ask your recruiter upfront so you can plan your schedule.

Is there a take-home design assignment in the Navi interview process?

Most candidates report receiving a take-home design task, typically a problem related to a financial product flow or a redesign challenge. The assignment is usually time-boxed. Focus on showing your thinking process through annotations and a clear problem statement rather than spending all your time on visual polish.

What salary can I expect for a UI/UX Designer role at Navi?

Navi has not publicly disclosed salary bands for this role. Glassdoor and levels.fyi list salaries for UI/UX Designers at Indian fintech companies, but these are self-reported and vary by experience and specialisation. Ask the recruiter for the band early in the process so you are not surprised at the offer stage.

Do I need fintech experience to apply for a UI/UX Designer role at Navi?

Not necessarily. Candidates from consumer apps, e-commerce, and other digital product backgrounds do get through. What matters more is showing that you can design for complex user journeys, handle constraints, and empathise with users making financial decisions. If your portfolio is not from fintech, bridge the gap explicitly during your presentation.

What design tools does Navi's team use?

Candidates report that Figma is the primary tool used for design and collaboration at most Indian product companies. Be comfortable with component libraries, auto-layout, and prototyping in Figma. Familiarity with how designs are handed off to engineers is also a common point of discussion in interviews.

How should I prepare my portfolio for the Navi interview?

Pick three to four projects that show end-to-end design thinking, from research to final screens. At least one should involve a complex multi-step flow. For each project, be ready to explain the problem, your process, what you tried that did not work, and the outcome. Interviewers at product-focused companies care more about your reasoning than the visual quality of the final screens.
