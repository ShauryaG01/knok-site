02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up frequently at AI-product companies like OneShot AI, based on what candidates report and the nature of the role:

  1. Walk me through your experience with test automation frameworks. Which ones have you used and why did you choose them?
  2. How would you design a test plan for a new AI-powered feature where the output is not always the same?
  3. Explain the difference between functional and non-functional testing. Give an example from a project you have worked on.
  4. How do you handle flaky tests in a CI/CD pipeline? What steps do you take to investigate and fix them?
  5. Describe your approach to API testing. What tools do you use and what do you actually verify?

  10. Tell me about a time you found a critical bug late in the release cycle. What happened and what did you learn?
  11. How do you collaborate with developers to shift testing left in the development process?
  12. What metrics do you use to measure the health of a test suite?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a time you found a critical bug late in the release cycle.

*Situation:* I was the only QA engineer on a mobile payments feature that was two days from release. During a final regression run, I noticed transaction amounts were being rounded incorrectly for values above a certain threshold.

*Task:* I had to document the bug clearly, assess its severity, and communicate the risk to the team without causing panic, since the deadline was already tight.

*Action:* I wrote a detailed bug report with exact reproduction steps, screenshots, and the specific conditions that triggered the rounding error. I escalated it directly to the tech lead with a clear severity assessment and also suggested a possible fix location based on where I had seen similar issues before.

*Result:* The developer fixed it within a few hours. We delayed release by one day, caught the issue before it reached users, and the PM later said the clear bug report made the difference between a quick fix and a prolonged investigation.

---

Q: How do you design a test plan for a feature with non-deterministic output, like an AI response?

*Situation:* At my previous company, we launched a summarisation feature powered by an LLM. Traditional pass/fail test cases did not apply because the output changed every run.

*Task:* I needed a test strategy that gave the team real confidence without asserting exact output strings.

*Action:* I shifted to property-based checks instead of exact value assertions. I wrote tests that verified the response was within an expected length range, contained key topic words from the input, had no personally identifiable information, and returned within the agreed latency. I also set up a manual review checklist for edge cases that automated checks could not catch.

*Result:* The team shipped with confidence, and we caught two important regression issues in later sprints because the property-based checks flagged outputs that drifted outside acceptable ranges.

---

Q: Describe a time you had to prioritise testing under a deadline.

*Situation:* Our sprint ended on a Friday and a release was scheduled for Monday morning. A last-minute requirement change added scope but the timeline stayed the same.

*Task:* I had roughly two days to cover testing that would normally take four, including new features and full regression.

*Action:* I mapped every test case to a risk level and split them into: critical paths that had to pass, medium-risk areas that could be spot-checked, and low-risk areas I could defer with a documented note to the PM. I focused automation on the stable regression layer so it could run overnight, freeing my manual time for the new changes.

*Result:* We released on time with zero critical defects in production. Two minor UI issues appeared post-release, but I had already flagged them as accepted risk in my sign-off document, so the team was not surprised.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For technical questions about your process, use a brief context, approach, tool, outcome structure. Name the specific tool or framework you used, explain why you chose it, and close with a concrete result. Vague answers like 'I used Selenium for automation' land flat. Say instead: 'I used Selenium with TestNG because the team was already on Java, and I cut manual regression time by restructuring the suite to run in parallel.'

For scenario questions (how would you test X), show your thinking out loud. Interviewers at AI companies care about how you break down an ambiguous problem. Start by clarifying assumptions, then describe your coverage strategy, then mention the edge cases or failure modes you would prioritise.

For behavioural questions, STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) works well. Keep the Situation and Task brief together, spend most time on the Action, and close with a specific and honest Result. If the result was not perfect, say what you learned. Interviewers at product companies value honesty over polished storytelling.

For questions about AI or non-deterministic testing, show you understand the difference between testing deterministic and probabilistic systems. Talk about property-based testing, coverage of input distributions, latency and reliability checks, and human-in-the-loop review for cases automated checks cannot handle.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Automation skill is table stakes. For an AI product company, comfort with at least one automation framework (Selenium, Playwright, Cypress, or similar) and the ability to write clean test code is expected. If your background is mostly manual testing, be upfront and show a clear plan to close the gap.

Curiosity about AI-specific quality problems. OneShot AI builds AI-powered tools, so interviewers typically want to see that you have thought about what 'quality' means for AI outputs. How do you test a feature that gives different answers every time? What does a good AI output look like versus a bad one? Prepare at least one concrete example or framework for this.

Communication with developers. QA engineers at small AI companies rarely sit behind a ticket queue. Interviewers typically look for someone who can work closely with developers, catch issues early in the sprint, and write bug reports that are clear and actionable.

Comfort with ambiguity. Startups and AI companies often have incomplete specs, fast-moving requirements, and shifting priorities. Candidates who can describe how they handled unclear requirements or last-minute changes tend to perform better than those who only describe working in ideal conditions.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1, days 1-3: Core testing concepts. Review test design techniques (equivalence partitioning, boundary value analysis, exploratory testing). Practice writing test cases for simple features from scratch. Make sure you can clearly explain the difference between unit, integration, and end-to-end tests.

Week 1, days 4-7: Automation and tooling. Pick one automation framework you know well and practice writing a small test suite from scratch. Be ready to discuss CI/CD integration, how you manage test data, and how you keep tests from becoming brittle. If you have not done API testing, spend a session with Postman or RestAssured.

Week 2, days 1-3: AI-specific testing prep. Read about property-based testing and testing LLM-powered features. Prepare two or three talking points on how you would approach testing a chatbot or a recommendation system. You do not need to be an AI expert, but showing you have thought about it sets you apart from candidates who have not.

Week 2, days 4-5: Company and role research. Look up OneShot AI on LinkedIn, explore their product, and note what their AI tool actually does. Prepare a question or two about how the QA team is structured and what the biggest quality challenges are for their product.

Day before the interview: Review your STAR stories. Have three or four ready covering: finding a critical bug, handling a deadline crunch, improving a test process, and working closely with developers. Do a mock interview out loud, not just in your head.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Claiming AI testing expertise without evidence. Interviewers at AI companies have heard candidates oversell their experience. If your background is mostly traditional web or mobile QA, say so honestly and show you have researched the differences.

Giving tool names without depth. Saying 'I know Selenium, Playwright, and Cypress' is less convincing than walking through one tool you have used deeply. Pick your strongest tool and be ready to go deep on it.

Writing test cases only for happy paths. In technical rounds that ask you to write test cases on the spot, many candidates forget edge cases, error states, and boundary conditions. Show your thinking by asking 'what are the ways this could go wrong?'

Not asking any questions at the end. Skipping the 'do you have questions?' section signals low interest. Prepare two or three genuine questions about the team, the product, or what success looks like in the first few months.

Being vague about results in STAR answers. 'The project went well' is not a result. 'We released on time and had zero critical defects in the first week' is a result. If you do not have numbers, describe the outcome in specific and concrete terms.

Underestimating the coding component. Even if the role is not a pure SDET position, many AI product companies ask you to write test code or read existing automation scripts. Practice writing simple test functions before the interview.
