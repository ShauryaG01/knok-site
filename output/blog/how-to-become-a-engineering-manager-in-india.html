01 Role Overview

Role Overview

An Engineering Manager (EM) leads a team of software engineers, balancing technical oversight with people management. You own three things: delivery (getting features out on time), talent (hiring, mentoring, and growing your engineers), and strategy (turning business goals into an engineering roadmap). This is not a purely technical role, but you need enough technical depth to earn your team's trust and make good trade-off decisions.

As of July 2026, knok jobradar tracks 975 active Engineering Manager openings across India. Bangalore leads with 182 openings, followed by Delhi (53), Pune (20), Chennai (20), Hyderabad (16), and Mumbai (12). Demand is strongest at product companies and Global Capability Centres (GCCs).

02 Skills You Need

Skills You Need

People management is the core of the job. You need to run effective 1:1s, give honest feedback, write performance reviews, and have career conversations that actually help your engineers grow. Conflict resolution, both within your team and across teams, is something you will deal with every week.

Hiring and team building matters more than most new managers expect. You will write job descriptions, run structured interviews, calibrate candidates with your panel, and build a team that works well together. In India, this also means navigating a competitive talent market where strong engineers often hold multiple offers at the same time.

Technical credibility keeps you effective. You do not need to write production code daily, but you must understand system design well enough to review architecture decisions, ask the right questions in design reviews, and unblock engineers who are stuck. If you check out of technical conversations entirely, your team will feel it quickly.

Stakeholder communication is the hidden skill many senior ICs underestimate. Engineering Managers in India often work with product managers, business stakeholders, and sometimes external clients. You translate between technical reality and business timelines, say no when you need to, and keep relationships intact while doing it.

Delivery and process rounds out the picture. Agile facilitation, sprint planning, roadmap negotiation, and incident management are day-to-day responsibilities. You track engineering health, manage technical debt conversations, and keep your team's output predictable quarter over quarter.

03 Step By Step Path

Step By Step Path

1. Build a strong individual contributor (IC) foundation.
Spend at least 4-6 years as a software engineer before targeting EM roles. Aim for a Senior Engineer title first. Companies want managers who have shipped large features end-to-end, survived production incidents, and mentored juniors. Skipping this foundation is the most common mistake aspiring managers make.

2. Take on informal leadership before your title changes.
Volunteer for tech lead or module lead responsibilities on your current team. Onboard new joiners, lead design reviews, and coordinate across teams. This builds the evidence your manager needs to promote you, and gives you a realistic preview of what EM work actually feels like.

3. Find a mentor who is already an EM.
Look inside your company first. Ask a senior EM or Director for monthly check-ins and ask them about their toughest challenges, not just their wins. If you cannot find one internally, LinkedIn is genuinely useful here: many Indian Engineering Managers post about their career journeys and respond to thoughtful, specific messages.

4. Study management fundamentals deliberately.
Read 'The Manager's Path' by Camille Fournier. It is the closest thing to a textbook for this career. 'An Elegant Puzzle' by Will Larson is useful once you are mid-level. You do not need an MBA, but you do need the vocabulary and mental models that come with reading seriously in this space.

5. Target an internal promotion before looking externally.
Most first-time managers in India get promoted internally, not hired from outside as a first-time EM. Have an explicit conversation with your manager about your goals. Ask what milestones you need to hit and map yourself against your company's career ladder as concretely as possible.

6. Prepare specifically for EM interviews.
EM interviews are different from IC interviews. You will face behavioural questions ('tell me about a time you had to manage a difficult performer'), system design rounds, and sometimes a roadmap or org design exercise. Practice the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for behavioural questions. Study how to run structured hiring, since many companies probe this in depth.

7. Evaluate offers carefully, not just on title.
Once you have an offer, check the level, scope (team size, number of direct reports), and total compensation before accepting. A 'Manager' title at one company may be equivalent to 'Senior Manager' at another. Scope and ownership matter as much as the label on your business card.

04 Timeline And Milestones

Timeline And Milestones

The path to Engineering Manager in India is not a straight line, but most people follow a broadly similar arc.

Years 1-3: Build your IC foundation.
Focus on becoming a strong and reliable engineer. Ship features end-to-end, understand your team's systems deeply, and learn how your work connects to the larger product. By the end of this phase, you should be approaching mid-level or Senior.

Years 4-6: Senior IC and informal leadership.
This is where real preparation happens. Take on tech lead responsibilities, mentor junior engineers, and lead projects with cross-team coordination. By year 5 or 6, you should have at least one clear example of guiding a team through a significant delivery, a tough incident, or an ambiguous problem with no obvious answer.

Years 6-8: First management role.
Most first-time Engineering Managers in India step into the role somewhere in this window. Your first team will likely be a small group of engineers. The first 6 months will feel uncomfortable: you will miss coding, and you will make mistakes managing people. Both are expected and normal parts of the transition.

Years 8-10: Consolidation and scope expansion.
After a few years as a manager, you are ready for a Senior Manager role or a team with broader scope. This is also when external moves become more valuable, since your market worth as a proven people manager rises significantly and recruiters start reaching out proactively.

Year 10 and beyond: Director and above.
Director roles (90-150+ LPA, per knok jobradar data) require managing multiple teams or a large single team with clear business impact. Most Directors have a decade or more of total experience, with several years spent specifically in management rather than individual contribution.

05 India Specific Tips

India Specific Tips

Bangalore is where the market is concentrated. Of the 975 EM openings tracked by knok jobradar, 182 are in Bangalore. Delhi and NCR come second with 53 openings, driven largely by startups and GCCs. Being in or open to Bangalore significantly expands your options. Pune and Chennai each have 20 openings, and Hyderabad has 16, mostly at established tech companies.

IIT and NIT pedigree opens doors but is not a gate. Many Engineering Managers at top product companies in India come from tier-2 engineering colleges. At the EM level, your track record carries more weight than your institution: teams you have grown, products you have shipped, and situations you have navigated. A strong LinkedIn profile with specific outcomes beats a famous college name at this stage of your career.

Naukri is not optional. Many Indian professionals treat Naukri as a backup and focus entirely on LinkedIn, but a significant share of EM roles at mid-sized product companies and GCCs are sourced through Naukri. Keep both profiles updated and use keywords like 'people management', 'team leadership', 'engineering delivery', and your core technical stack terms.

LinkedIn visibility matters more than most engineers expect. Indian Engineering Managers who post about real challenges and lessons learned attract noticeably more recruiter interest. You do not need to become an influencer. Posting once or twice a month about a specific management situation is enough to become visible in the right circles.

Communities worth joining. Hasgeek runs events and discussions for engineering leaders in India. iSPIRT connects product company leaders and surfaces leadership conversations. Engineering Leadership India groups on LinkedIn have active peer discussions and are where referrals quietly circulate among hiring managers.

GCCs are a strong and underrated stepping stone. Global Capability Centres of large multinationals often have more structured promotion paths, dedicated management training, and better internal mobility than early-stage startups. If your goal is to develop well as a manager, a GCC role as a senior IC or tech lead can give you the framework and the internal track to make the move cleanly.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

Do I need an MBA to become an Engineering Manager in India?

No, an MBA is not required and most Engineering Managers in India do not have one. The role values engineering credibility and people leadership far more than a business degree. Some people pursue executive management programmes from institutions like IIM after becoming EMs, to accelerate toward Director or VP roles, but this is a personal choice and not a standard hiring requirement at any level.

How many years of experience do I typically need before becoming an EM?

Most first-time Engineering Managers in India have between 6 and 9 years of total engineering experience when they make the move. The specific number matters less than the quality of that experience: have you led projects, mentored others, and operated at a senior IC level? Some engineers move faster at high-growth startups; others prefer to deepen their IC craft longer. Both are legitimate paths.

What is the difference between a Tech Lead and an Engineering Manager?

A Tech Lead focuses on technical direction: architecture decisions, code quality, and unblocking engineers on technical problems. An Engineering Manager focuses on people: hiring, performance, career growth, and team health. At some companies, especially smaller startups, one person does both under the title of Tech Lead Manager (TLM). At larger companies, these are distinct roles with separate hiring tracks, and you should decide early which path you are targeting.

Is it better to become an EM at a startup or a large company?

Both paths work, and the right choice depends on what you want from the experience. Startups offer faster timelines: you may move into management earlier if you join when the team is small and it grows around you. Large companies and GCCs offer structured training programmes, clearer career ladders, and more predictable compensation growth. If you want a solid framework for developing as a manager before taking on more scope, a large company is the safer starting point.

How are EM interviews different from software engineer interviews in India?

EM interviews have three main parts. Behavioural questions come first: you will be asked to walk through specific situations where you handled conflict, delivered a difficult project, or navigated a performance issue. Most companies still include system design to check your technical depth. Prepare concrete stories using the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) and be ready for strategy questions like 'how would you build a roadmap for a new product area.'
