02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions are commonly reported by candidates who interviewed for Engineering Manager roles at banks and financial services firms. Tailor your answers to your own experience.

  1. Walk us through how you have built or scaled an engineering team in a regulated environment.
  2. How do you ensure your team's deliverables meet compliance and audit requirements without slowing down sprint velocity?
  3. Describe a time you pushed back on a business stakeholder who wanted a feature shipped before it was production-ready.
  4. Resource Bank manages both legacy core-banking systems and modern cloud services. How do you handle tech debt while keeping critical systems running?
  5. How would you respond if a payment system went down during peak settlement hours and your on-call engineer was unreachable?
  6. Tell us about a time you had to let someone go, or move a team member to a different role because they were not the right fit.
  7. How do you measure engineering team performance? Which metrics do you trust and which do you treat with scepticism?
  8. We are building API-first banking products consumed by external partners. How have you led teams doing this kind of work?
  9. Describe your hiring process. What does your ideal interview loop look like for a senior engineer?
  10. How do you handle a mid-quarter regulatory change that lands on top of existing sprint commitments?
  11. Two of your senior engineers strongly disagree on a system architecture decision. What do you do?
  12. How do you keep engineers engaged when the work is primarily maintenance, compliance, or legacy modernisation rather than greenfield product development?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Three STAR-format sample answers to help you structure your responses. Adapt these to your own experience.

---

Q: How do you manage compliance requirements without hurting team velocity?

*Situation:* My team at a mid-size fintech was responsible for a payments reconciliation service processing high transaction volumes daily. A new RBI directive required detailed audit logging across every transaction flow within a tight compliance deadline.

*Task:* I needed to deliver the compliance work on time without pushing back roadmap items the business had already committed to external partners.

*Action:* I ran a quick spike to scope the logging work. We built a shared logging library that all services could plug into, and I pulled a senior engineer from each squad to build it while the rest of the team kept moving on roadmap items. I also set up a weekly sync with our compliance officer to catch interpretation gaps early rather than at audit time.

*Result:* We shipped the audit logging ahead of the RBI deadline. Roadmap items slipped by only a single sprint on average, which the business accepted. The logging library was later reused across multiple other compliance initiatives.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you resolved a serious conflict within your team.

*Situation:* Two of my senior engineers had a public disagreement in a design review about whether to adopt a new message-queue architecture. The tension spilled into Slack threads and was starting to affect team morale.

*Task:* I needed to resolve the technical question and repair the working relationship before it damaged the broader team culture.

*Action:* I met each engineer separately first to understand their real concerns, not just their stated positions. One was worried about operational complexity for the on-call team. The other was focused on throughput for an upcoming load target. I then facilitated a structured decision session with both of them and a neutral tech lead, using agreed criteria: ops burden, throughput, and migration cost. We chose a time-boxed proof of concept instead of a committee vote.

*Result:* The PoC concluded within a sprint cycle. The throughput-focused engineer's approach won on the criteria we had all agreed to, so the other engineer accepted the outcome without resentment. The broader team saw that technical disagreements could be resolved with data, which improved how we ran design reviews going forward.

---

Q: Describe a time you made a tough call on tech debt versus new feature delivery.

*Situation:* Our core transaction ledger had accumulated significant tech debt over several years. A new product team wanted us to add real-time balance notifications, which would have required building on top of the most fragile parts of the ledger.

*Task:* I had to decide whether to take on the feature request as-is or invest time in stabilising the ledger first, knowing the business was eager to ship.

*Action:* I put together a one-page risk brief covering the incident history of the ledger, the likely failure modes if we built on top of it, and the cost of a stabilisation effort. I presented it to the VP of Product and the CFO together so both business and finance had the same picture. I also proposed a middle path: stabilise the highest-risk module first, then build the notification feature in parallel using the stabilised layer.

*Result:* The business approved the middle path. We shipped the notification feature slightly later than originally asked, with zero production incidents through the initial launch period. The CFO later referenced that risk brief as the kind of communication she wanted from engineering leaders.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For behavioural questions use the STAR format: Situation (one or two sentences of context), Task (what you were personally responsible for), Action (specific steps you took, using 'I' not 'we'), Result (quantify where possible, or describe the outcome clearly).

For technical leadership questions use a three-part structure: the problem and its constraints, the approach and trade-offs you considered, and the outcome plus what you learned. Avoid going deep into code-level details. Interviewers at EM level want to see systems thinking and the ability to communicate risk, not syntax.

For stakeholder conflict questions use the 'align, negotiate, escalate' ladder. Show first that you tried to find common ground. Then show how you negotiated scope or timeline. Mention escalation only as a last resort, framed as protecting the team and the product rather than winning an argument.

For 'tell me about yourself' open with your current role and team size, name the types of systems you own, share one specific outcome you are proud of, and connect it to why Resource Bank's engineering challenges interest you. Keep this to around two minutes.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

At Resource Bank, interviewers are typically looking for the following signals, based on what candidates in banking EM roles commonly report.

Domain awareness. You do not need to be a core-banking veteran, but you should be comfortable speaking to concepts like reconciliation, settlement windows, regulatory reporting, and why uptime carries different stakes in fintech than in a consumer app.

Regulatory sensibility. Resource Bank operates under RBI guidelines and standard banking compliance frameworks. Interviewers want to see that you treat compliance as a design constraint built into delivery planning, not as a checkbox added at the end.

Delivery track record. Concrete examples of shipping under pressure, managing competing priorities, and keeping commitments to stakeholders matter far more than theoretical frameworks or certifications.

People leadership depth. Surface-level answers like 'I hold 1:1s and give feedback' will not be enough. Show how you have grown engineers, handled underperformance constructively, and built psychological safety in your team.

Clear communication upward. Banking engineering leaders regularly present to risk committees, senior finance leadership, and sometimes regulators. Interviewers want evidence that you can translate technical risk into business language without losing accuracy.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know the company and the domain
Read Resource Bank's recent investor communications or annual report for context on their technology strategy. Understand their digital products, any publicised system migrations, and their regulatory footprint. If you are not already familiar with RBI guidelines on payments and data localisation, spend time here.

Week 2: Build your story bank
Write out a solid set of STAR stories from your career. Cover at least these scenarios: a delivery under pressure, a compliance or risk situation, a team conflict, a hiring decision, a tech debt trade-off, and a time you influenced without authority. Include at least a couple of stories where things did not go perfectly but you recovered and learned something.

Week 3: Practice out loud
Answer questions aloud, not just in your head. Aim for answers in the 2-3 minute range. Ask a peer or mentor to play interviewer. Focus especially on questions about regulated environments and stakeholder management, which are commonly emphasised in banking EM rounds, candidates report.

Final prep (day before)
Re-read the job description and map each listed requirement to one of your stories. Prepare thoughtful questions for your interviewers about engineering culture, incident response practices, and how success is measured in the first six months.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Saying 'we' when you mean 'I'. Interviewers are assessing your individual contribution. Use 'I' when describing actions you personally took, and save 'we' for outcomes the team achieved together. Saying 'we handled everything as a team' signals you may not have clarity on your own impact.

Being vague about scale. 'A large team' or 'significant improvement' signals you are not close to your own metrics. Use approximate ranges instead: 'a team of around a dozen engineers' or 'we cut incident frequency by roughly half over a couple of quarters.'

Underestimating the compliance angle. Candidates who come from product companies and brush past regulatory questions tend to score lower in banking interviews. Show genuine interest in the constraints rather than just tolerance for them.

Over-explaining technical architecture. EM interviews are not system design rounds. Spending many minutes on database schema choices signals that you have not fully shifted into a leadership mindset.

Not asking about failure. Strong EM candidates ask interviewers about the hardest engineering challenge the team faced recently and how they handled it. This signals maturity and genuine curiosity rather than just interest in the offer.

Arriving unprepared for the HR screen. The HR screen typically covers notice period, compensation expectations, and a quick culture-fit check. Know your numbers and your current notice period before that first call.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Resource Bank Engineering Manager interview typically have?

Candidates report anywhere from 3 to 5 rounds, typically spread over 3-6 weeks. The process usually starts with an HR screening call, followed by a technical leadership discussion and a behavioural or panel round. Some candidates report an additional case study or stakeholder simulation round, particularly for Senior Manager level roles.

What salary can I expect for an EM role at Resource Bank?

Based on the data available, Manager-level roles in banking engineering typically fall in the 35-60 LPA range, Senior Manager in the 55-90 LPA range, and Director-level in the 90-150+ LPA range. Actual offers depend on your years of experience, team size managed, and the specific level the role maps to. Clarify the level early in the process so you are negotiating the right band.

Do I need banking or fintech experience to get an EM role at Resource Bank?

Not necessarily. Many Engineering Managers at Indian banks come from product companies or other regulated industries. What matters more is your ability to demonstrate awareness of compliance constraints, uptime expectations, and high-stakes stakeholder communication. If you lack direct banking experience, prepare specific examples of working under regulatory or audit requirements from your current domain.

How should I talk about tech debt in a banking interview?

Frame tech debt as a risk management problem, not just a technical inconvenience. Banks are comfortable with risk frameworks, so present tech debt in terms of incident probability, blast radius, and the cost of remediation versus the cost of deferral. Pair your answer with a real example where you made a deliberate, documented decision about tech debt and communicated it clearly to business stakeholders.

What questions should I ask Resource Bank at the end of the interview?

Ask how engineering decisions are made when business urgency conflicts with technical quality. Ask what the on-call culture looks like and how incident response is structured. Ask how success is measured in the first six months for a new Engineering Manager. These questions signal that you are thinking like a leader who will own outcomes, not just execute tasks.
