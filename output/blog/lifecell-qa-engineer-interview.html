02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions are drawn from candidate accounts and are typical for a product-focused QA role at a healthcare company like Lifecell. Prepare a concrete example from your own work for each one.

  1. Walk me through how you approach writing test cases for a new feature in a customer-facing portal.
  2. What is the difference between smoke testing and regression testing? Give a real example from a project you have worked on.
  3. How do you handle a situation where a developer says 'that's not a bug, it's working as designed'?
  4. Describe a time you found a critical bug close to a release deadline. What did you do?
  5. How would you test a form that collects sensitive personal or medical data?
  6. Write a SQL query to find duplicate email entries in a customer records table.
  7. Have you used test management tools like JIRA, TestRail, or Zephyr? How do you track and report test progress to the team?
  8. What is your experience with API testing? Which tools have you used and what do you verify in a response?
  9. How do you prioritise test cases when time is short before a go-live?
  10. What does 'quality' mean to you when the product affects real people's health records?
  11. Have you built or contributed to an automation framework? Walk me through the structure you used.
  12. How do you keep test documentation current when requirements change frequently during a sprint?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time you found a critical bug close to a release deadline.

*Situation:* At my previous company, we were two days away from releasing an updated customer onboarding module. I was doing a final round of regression testing.

*Task:* My job was to sign off on the release candidate and confirm all critical user flows were working correctly.

*Action:* While testing the document upload step, I noticed that PDF files above a certain size were silently failing to save, with no error shown to the user. I logged a detailed bug report in JIRA with screenshots, steps to reproduce, and the exact file sizes that triggered the issue. I flagged it directly to the dev lead and release manager the same hour, rather than waiting for the daily standup.

*Result:* The team patched the issue within a day and the release went out on schedule. The fix also caught a secondary edge case we had not originally noticed. The release manager later used this incident as a training example for 'why silent failures need immediate escalation.'

---

Q: How do you handle a developer who disagrees with a bug you have logged?

*Situation:* I had logged a bug where a date field accepted invalid future dates on a medical record entry screen. The developer marked it 'not a bug,' saying the business team had not restricted that field.

*Task:* I needed to resolve the disagreement without damaging the working relationship, and make sure the right decision was made for the product.

*Action:* I set up a short call with the developer and brought in the product owner. I showed the original requirements document and pointed out the ambiguity. I proposed treating it as a defect because the system handled health records and bad data could cause downstream reporting issues. I also suggested a simple validation rule as the fix.

*Result:* The product owner sided with adding validation. The developer agreed once the downstream risk was explained clearly. We added the fix before release and updated the requirements document to prevent similar ambiguity in future sprints.

---

Q: How would you test a feature that handles sensitive personal or medical data?

*Situation:* I was asked to test a new module that allowed customers to update their registered mobile number and linked medical record access.

*Task:* My goal was to confirm the feature worked correctly, but also that it was secure and privacy-safe.

*Action:* I covered functional cases first: valid updates, invalid formats, and empty fields. Then I checked boundary conditions, duplicate number entries, and session handling. For data sensitivity, I verified that the old number was masked in logs, that confirmation messages went to the right contact, and that an unauthorised user could not trigger a change for another account. I flagged any privacy-adjacent issues in TestRail with a 'data sensitivity' tag.

*Result:* We found two issues before go-live: the old number appeared in plain text in an internal log, and the confirmation SMS fired before the change was verified. Both were fixed. The privacy tagging approach I introduced was later adopted by the full team.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For 'tell me about a time' questions, use the STAR structure: Situation (brief context), Task (your specific responsibility), Action (what you actually did, in detail), Result (what changed because of it). Keep Situation short and spend most of your time on Action and Result. Interviewers lose interest when the setup takes more than a minute.

For 'how would you' questions, walk the interviewer through your thinking out loud. Start with 'I would first understand the requirement and identify the key user flows,' then move to your test design approach, edge cases, and how you would log and track findings. This shows structured thinking, not just tool familiarity.

For SQL questions, practise these patterns: SELECT with WHERE and GROUP BY, finding duplicates using GROUP BY with HAVING COUNT greater than 1, and simple two-table JOINs. Write them from scratch on paper, since you may be asked to write on a whiteboard or shared doc with no auto-complete.

For 'what does quality mean to you', avoid vague answers like 'zero defects.' Connect quality to user trust and business risk. For a healthcare company, a strong answer ties quality to data integrity and the real-world impact of an error on a customer's records or care decisions.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Lifecell's QA interviews look for a specific combination of skills and mindset, based on what candidates report from the process.

Attention to detail is the most cited quality. Interviewers want to see that you catch things others miss and document them clearly enough for a developer to reproduce without needing follow-up questions.

Domain awareness matters more here than at a generic IT firm. Because the product touches health and personal data, interviewers want to know you understand why accuracy and privacy are important, not just how to follow a test script.

Communication under pressure comes up in scenario-based questions. They want to see that you can escalate a critical issue clearly and quickly without causing panic, and that you can push back on a developer or a deadline when quality is genuinely at risk.

Practical tool knowledge is assessed directly. Expect questions on your experience with JIRA or a similar tracker, any automation tools (Selenium, Cypress, or similar), and basic SQL. You do not need to be an automation expert at the mid level, but knowing the basics helps significantly.

Process discipline is valued because Lifecell operates in a space adjacent to healthcare compliance. Candidates who describe how they maintain test documentation, write clear test cases, and keep a regression suite updated tend to perform well in the final round.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Start with the company context. Read Lifecell's website to understand their core offerings: cord blood banking, stem cell storage, and the portals they run for customers and hospital partners. This gives you material for 'how would you test X' questions and shows the interviewer you took the role seriously before walking in.

Identify your STAR examples early. From your own experience, pick three to five stories covering: finding a critical bug, disagreeing with a developer, testing under time pressure, and handling sensitive data. Write them out in full before the interview so you are not constructing them on the spot.

Sharpen technical basics in the week before. Practise writing test cases for a sample feature such as a login form or a document upload screen. Review the difference between functional, regression, smoke, and sanity testing in your own words. For SQL, practise SELECT with WHERE, GROUP BY, HAVING, and simple JOINs, and write at least five queries from scratch.

Check your API testing claims. If your resume mentions API testing, open Postman and test a public API before the interview. Be ready to explain what you verify: status codes, response body structure, error handling, and headers. A vague answer here after a confident claim on your resume can shift the interview quickly.

Prepare questions to ask the interviewer. Good ones: 'What does the current release cycle look like for the QA team?', 'How is QA structured relative to the development team?', and 'What automation tooling is the team currently using or planning to adopt?' Specific questions signal genuine interest and leave a stronger impression than silence.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Vague test case examples are the most common reason candidates underperform. Saying 'I write positive and negative test cases' sounds memorised. Always attach a real feature or scenario from your own project history.

Ignoring the healthcare context is a missed opportunity. Many candidates prepare for a generic software QA interview and skip thinking about why a healthcare company cares about data quality differently. Mentioning data privacy, audit trails, or compliance awareness at least once makes a noticeable difference.

Overclaiming automation experience can backfire quickly. If you list Selenium on your resume but cannot explain a Page Object Model or write a basic locator, interviewers will notice. Be honest about your current level and show a learning plan if automation is a gap.

Not asking questions at the end signals low interest. Interviewers at most companies, including Lifecell, note whether a candidate is curious about the role and the team. Prepare at least two specific questions before the interview.

Weak SQL preparation trips up many mid-level candidates who assume it will not come up. Even one poorly answered query can shift an interviewer's confidence. Set aside dedicated time for SQL practice in the days before your interview, not the night before.

How many rounds does the Lifecell QA Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically runs 2-3 rounds. What SQL level do I need to clear the technical round?

Candidates report that SQL questions are at a practical, intermediate level rather than advanced. You should be comfortable writing SELECT queries with WHERE conditions, GROUP BY with HAVING to find duplicates, and simple two-table JOINs. Complex window functions or stored procedures are rarely required at the QA Engineer level. Practise writing queries from scratch without auto-complete as part of your preparation.

Does Lifecell expect automation testing experience for QA Engineer roles?

For mid-level roles, automation experience is a plus but not always mandatory, based on candidate accounts. Manual testing fundamentals, clear bug reporting, and SQL matter more at the entry and mid levels. For senior or lead roles, automation knowledge (Selenium, Cypress, or similar) is more commonly expected. Be honest about your current level and show genuine interest in upskilling if automation is a gap.
