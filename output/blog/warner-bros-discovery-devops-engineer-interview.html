01 Overview

Overview

Warner Bros Discovery (WBD) is a global media and entertainment company running streaming platforms, broadcast networks, and large-scale content delivery infrastructure. Their engineering teams handle real-time video processing, CDN orchestration, and heavy cloud workloads across multiple regions. DevOps roles at WBD sit at the crossroads of media tech and platform engineering, making the interview more specialized than a typical product company interview.

As of July 2026, WBD has 55 open DevOps-related roles tracked by knok jobradar, reflecting active hiring. Candidates typically report a process that includes a recruiter screen, one or two technical rounds covering infrastructure and tooling, and a final system design or behavioral discussion. The exact format varies by team, so treat this as a general guide rather than a guarantee.

The tech stack commonly involves AWS or GCP, Kubernetes, Terraform, Jenkins or GitHub Actions, and observability tools such as Datadog or Splunk. For streaming-focused teams, familiarity with CDN behavior, adaptive bitrate streaming, and latency management gives you a clear edge over candidates with only generic cloud experience.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How have you handled a production incident where a deployment caused a service outage?

*Situation:* At my previous company, a routine deployment to our video transcoding service caused an unexpected drop in processing capacity during peak viewing hours.

*Task:* I was the on-call engineer and needed to restore service quickly while keeping the team and stakeholders informed.

*Action:* I pulled up our monitoring dashboards immediately and identified that the new container image had a misconfigured environment variable causing worker processes to crash on startup. I initiated a rollback using our standard rollback procedure, confirmed service recovery in the dashboards, and then reproduced the issue in a staging environment to understand the root cause. I also wrote a brief incident note for the team so everyone had the same context.

*Result:* Service was restored within a short window. I then added a pre-deployment config validation step to the pipeline so similar misconfigurations would be caught before reaching production.

---

Q: Walk me through how you would set up a CI/CD pipeline for a microservices-based streaming application.

*Situation:* My team inherited a brittle deployment process where each microservice was deployed manually, leading to frequent configuration drift and deployment errors.

*Task:* I was asked to design and implement a unified CI/CD pipeline across all services with consistent standards.

*Action:* I introduced GitHub Actions as the orchestration layer, created reusable workflow templates for build, test, and deploy stages, integrated Terraform for infrastructure changes, and added automated smoke tests post-deployment. I worked closely with the QA team to decide which tests belonged in every pipeline run versus in nightly scheduled jobs.

*Result:* Deployment confidence improved noticeably across the team. Engineers were able to ship changes more frequently with fewer rollbacks, and the manual steps that previously caused errors were eliminated entirely.

---

Q: How would you design auto-scaling for a system that sees unpredictable traffic spikes?

*Situation:* A content platform I worked on saw sudden viewer spikes whenever a new series dropped. Static provisioning meant we either over-spent on idle capacity between releases or scrambled to scale manually.

*Task:* Design a proactive and reactive scaling strategy that could handle these spikes automatically without constant manual intervention.

*Action:* I implemented Kubernetes Horizontal Pod Autoscaler using custom metrics from our CDN edge layer, set up cluster autoscaler for node-level scaling, and created scheduled scaling rules tied to our content release calendar. I also ran load tests before major releases to validate that the scaling configuration behaved as expected under simulated traffic.

*Result:* The next major launch ran smoothly with no manual intervention needed from the infrastructure team. Infrastructure costs also became more predictable because we were no longer over-provisioning by default.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For technical 'how would you' questions: Use an 'explain, demonstrate, reflect' structure. Start by explaining the concept or approach in plain terms, give a concrete example from your experience, then reflect on trade-offs or what you would do differently. This shows both knowledge and judgment.

For incident and behavioral questions: STAR works well, but compress the Situation and Task sections. Interviewers at WBD want to hear how you think and act under pressure, so spend more time on your specific Actions and the measurable or observable Result.

For system design questions: Begin with requirements clarification before proposing any architecture. For WBD specifically, show awareness of streaming-specific constraints such as latency sensitivity, high-read and low-write patterns, CDN edge behavior, and the consequences of downtime during a live event.

For 'tell me about a time' questions: Prepare two or three flexible stories that can cover different themes: reliability, cross-team collaboration, and technical improvement. Avoid stories where you are the lone hero. WBD teams value collaborative problem-solving, and interviewers notice when every answer credits only you.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

WBD DevOps interviewers typically look for engineers who understand the specific demands of media and streaming infrastructure. A few themes candidates report coming up repeatedly:

Streaming-first thinking. Knowing Kubernetes and Terraform is expected. Interviewers want to see that you understand why latency, throughput, and availability matter differently for video delivery than for a typical SaaS product.

Ownership mindset. WBD operates at global scale. Candidates report that interviewers probe for whether you take responsibility for systems end-to-end, from writing the Terraform module to being on-call for what you deploy.

Communication under pressure. Incident response questions often have a soft-skill layer: can you communicate clearly with non-technical stakeholders while simultaneously debugging a live system?

Cost awareness. Cloud spend is significant for a streaming business. Expect questions that touch on how you balance performance with cost efficiency, not just uptime.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Core foundations.
Review Kubernetes concepts including pods, deployments, services, HPA, and cluster autoscaler. Brush up on Terraform module design and state management. Make sure you can explain the difference between blue-green and canary deployments clearly and confidently.

Week 2: WBD-specific depth.
Read publicly available engineering blog posts and conference talks from media-scale companies. Understand how CDNs work with streaming, what adaptive bitrate streaming is, and the common failure modes in video delivery pipelines. Practice sketching a streaming platform architecture from scratch on paper or a whiteboard.

Week 3: Behavioral and system design.
Write out two or three STAR stories covering: an incident you handled well, a system you improved, and a time you disagreed with a technical decision and how you resolved it. Practice talking through a full system design: 'design a CI/CD system for a globally distributed streaming platform.'

Final days.
Review the job description carefully and map your experience to each requirement listed. Prepare a few questions to ask the interviewer about team structure, on-call expectations, and deployment frequency. 07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

  1. Giving generic answers. Saying 'I used Kubernetes to deploy microservices' without explaining the problem you were solving. WBD interviewers probe for depth, so always connect your tool choices to the specific constraints you were working within.
  1. Ignoring the streaming context. Treating WBD like any other cloud company. Show that you have thought about media-specific concerns: latency budgets, content delivery at scale, and what happens when a live stream goes down.
  1. Skipping the 'why'. Explaining what you did without explaining why. Saying 'I set up Prometheus' without explaining what problem it solved or how it shaped your incident response tells the interviewer very little.
  1. Overselling solo work. Claiming full ownership of large systems in a way that sounds implausible. Mention collaborators and be honest about your specific contribution. Interviewers notice when every answer is a solo heroics story.
  1. Not asking questions. Candidates who have nothing to ask at the end are often seen as less engaged. Ask about the team's on-call rotation, deployment frequency, or how they approach post-mortems.
  1. Freezing on metrics. If asked about scale or performance numbers, it is fine to say 'I do not recall the exact figure, but the system handled...' and describe the shape of the load. Honest vagueness is better than a confidently wrong number.
