01 Overview

Overview

CommIT currently has 172 open roles, and the Solutions Engineer position sits at the heart of their active hiring push. As of July 2026, there are 1,270 Solutions Engineer openings across India on major job platforms, with Bangalore (55 openings), Mumbai (23), and Delhi (20) leading in volume. Pune (12), Hyderabad (6), and Chennai (5) also have active demand.

The CommIT Solutions Engineer role places you at the crossroads of technical depth and client-facing communication. You are expected to run product demos, build proof-of-concepts, handle pre-sales queries, and coordinate between sales and engineering teams. This is not a pure developer role, and it is not a pure sales role. Success depends on how well you can translate customer requirements into technical solutions and back again.

The interview process typically involves two to four rounds: an initial HR or recruiter conversation, a technical or product knowledge round, a mock demo or case study exercise, and sometimes a final discussion with senior leadership. Candidates report that CommIT interviewers place strong emphasis on how you handle objections, how clearly you communicate under pressure, and whether you understand the full pre-sales cycle.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These are the questions candidates commonly report facing in CommIT Solutions Engineer interviews. Prepare a specific example for each.

  1. Walk me through a complex technical demo you ran for a client. How did you prepare, and what was the outcome?
  2. How do you handle a situation where the product does not have a feature the client is asking for?
  3. Describe a time you worked closely with the sales team to close a deal. What was your specific contribution?
  4. How do you qualify whether a prospect is worth investing a full proof-of-concept for?
  5. Tell me about a time you had to explain a technical concept to a non-technical decision-maker.
  6. How do you stay updated on competitor products, and how do you use that knowledge in client conversations?
  7. Describe a situation where a proof-of-concept did not go as planned. What did you do?
  8. How do you manage multiple active prospects at different stages of the sales cycle?
  9. Give an example of a time you lost a deal. What did you learn from it?
  10. How do you coordinate with engineering or product when a client raises a feature request during pre-sales?
  11. Describe a client escalation you handled. What was the issue, and how did you resolve it?
  12. What does your process look like for building a custom demo or sandbox environment tailored to a specific client?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for every behavioural question. Keep Situation and Task concise, and spend most of your time on Action and Result. Here are three model answers you can adapt.

Q: Walk me through a complex technical demo you ran for a client.

*Situation:* I was supporting a pre-sales cycle for a mid-size logistics company that had specific workflow automation requirements. Their technical team was skeptical about whether our platform was flexible enough for their use case.

*Task:* My job was to build and deliver a customised demo that addressed their exact pain points, not a generic product walkthrough.

*Action:* I spent three days working with their IT lead to understand their current stack. I then built a sandbox replicating two of their core workflows inside our platform, scripted the demo around their own terminology, and prepared specific answers to the objections I expected their CTO to raise.

*Result:* The demo ran for ninety minutes without a single major objection going unanswered. The client moved to a paid pilot the following week, and the account executive noted that the level of customisation was the deciding factor in their choice.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you lost a deal. What did you learn?

*Situation:* We were in the final stages of a competitive evaluation with a fintech client. Two vendors had been shortlisted, and we were the frontrunner on technical capability.

*Task:* I was responsible for making the technical case and managing the proof-of-concept environment.

*Action:* I focused heavily on feature comparison but did not spend enough time mapping stakeholders. The person I had been building the relationship with turned out not to be the final decision-maker, and a competing vendor had better access to the CFO.

*Result:* We lost the deal. After a post-mortem with the account executive, I changed my approach: I now confirm who has sign-off authority in the first discovery call and build a stakeholder map before investing in a full proof-of-concept. That mistake has not repeated itself.

---

Q: How do you handle it when the product does not have a feature the client needs?

*Situation:* During a live demo, a prospect asked whether our platform could integrate with a niche ERP system we did not officially support.

*Task:* I needed to be honest without losing the deal, and without over-promising on the product roadmap.

*Action:* I acknowledged the gap directly, explained what our API layer could do, and offered to connect them with our engineering team to assess feasibility. I also shared two client case studies where similar custom integrations had been built successfully.

*Result:* The client appreciated the transparency. We scheduled a technical call with our engineering team the following week, and the deal moved forward to the next stage rather than stalling on the objection.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For behavioural questions: Use STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result). Keep Situation and Task to two or three sentences. Spend the most time on Action and Result. Quantify the result where possible. If exact numbers are not available, describe the qualitative outcome clearly.

For technical and product questions: Use an 'Explain, Demonstrate, Differentiate' approach. First explain what the feature or concept does in plain terms, then give an example of how you have used or demonstrated it, then describe how it compares to alternative approaches or competitor products.

For objection-handling questions: Use an 'Acknowledge, Reframe, Advance' structure. Start by validating the client's concern, then reframe the issue around what your product does well, then move the conversation forward with a specific next step such as a pilot, a technical call, or a follow-up demo.

For 'why CommIT' questions: Connect your answer to their scale and active hiring across Bangalore, Mumbai, and Delhi. Show that you have researched their client verticals and that your pre-sales experience maps directly to the type of deals they work on.

General delivery tip: Avoid starting every sentence with 'I.' Vary your sentence structure. Interviewers at this level pay close attention to communication fluency, not just the content of your answers.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

CommIT Solutions Engineer interviewers typically look for four qualities.

Technical credibility without being a developer: You should understand APIs, integrations, cloud infrastructure, and the company's product well enough to answer hard questions during a live demo. You do not need to write production code. Candidates report that interviewers test whether you can hold a technical conversation with a client's IT team and stay credible under pressure.

Commercial awareness: Solutions Engineers sit inside the revenue function. Interviewers want to see that you understand sales cycles, can qualify prospects, and think about deals in terms of pipeline and close probability, not just technical fit.

Composure under pressure: Expect at least one scenario question about handling a difficult client, a failed demo, or a product limitation uncovered mid-conversation. They want to see calmness, honesty, and a clear process for recovery.

Collaboration with internal teams: The role requires constant coordination with sales, product, and engineering. Interviewers will probe whether you can manage competing priorities and advocate for client needs internally without creating conflict.

Direct, solutions-focused communication: Candidates report that CommIT values clarity over formality. Avoid jargon-heavy language. Say what you mean and show how you would move a situation forward.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Know the product and the company

Research CommIT's core offerings, the industries they serve, and any publicly available client case studies or product documentation. Understand how their product is positioned relative to key competitors. If a product demo or documentation is available online, go through it carefully and note the terminology they use.

Step 2: Build your story bank

Identify six to eight situations from your past work that you can adapt for behavioural questions. Cover at least: a successful demo, a failed deal, a difficult client conversation, a cross-functional collaboration, a technical explanation to a non-technical audience, and a time you handled a competitor question in front of a client. Write each story in STAR format.

Step 3: Practice delivery

Record yourself answering questions on video. Watch it back and check for filler words, vague Action steps, and weak Result statements. Run at least two mock interviews with someone who can give honest feedback. If you know anyone in a pre-sales or Solutions Engineer role, ask them to roleplay a live objection-handling scenario with you.

Step 4: Prepare questions for the interviewer

Have three to five questions ready. Good options: how the Solutions Engineer team is structured relative to the sales team, what a typical proof-of-concept cycle looks like at CommIT, and which client verticals are growing fastest for them right now.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Being too technical or too sales-focused: The Solutions Engineer role is a hybrid. Candidates who go deep on code and ignore commercial context, or who focus only on relationship skills and cannot handle technical questions, both struggle. Every answer should show both sides.

Using generic examples: Saying 'I ran demos for clients' without specifics does not land. Always describe the client context, what made the situation challenging, and what you did differently from the default approach.

Over-promising on product capabilities: A common mistake in mock scenario questions is agreeing to client requests the product cannot support. Interviewers test for this directly. Honesty paired with a clear next step is always the right answer.

Not knowing the competitive landscape: If you cannot name two or three competing products and explain how CommIT compares, interviewers will question your readiness for real client conversations.

Weak Result statements: Many candidates describe what they did but forget to say what happened. Every STAR answer must end with a concrete outcome: the deal advanced, the client moved to the next stage, the team adopted the new process.

Ignoring stakeholder mapping: Candidates often describe situations without mentioning who the decision-makers were or how they navigated multiple stakeholders. For enterprise pre-sales roles, this is a skill interviewers look for specifically.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the CommIT Solutions Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report that the process typically runs two to four rounds. This usually includes an HR or recruiter screening, a technical or product knowledge round, and a mock demo or case study exercise. Some candidates mention a final conversation with a senior manager or director, though this varies by team and location.

Is there a coding test for the Solutions Engineer role at CommIT?

Candidates typically report that the Solutions Engineer interview does not include a competitive coding round of the kind used for software developer hiring. Instead, expect technical questions about product integrations, APIs, cloud architecture, and how you would approach a client's specific technical environment. You should be comfortable reading and discussing technical documentation, but live coding is not commonly reported for this role.

Which cities have the most Solutions Engineer openings right now?

Based on knok jobradar data as of July 2026, Bangalore leads with 55 Solutions Engineer openings across the Indian market, followed by Mumbai (23), Delhi (20), and Pune (12). Hyderabad and Chennai also have active demand. CommIT itself has 172 open roles across locations, making it one of the more active hirers in this space.

What is the typical salary for a Solutions Engineer at CommIT?

CommIT has not publicly disclosed a salary band for this role, and no salary figures were available in the source data for this guide. For market benchmarks, candidates typically reference Glassdoor or levels.fyi, where publicly reported figures for Solutions Engineer roles in India vary widely based on years of experience, city, and the company's client profile. Research both the CommIT-specific page and the broader role category before your compensation discussion.

How important is prior pre-sales experience for the CommIT Solutions Engineer role?

Prior pre-sales or Solutions Engineer experience is typically preferred, but candidates report that it is not always a hard requirement. Professionals with a background in technical support, implementation consulting, or product management have successfully made the transition into this role. What interviewers consistently look for is the ability to run a product demo confidently, handle technical objections, and demonstrate an understanding of how deals move through a sales cycle.

Should I prepare a mock demo for the CommIT interview?

Yes, candidates report that a demo or case study component is a common part of the CommIT Solutions Engineer process. You may be asked to demo a product you have worked with before, or to walk through how you would approach a hypothetical client scenario. Prepare a structured demo flow, anticipate objections, and practice delivering it clearly so you leave time for questions and discussion at the end.
