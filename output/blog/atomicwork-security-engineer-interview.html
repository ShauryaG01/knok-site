01 Overview

Overview

Atomicwork is a fast-growing SaaS company building an AI-powered IT service management platform for enterprises. With 27 open roles currently listed, the company is scaling across engineering, and the Security Engineer role sits at the intersection of product security and enterprise customer trust.

As a Security Engineer at Atomicwork, you would typically own vulnerability management, secure architecture reviews, cloud security (mainly AWS or GCP), and compliance work for enterprise customers. Because Atomicwork sells to large enterprises, your work directly affects customer trust and deal closures.

Candidates report a process that typically includes an initial HR screen, one or two technical rounds, and a final discussion with a senior engineering or leadership member. The team values engineers who can think like attackers, communicate risk clearly to non-security stakeholders, and work independently in a lean setup.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly in Security Engineer interviews at product-led SaaS companies like Atomicwork, based on candidate reports and the nature of their platform:

  1. Walk us through how you would do a security review for a new AI feature before it ships.
  2. What experience do you have with compliance frameworks such as SOC 2, PCI-DSS, or NIST standards?
  3. How have you handled a security incident or vulnerability disclosure in a previous role?
  4. Describe your approach to securing a multi-tenant SaaS application.
  5. How do you prioritize vulnerabilities when you have ten findings and limited engineering bandwidth?
  6. What does your process look like for threat modelling a new service or API endpoint?
  7. How would you approach secrets management and key rotation in a cloud-native environment?
  8. What SAST, DAST, or SCA tools have you worked with, and how did you integrate them into a CI/CD pipeline?
  9. How do you communicate a critical security risk to a product manager or an engineer who does not have a security background?
  10. Atomicwork handles IT data for large enterprise clients. What controls would you put in place to protect customer data at rest and in transit?
  11. Tell us about a time you pushed back on a feature because of security concerns. How did you handle the conflict?
  12. How do you stay current with new attack techniques and CVEs, and how do you decide which ones actually matter for your environment?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How have you handled a security incident or vulnerability disclosure in a previous role?

*Situation:* At my previous company, a bug bounty researcher reported a broken object-level authorisation vulnerability in our REST API that could let one tenant read another tenant's data.

*Task:* I was the on-call security engineer. I had to triage the report, contain the exposure, coordinate a fix, and communicate status to the affected customers.

*Action:* I immediately disabled the affected endpoint, confirmed the scope by reviewing API logs from the prior week, and filed a priority-zero ticket with the backend team. I wrote a one-page incident brief for the VP of Engineering that described the risk in plain language without jargon. I worked with the engineer who owned the service to push a patch within a few hours, and I drafted the customer communication that went out the same day.

*Result:* We contained the issue before any confirmed data access occurred. The researcher gave us a positive disclosure review, and the experience led us to add broken object-level authorisation checks to our standard API review checklist going forward.

---

Q: How do you prioritize vulnerabilities when you have ten findings and limited engineering bandwidth?

*Situation:* After a penetration test at my previous employer, we received a report with a mix of findings ranging from critical remote code execution to low-severity information-disclosure issues.

*Task:* Engineering had capacity to fix only a few items in the current sprint, so I needed a clear, defensible prioritization.

*Action:* I scored each finding using a combination of CVSS base score, exploitability in our specific environment, and business impact (which services were customer-facing, which held PII). I presented a one-page stack-rank to the engineering lead with the top items highlighted, the reasoning for each, and a proposed timeline for the rest. I also flagged one medium-severity finding that was trivially exploitable and moved it into the priority group.

*Result:* The team fixed the priority items in two sprints. When auditors reviewed our process during the next compliance review, they cited it as a strong example of risk-based prioritization.

---

Q: Walk us through how you would do a security review for a new AI feature before it ships.

*Situation:* At a previous role, the product team was shipping a new AI assistant that would answer questions by querying internal company data.

*Task:* I needed to review it for security risks specific to AI systems, not just standard web-app threats.

*Action:* I started with a threat model focused on prompt injection, data leakage through model outputs, and excessive agency (the model taking actions it should not). I reviewed the data access patterns to ensure the model could only query data the logged-in user was already authorised to see. I tested a set of adversarial prompts manually and worked with the ML team to add an output filter for PII patterns. I also documented the review so future AI features could use it as a template.

*Result:* We shipped the feature with no security-related incidents. The threat model template was adopted by the team and reused for the next two AI features.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For incident and vulnerability questions, use the STAR structure but make sure your 'Action' section shows three things: how fast you moved to contain, how you communicated risk to non-technical people, and what process improvement you put in place afterwards. Atomicwork is a lean team, so showing you can handle incidents without a large SOC behind you matters.

For architecture and design questions, lead with your threat modelling approach. Name the specific threat (data leakage, privilege escalation, injection), describe the control you chose, and explain why you chose it over alternatives. Interviewers want to see structured thinking, not a checklist recitation.

For prioritization questions, avoid saying you 'use CVSS scores.' Interviewers have heard this many times. Instead, show that you layer CVSS with exploitability-in-context and business impact. Mention that a medium-severity bug on a customer-facing, PII-handling endpoint can outrank a theoretical critical finding on an internal tool.

For compliance and framework questions, ground your answer in what you actually did: which controls you owned, how you gathered evidence, how you worked with engineering to remediate gaps. Naming a framework like SOC 2 or NIST means nothing unless you can describe the hands-on work behind it.

For communication questions, give a concrete example of a time you translated a technical risk into a business-impact statement. Show the before ('I told the team there was a server-side request forgery') and the after ('I reframed it as: an attacker could use our server to reach internal cloud metadata and steal credentials, which could mean a full environment takeover').

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Atomicwork serves enterprise customers who scrutinize their vendors' security posture closely. Interviewers are typically looking for four things.

Ownership without hand-holding. Because the security team is small, they need someone who can run a review, write the finding, coordinate the fix, and close the loop, all without waiting to be told what to do at each step.

Product and engineering empathy. Security engineers who block features without offering alternatives frustrate product teams. Candidates who can say 'here is the risk, here are two ways to address it, here is my recommendation' stand out.

Cloud-native fluency. Atomicwork runs on the cloud. Expect questions about IAM policies, storage misconfigurations, container security, and secrets management in a Kubernetes or similar environment.

Clear written and verbal communication. Enterprise sales often depends on security questionnaires, penetration test reports, and trust documentation. Interviewers pay attention to how clearly you explain risk, not just whether you can find it.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Phase 1: Research

Spend the first few days studying Atomicwork's product in depth. Read their security and trust documentation if publicly available. Understand what data their platform processes and which enterprise compliance requirements their customers care about.

Review the core technical areas likely to come up: cloud IAM (AWS or GCP), multi-tenant SaaS security patterns, API security vulnerabilities, and secrets management.

Phase 2: Technical depth

Refresh your knowledge of common compliance frameworks (SOC 2 controls, NIST CSF, PCI-DSS basics) at a practical level, meaning what controls look like in a real engineering environment, not just what the documents say. Review recent CVEs relevant to cloud and SaaS environments and practice explaining their business impact in plain language.

Phase 3: Stories and mock interviews

Build three to four STAR stories covering: an incident you handled, a prioritization decision you made, a time you pushed back on a product decision, and a compliance or audit you supported.

Do two to three mock technical interviews. Practice the threat modelling exercise out loud, because thinking through a problem while someone watches you is different from thinking alone. Prepare two or three specific questions to ask the panel about how the security team works with product and engineering day to day.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating compliance as the goal. Saying 'we passed our SOC 2 audit' is not the same as saying 'I implemented the controls that made us pass.' Interviewers want to hear what you personally did.

Listing tools instead of impact. 'I used Burp Suite and Semgrep' tells an interviewer nothing. 'I integrated Semgrep into our CI pipeline and reduced the number of injection-class findings that reached production' tells a story.

Skipping the business context. Security engineers who talk only in CVE IDs and CVSS scores miss the point. Practice translating every vulnerability into a sentence that a product manager or a CFO would understand.

Being vague about cloud security. If you say you have cloud security experience, be ready to go deep on IAM policy structure, least-privilege patterns, or a specific misconfiguration you caught. Surface-level answers get probed hard.

Not asking questions. Candidates who have no questions for the panel often come across as uninterested. Prepare genuine questions about the team's current challenges, how security is embedded in the development process, or how customer security requirements influence the roadmap.

How many rounds does the Atomicwork Security Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report a process that typically runs three to four rounds. This usually includes a recruiter or HR screen, one or two technical rounds focused on security concepts and past experience, and a final round with a senior engineer or team lead. The exact structure can vary, so confirm the details with your recruiter after the first call.

Does Atomicwork ask coding questions in the Security Engineer interview?

Candidates report that the focus is primarily on security concepts, architecture discussions, and past experience rather than competitive coding. You may be asked to write a simple script, review a code snippet for vulnerabilities, or walk through a technical exercise. Brushing up on Python or Bash for security automation tasks is a reasonable precaution.
