01 Overview

Overview

Spinny is one of India's leading full-stack used car platforms, built on a technology core that powers everything from vehicle inspection to buyer financing. The Technical Program Manager (TPM) role sits at the crossroads of engineering, product, and city-level operations, making it one of the more demanding and rewarding positions in the company.

As of mid-2026, Spinny has 7 open roles on knok's tracker. Nationally, there are 313 TPM openings across India, with Bangalore leading at 41 openings, followed by Delhi (14), Pune (13), and Hyderabad (12). Spinny's active hiring reflects a broader push to scale its tech teams as the used car market matures.

Candidates report the interview process typically runs 3-5 rounds, covering technical depth, program management scenarios, and leadership discussions. Expect questions that test your ability to work through ambiguity, coordinate across engineering and business teams, and drive large programs to completion in a fast-moving environment.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How would you manage a program to roll out Spinny's vehicle inspection technology across a new set of cities?

*Situation:* At my previous company, we needed to roll out a field inspection tool across several new cities in a tight window. Each city had different network constraints, staffing models, and device availability.

*Task:* I was the program lead, responsible for coordinating mobile engineering, backend infrastructure, city ops leads, and the training team.

*Action:* I created a phased rollout plan, piloting in 2 cities first with a strict go/no-go checklist, then expanding in batches. I set up a weekly sync with all workstream leads, built a shared tracker in Confluence, and sent a daily async update flagging blockers. When device procurement slipped in one city, I renegotiated the launch sequence so training could run in parallel without losing time.

*Result:* We launched across all target cities within the committed window. The phased approach let us catch a critical offline-sync bug before it hit the larger rollout, which saved significant rework.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you built alignment between engineering and business stakeholders who had conflicting priorities.

*Situation:* Our product team wanted to ship a new seller-facing feature by a specific quarter, but the backend team had committed that same sprint to a critical infrastructure migration.

*Task:* I was accountable for both programs and needed to resolve the conflict without derailing either.

*Action:* I scheduled a focused session with the engineering lead and the product director. I brought a written impact analysis showing the cost of delaying the infrastructure work (tech debt, on-call risk) against the business impact of delaying the feature. Together we agreed to a short feature delay with a firm new date, and the product team used the extra time to sharpen their acceptance criteria.

*Result:* Both programs shipped within their revised timelines. The product director noted the extra time actually improved feature quality. The engineering team appreciated being heard rather than overruled.

---

Q: Walk me through a program that failed or slipped. What did you learn?

*Situation:* I was running a cross-functional data pipeline program with a hard external deadline tied to a regulatory filing.

*Task:* I was accountable for delivery across 3 engineering squads and a data science team.

*Action:* I had underestimated the dependency on a third-party API with inconsistent uptime. I caught it late, escalated quickly, and pulled in a parallel workaround, but it still added time to the timeline.

*Result:* We missed the internal deadline and delivered later than planned. The regulatory filing was still on time because we had buffer built in, but the internal miss was costly to trust. After this, I added a 'critical dependency review' step to my program kickoff template to surface third-party risks early in every engagement.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use STAR for every story-based question. Situation sets context (keep it brief), Task clarifies your specific role, Action is where you spend the most time (what you personally did, not what the team did), and Result is the measurable or observable outcome. Spend the least time on Situation and the most on Action.

For 'how would you approach' questions, use a structured think-aloud: state your goal, name the stakeholders you would involve, describe the first 2-3 concrete steps, then explain how you would track progress and define success. Interviewers want to see your logic, not just your conclusion.

For prioritization questions, use a simple framework: impact on the core business metric (for Spinny, that is likely transaction volume or customer trust), effort to implement, and reversibility. Name the framework out loud so the interviewer can follow your reasoning.

For conflict or negotiation questions, lead with facts rather than feelings. Show you brought data to the conversation, not just opinions. Interviewers at Spinny typically look for candidates who can influence without authority, since TPMs rarely have direct control over engineering teams.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Spinny TPM interviewers are typically looking for a few core signals, based on what candidates report.

Cross-functional coordination at scale. Spinny runs operations across multiple cities, with engineering, ops, and business teams working in parallel. They want to see that you have managed programs with real complexity, not just tracked tickets in a project management tool.

Comfort with ambiguity. The used car market moves fast and requirements change. Interviewers probe for how you make decisions when information is incomplete and how you re-plan when priorities shift mid-program.

Technical credibility without being hands-on. You do not need to write code, but you should hold a substantive conversation with an engineering team. Expect at least one question testing whether you understand system design trade-offs at a conceptual level, for example latency versus throughput, or the implications of splitting a monolith on a growing platform.

Ownership mindset. Spinny is in a growth phase. They want TPMs who treat programs as their own accountability, not as coordination tasks. Use language that shows you drove outcomes rather than managed processes.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Company and domain research.
Read everything publicly available about Spinny's product, city expansion history, and any tech content the team has published. Understand the used car buying and selling flow end to end. Map out which engineering domains a TPM at Spinny would likely touch: mobile apps, inspection tools, pricing systems, financing integrations, and logistics.

Week 2: Build your story bank.
Write out 8-10 STAR stories from your own experience. Tag each one with themes: conflict resolution, delivery under pressure, cross-team alignment, stakeholder communication, and technical trade-offs. Spinny interviews typically probe most of these themes across rounds.

Week 3: Practice and mock interviews.
Do at least 3 mock interviews with a peer or mentor. Focus on conciseness: your STAR answers should land clearly without rambling. Record yourself once to catch filler words and pacing issues.

Day before the interview.
Review the specific job description again. Note any keywords, for example 'scaled programs', 'city expansion', or 'data-driven', and make sure you can connect at least one story to each. Prepare 3 thoughtful questions for the interviewer about the team's current programs and how success is measured.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Talking about the team instead of yourself. Interviewers want to know what you did. Replace 'we built' with 'I drove' or 'I coordinated.' You can acknowledge team effort, but your individual contribution must be clear.

Skipping the result. Many candidates give a strong Situation and Action, then end with 'and it went well.' Quantify the outcome wherever possible. If you do not have a hard number, use relative language: 'we shipped 2 weeks ahead of schedule' or 'escalations dropped significantly in the next quarter.'

Being vague about technical depth. Saying 'I coordinated the backend team' is weaker than 'I worked with the backend team to redesign the API contract so the mobile team could ship independently.' The second version shows you understand how technical decisions affect program timelines.

Preparing only for the first-level question. Spinny interviewers typically probe with follow-ups, not just the opening question. If you say 'I escalated to leadership,' expect: 'How did you decide when to escalate?' Have a second layer ready for every answer.

Not researching Spinny specifically. Generic TPM answers that could apply to any company signal low interest. Reference Spinny's specific context, multi-city operations, inspection-led trust model, or tech-driven pricing, at least once per round.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Spinny TPM interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically runs 3-5 rounds. This usually includes a recruiter screen, one or two technical or program management discussions, and a final leadership or culture-fit conversation. The exact number can vary by level and team, so confirm the structure with your recruiter after the first call.

Does the Spinny TPM interview include a system design component?

Candidates report that system design comes up, but typically at a conceptual level rather than a whiteboard coding exercise. You may be asked to walk through how you would structure a program around a technical system, for example how an inspection data pipeline should be organized, rather than designing the system itself. Brushing up on common architectural trade-offs at a high level is sufficient preparation.

What is the salary range for a TPM at Spinny?

Spinny does not publicly publish salary bands for this role. Publicly reported figures on platforms like Glassdoor and levels.fyi for TPM roles at similar-stage Indian startups vary widely by level and experience. Use offer data from comparable companies as your benchmark and come prepared to discuss your expectations directly.

Is prior experience in automotive or e-commerce required?

Domain experience helps but is not typically a hard requirement for a TPM role. What matters more is your track record managing complex, cross-functional programs in a fast-moving tech company. Candidates from fintech, logistics, healthtech, and other sectors regularly clear Spinny interviews by drawing clear parallels between their experience and Spinny's operational context.

How should I frame my answers to sound Spinny-specific?

Start by understanding Spinny's core business: how a car is listed, inspected, priced, and sold. When telling your STAR stories, briefly connect your past work to a similar challenge Spinny faces, such as city expansion, multi-team coordination, or inspection quality at scale. This shows you have thought about the role, not just prepared generic answers.
