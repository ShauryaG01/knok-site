02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions are commonly reported by candidates interviewing at BI and analytics software companies. Adapt each answer to your own experience.

  1. Walk me through how you would design a reporting pipeline that pulls data from multiple sources and delivers aggregated results to a dashboard.
  2. How do you diagnose and fix a slow-running SQL query? Give a concrete example from your past work.
  3. What is the difference between OLAP and OLTP systems, and when would you choose one over the other?
  4. How would you design a REST API that serves large volumes of dashboard data to a front-end client without overwhelming it?
  5. Describe your experience with ETL or ELT pipelines. What tools, frameworks, or patterns have you used?
  6. How do you handle breaking schema changes in a production database without causing downtime for users?
  7. A customer reports that their dashboard takes too long to load. Walk us through your debugging and resolution process.
  8. How would you build a multi-tenant data architecture where each customer's data stays isolated from others?
  9. Tell me about a time you had to process or query a very large dataset. What made it challenging and how did you solve it?
  10. How do you ensure data accuracy and consistency when multiple services are writing to the same database?
  11. How do you approach writing testable, maintainable code in a codebase that is heavily data-driven?
  12. Describe how you have worked with non-technical stakeholders, such as data analysts or product managers, to turn a vague business requirement into a concrete technical feature.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Practise these STAR-format answers out loud. Adapt the specifics to your own projects.

Q: How do you diagnose and fix a slow-running SQL query?

*Situation:* At my previous company, a reporting query that summarised monthly sales across several tables was taking far too long to run, causing the dashboard page to time out for users.

*Task:* I was asked to bring the query response time down to an acceptable level without changing the underlying data model.

*Action:* I ran EXPLAIN ANALYZE to identify the bottleneck, which turned out to be a sequential scan on a large transactions table. I added a composite index on the date and customer columns, rewrote a correlated subquery as a JOIN, and added a materialised view for the monthly aggregation so the heavy computation ran nightly rather than on each page load.

*Result:* Query time improved dramatically. The dashboard loaded reliably for all users, and the product team was able to add new chart widgets without any performance concerns.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you handled a breaking schema change in production.

*Situation:* We needed to split a wide 'user_profile' table into two separate tables to support a new feature, but the table was used by many services in production.

*Task:* My job was to migrate the schema with zero downtime and no data loss.

*Action:* I used a phased approach. First, I added the new tables and wrote a dual-write layer so every write went to both the old and new schemas simultaneously. Second, I ran a backfill job to migrate existing rows in small batches during off-peak hours. Third, once the new tables were verified, I updated all services to read from them and removed the dual-write. I wrote automated checks after each phase to confirm row counts matched exactly.

*Result:* The migration completed over two weekends with no production incidents, and all services switched cleanly to the new schema.

---

Q: Describe how you worked with a non-technical stakeholder to turn a vague requirement into a feature.

*Situation:* A product manager came to us with a request: 'I want users to see how their numbers compare to last year.' There were no further specs.

*Task:* I needed to translate this into a concrete technical design before we could estimate or build anything.

*Action:* I set up a short discovery call with the PM and used targeted questions to clarify which metrics to show, which user segments were in scope, what time granularity was needed, and what should happen when prior-year data is missing. I then wrote a one-page spec and walked the PM through a wireframe to confirm alignment before writing a single line of code.

*Result:* We shipped the year-over-year comparison feature in one sprint with no scope changes, because we had agreed on all edge cases upfront.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions

Most 'tell me about a time...' questions expect a structured story. Keep each part tight.

  • *Situation:* One or two sentences on the context.
  • *Task:* Your specific responsibility, not the team's.
  • *Action:* What YOU did, step by step. This should be the longest part.
  • *Result:* A concrete outcome, ideally tied to a clear business impact.

RADIO for system design questions

For open-ended design questions ('How would you build X?'), a useful structure is:

  • *Requirements:* Clarify functional and non-functional needs before drawing anything.
  • *Architecture:* Sketch the high-level components and how data flows between them.
  • *Data model:* Define the key tables or data structures.
  • *Infrastructure:* Discuss storage, caching, and scalability choices.
  • *Observability:* Explain how you would monitor and debug the system.

For SQL and coding questions

Think out loud. State your approach before writing code. If you spot a simpler solution mid-way, say so and explain why you are switching. Interviewers at data companies like UsefulBI typically value clear reasoning over raw speed.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Data-system fluency, not just coding ability

UsefulBI builds tools for data teams, so engineers who can talk comfortably about SQL optimisation, data pipelines, and reporting architecture stand out. You do not need to be a data engineer, but you should be comfortable reasoning about how data moves and where it slows down.

Clean, readable code

Candidates report that reviewers pay attention to code clarity, naming, and structure, not just whether the solution runs. Avoid over-engineering. A simple, well-named solution beats a clever but unreadable one.

Communication and collaboration

Because UsefulBI's engineers typically work closely with analysts and product managers, interviewers look for people who can explain technical decisions in plain language. Practise summarising your choices without using jargon.

Ownership mindset

Expect questions about how you handled ambiguity, bugs in production, or disagreements with teammates. Interviewers want to see that you take responsibility and follow through, not that everything always went smoothly.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Follow this plan if you have a few weeks before your interview.

Week 1: Foundations
- Revise core SQL: JOINs, window functions, GROUP BY, query optimisation, and indexing strategies.
- Review data structures and algorithms at a medium difficulty level.
- Read about ETL vs ELT patterns and when to use each.
- Set up a small personal project or be ready to discuss a past project in detail.

Week 2: System Design and Company Context
- Study system design for data-heavy applications: read-heavy APIs, caching layers, database sharding, and multi-tenancy.
- Research UsefulBI's product: understand what kind of BI tool it is, who the users are, and what problems it solves.
- Practise explaining your past projects using the STAR framework.
- Do a few mock interviews, ideally with a peer who can give honest feedback.

Week 3: Polish and Practise
- Do a timed coding session every day.
- Prepare several STAR stories covering ownership, technical decisions, conflict, and failure.
- Write down a few thoughtful questions to ask the interviewer at the end.
- Review any take-home or online assessment feedback if you are already at that stage.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

1. Jumping into code without clarifying requirements

For design and open-ended questions, candidates who ask zero clarifying questions often end up solving the wrong problem. Always spend a few minutes aligning on scope before you start writing or drawing anything.

2. Ignoring data-layer considerations

At a BI company, saying 'I would just store it in a database' is not enough. Think through indexing, query patterns, and what happens at scale. You do not need perfect answers, but you need to ask the right questions.

3. Vague STAR answers

Answers like 'we improved performance significantly' lose points. Tie every result to a concrete outcome, even if you have to say 'my manager told me this was the smoothest migration the team had done.' Specificity signals credibility.

4. Not asking questions at the end

Candidates who say 'I have no questions' often come across as uninterested. Prepare a few genuine questions about the team, the product roadmap, or the engineering culture.

5. Treating every question as a coding problem

Some questions are about judgement and communication. If an interviewer asks how you would handle a disagreement with a colleague, they want to hear your reasoning, not a technical solution.
