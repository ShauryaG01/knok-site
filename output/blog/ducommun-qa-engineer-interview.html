01 Overview

Overview

Ducommun is a US-based aerospace and defense manufacturer with a history of supplying structural components and electronic systems to major defense and commercial aerospace programs. With 134 open roles currently listed, they are actively hiring across disciplines, and QA Engineer is one of the more critical positions given the strict quality requirements in aerospace manufacturing.

The interview process typically follows a few rounds: a recruiter screen, one or two technical interviews, and sometimes a panel or hiring manager round. Candidates report that technical questions focus heavily on quality systems, inspection methods, and how you handle non-conformances in a manufacturing environment. Some interviewers also probe your familiarity with aerospace-specific quality requirements, so knowing the vocabulary and processes matters even if you come from a different regulated industry.

Knowing how Ducommun positions itself (defense, commercial aerospace, complex assemblies) helps you frame your answers. Tailor every story to manufacturing quality, not software testing.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Here are the questions candidates report most often in Ducommun QA Engineer interviews. Study each one and prepare a specific, example-backed answer.

  1. Walk us through your hands-on experience with quality management system requirements in aerospace or another regulated manufacturing environment.
  2. Do you have experience with First Article Inspection (FAI)? Describe a FAI you led or supported.
  3. How do you write and close a non-conformance report (NCR)? Walk us through the steps.
  4. Describe how you would apply Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA) during a new product introduction.
  5. How do you handle a situation where production is pushing to ship a part that does not meet drawing requirements?
  6. What inspection techniques and measuring equipment have you used, and how do you ensure calibration compliance?
  7. Tell me about a time you identified a recurring defect and drove a corrective action that actually fixed the root cause.
  8. How do you read and interpret engineering drawings, GD&T callouts, and tolerances?
  9. Describe your experience with Statistical Process Control (SPC) or other data-driven quality tools.
  10. How do you prioritize when multiple inspection requests hit your queue at the same time?
  11. Tell me about a time you had to push back on engineering or production. What happened?
  12. What does a strong supplier quality program look like, and have you ever conducted a supplier audit?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time you identified a recurring defect and drove a corrective action that fixed the root cause.

*Situation:* At my previous company we were seeing repeated dimensional rejections on a machined bracket. The same callout kept failing incoming inspection roughly once every two weeks.

*Task:* I was responsible for closing the NCR, but I decided to dig deeper rather than just documenting the rejection and sending parts back.

*Action:* I pulled six months of NCR data, mapped the failures against specific machine operators and shift patterns, and ran a fishbone analysis with the machining supervisor. We discovered the CNC program had a tool offset that drifted after a certain number of cycles. No one had flagged it because each individual rejection looked like a one-off. I worked with the programmer to add an in-process check at the right cycle count and updated the control plan.

*Result:* That specific rejection dropped to zero over the next three months. The corrective action was shared across two sister lines with similar setups, preventing the issue from surfacing there.

---

Q: How do you handle a situation where production is pushing to ship a part that does not meet drawing requirements?

*Situation:* During a high-pressure delivery week, a production lead asked me to pass a batch of housings that had a surface finish measurement just outside the drawing tolerance. The customer was waiting.

*Task:* My role was to make the final inspection call, and I had to balance schedule pressure against compliance.

*Action:* I explained to the supervisor that accepting without a formal deviation would be a quality escape risk and could affect our certification standing. I raised a Material Review Board (MRB) request the same day, gathered the engineering data, and contacted the customer liaison to ask whether they could grant a concession with supporting data. I documented everything in the system so the paper trail was clean.

*Result:* The customer granted a one-time concession within four hours because we had the right data ready. Shipment went out the same day. The supervisor later said handling it through MRB protected the team from a potential audit finding.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you led or supported a First Article Inspection (FAI).

*Situation:* We were onboarding a new supplier for a structural bracket going into a defense subassembly. This was their first delivery of this part number.

*Task:* I was assigned to lead the FAI, which meant verifying the supplier's documentation package, dimensional results, material certs, and process records before we accepted the first lot.

*Action:* I created a FAI checklist aligned to the program's quality requirements, coordinated with the supplier's QA team to get all documentation in advance, and then spent a day on-site witnessing key inspections and spot-checking measurements against the ballooned drawing. I found two characteristics where the supplier's measurement method did not match the drawing intent, and we corrected those before first article approval.

*Result:* The first production lot came in clean with zero dimensional rejections. The early catch saved rework cost and avoided a potential schedule slip on the program.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For quality system and compliance questions. Anchor your answer in a specific standard or process you have personally worked within, describe your day-to-day involvement (not just awareness), and connect it to an outcome like an audit pass, a reduction in escapes, or a successful certification. Avoid saying you 'know' a standard without giving a concrete example.

For conflict and pressure questions. Use a three-part structure: what the pressure was, how you held the line using process (MRB, deviation request, escalation path), and what the result was. Interviewers want to see that you stay calm and use the system rather than bending rules or creating unnecessary conflict.

For root cause and corrective action questions. Name the tool you used (fishbone, five-Why, fault tree), describe the data you gathered, and explain the containment action separately from the long-term fix. Showing you know the difference between containment and true corrective action signals real QA maturity.

For technical inspection questions. Be specific about equipment (CMM, optical comparator, surface profilometer, thread gauges), mention calibration awareness, and reference GD&T callouts if relevant. Generic answers like 'I used various tools' will not impress an aerospace interviewer.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Compliance ownership. They want engineers who treat quality standards as the floor, not an obstacle. Show that you have personally owned compliance tasks, not just supported them.

Manufacturing context. Ducommun makes physical parts for demanding programs. Answers rooted in a factory floor, not a software test lab or an office-based audit function, will land better.

Communication under pressure. QA engineers at aerospace manufacturers regularly have to deliver bad news to production, suppliers, or customers. Candidates who can show they did this professionally, using the right escalation paths, stand out.

Data instinct. They want to see that your first response to a quality problem is to pull data, not guess. Mention specific metrics you tracked, charts you used, or trends you spotted.

Collaborative problem-solving. Aerospace QA is a team sport. Show you have worked closely with engineering, production, and suppliers rather than policing from a distance.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week one: review the technical foundations. Refresh your knowledge of aerospace quality management principles, especially documentation requirements, traceability, and control plans. Brush up on GD&T interpretation and the measuring tools common in precision manufacturing. If your background is in software or a non-manufacturing industry, spend extra time on inspection workflows and NCR processes.

Week two: build your story bank. Map your work history to the top question areas: FAI, NCR and MRB, corrective action, supplier quality, and handling production pressure. Write out at least one STAR story for each. Practice saying them out loud so they sound natural, not rehearsed.

Research Ducommun. Look up their product lines, major customers, and any recent program wins. Understand that they serve both commercial aerospace and defense, and that quality standards in both segments are demanding. Mentioning a specific program area shows genuine interest.

Prepare your questions. Ask about the team's current audit calendar, how NCRs are tracked, what quality tools they use, and what a successful first year looks like in this role. Good questions signal that you take the job seriously.

Logistics. Candidates report that Ducommun interviews can include a practical component or a drawing review exercise. Bring a printed resume, know your measurement experience cold, and be ready to walk through a ballooned drawing if asked.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Vague answers without outcomes. Saying 'I improved quality' without any specific result will not satisfy an aerospace interviewer. Even if you cannot share exact figures, describe the direction and scale of improvement.

Knowing concepts but not applying them. Reciting quality management principles without showing how you used them in a real situation reads as theoretical. Every tool or method you mention should come with a personal example.

Underestimating the compliance culture. Some candidates treat quality standards as bureaucratic overhead. In aerospace manufacturing, compliance is the core of the job. Any hint that you see it as box-checking will hurt your chances.

Skipping containment when describing corrective action. A common gap is jumping straight to root cause without mentioning what you did to stop the bleeding immediately. Show you know: contain first, then investigate, then fix permanently.

Not researching Ducommun's business. Generic answers that could apply to any manufacturing company miss the chance to show you understand the demands of aerospace and defense programs. Reference their product focus in at least one or two answers.
