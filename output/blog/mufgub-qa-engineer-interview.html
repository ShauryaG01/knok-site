01 Overview

Overview

Mufgub is hiring QA Engineers at scale in 2026, with 105 open roles currently across India. Candidates report a structured interview process, typically covering manual testing fundamentals, automation experience, scenario-based problem solving, and a behavioural round. The process usually runs two to four rounds, though the exact structure varies by team.

This guide is built specifically for the Mufgub QA Engineer interview. It covers the questions that come up most, how to frame your answers, and a two-week prep plan you can start today. Whether you are at entry level (0-2 years) or mid-level (3-5 years), the core areas tested stay the same: the depth of answers expected is what changes.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These 12 questions come up frequently in Mufgub QA Engineer interviews, based on candidate reports. Study each one and have a concrete example ready.

  1. Walk me through how you would test a brand-new feature from scratch.
  2. How do you decide which test cases to automate and which to keep manual?
  3. Describe a time you caught a critical bug late in the release cycle. What did you do?
  4. What testing tools and frameworks have you used, and why do you prefer them?
  5. How do you write a test plan when requirements are incomplete or keep changing?
  6. Explain the difference between functional, regression, and exploratory testing, and give an example of each from your own work.
  7. How do you handle a situation where a developer pushes back on a bug you raised?
  8. Describe your experience with API testing. Which tools do you use and how do you structure your test cases?
  9. How do you prioritise what to test when you have very little time before a release?
  10. Have you worked with CI/CD pipelines? How do you fit QA into a continuous deployment workflow?
  11. How do you measure whether your testing is actually effective?
  12. Tell me about a process or workflow improvement you drove within a QA team.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time you caught a critical bug late in the release cycle.

*Situation:* Our team was two days away from releasing a payments feature. I was doing final regression testing.

*Task:* My job was to confirm existing test cases passed. I chose to also run exploratory tests because payments are high-risk.

*Action:* I simulated a network timeout mid-transaction and found that the app deducted the amount but showed no confirmation, leaving users unable to tell if the payment went through. I documented the steps, expected versus actual behaviour, and the risk level, then flagged it immediately to the developer and product manager with a short video recording.

*Result:* The release was delayed by one day to fix the issue. The product manager later said this catch prevented what could have been a significant customer support escalation.

---

Q: How do you prioritise testing when time is very short before a release?

*Situation:* At a previous company, a last-minute feature request was added three days before a sprint release. Running the full test suite would take far longer than that.

*Task:* I had to decide what to test, in what order, to give the team the most confidence in the least time.

*Action:* I listed all test cases and sorted them by: risk to the user (payments, login, data loss first), whether the area changed in this sprint, and whether automation already covered it. I automated the highest-priority flows overnight using Selenium and ran the rest manually in priority order. I shared a status log with the team every few hours.

*Result:* We shipped on time. Two minor defects were found post-release in lower-priority flows, nothing critical. The team adopted my priority approach for future crunch situations.

---

Q: Tell me about a QA process improvement you drove.

*Situation:* My team had no shared test case library. Every tester wrote cases independently, leading to duplicates, gaps, and no consistency.

*Task:* I proposed and led the effort to build a centralised test repository.

*Action:* I mapped all product modules, assigned ownership to team members, and ran two workshops to review and consolidate existing cases. I set up a folder structure in our test management tool and wrote a short guide on naming and tagging cases going forward.

*Result:* Within two months, coverage for core flows improved to include all documented requirements, verified by a traceability matrix. Onboarding new QA engineers dropped from taking over a week to about three days, according to team feedback.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use STAR for every behavioural question. Situation and Task together should take no more than thirty seconds to speak. Action is where you spend most of your time, because that is what the interviewer is evaluating. Result should be concrete: time saved, bugs caught, releases unblocked, or team feedback received.

For technical scenario questions, use a structured walkthrough:

  1. Clarify requirements. What does 'done' mean? What are the edge cases?
  2. Identify risk areas. Where is a failure most damaging to the user?
  3. Design test types. Unit, integration, end-to-end, exploratory, performance.
  4. Define entry and exit criteria. When do you start, and when are you confident enough to sign off?
  5. State your tools. Name the actual tools you would use and why.

For disagreement questions (developer pushback, timeline pressure), show that you can hold your ground professionally. Say what evidence you bring (reproducible steps, logs, video), who you escalate to if needed, and that you document the decision either way. Interviewers want to see that you do not simply fold, but also that you do not create unnecessary friction.

For 'how do you measure quality' questions, mention defect escape rate (bugs found in production versus in QA), test coverage, and mean time to detect. If you have used dashboards or reports to communicate quality status to stakeholders, mention that too.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Mufgub QA interviewers typically look for three things above technical skill level.

Ownership. They want to see that you treat quality as your responsibility, not just a checklist. Candidates who say 'I flagged it to the developer and moved on' score lower than those who followed up, verified the fix, and checked for related issues.

Communication across roles. QA sits between development and product. Interviewers test whether you can explain a bug clearly to a developer, translate a risk into business language for a product manager, and push back respectfully when you disagree. Your clarity in the interview itself is also being evaluated.

Automation judgement. At mid and senior levels, the question is not just 'can you write automation scripts' but 'do you know when not to automate.' Candidates who automate everything, or who resist automation entirely, both raise flags. Show that you make the call based on stability, frequency of use, and return on investment.

Attention to edge cases. Walk through a test scenario in your answer and include edge cases (empty inputs, network failures, concurrent users) without being prompted. This signals seniority regardless of your experience band.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Build your technical foundation

Days 1-3: Revisit the core testing concepts that come up in every QA interview. Review the testing pyramid (unit, integration, end-to-end), different testing types (smoke, sanity, regression, exploratory, performance), and how to write a test plan. Write definitions in your own words.

Days 4-5: Pick one automation framework you have used (Selenium, Cypress, Appium, or Postman) and refresh your knowledge. Practice writing a set of test scripts for a sample login or checkout flow. Be ready to walk through your code line by line.

Days 6-7: Practice API testing basics. Use a free public API to write GET, POST, and edge-case tests in Postman or a similar tool. Review which HTTP status codes matter and what you check in an API response beyond the status code.

Week 2: Apply and refine

Early week 2: Write out your three to five strongest work examples using the STAR format. Cover: a critical bug you caught, a process you improved, a disagreement you resolved, and a tight deadline you managed. Read them aloud to check timing and clarity.

Mid-week 2: Research Mufgub. Look at the company's products, recent news, and the job description you applied for. Connect what you know about their domain to your testing experience. Prepare one specific question for the interviewer about their QA culture or tooling.

Late week 2: Do two mock interviews. Record yourself or ask a peer to play interviewer. Time your answers and cut anything that is too long or vague.

Final day: Rest, review your notes lightly, check your setup (video, audio, internet if remote), and go in confident.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Giving answers with no specifics. 'I always prioritise high-risk areas' is not an answer. An answer is: 'I ranked all open test cases by user impact and by whether the code changed, then ran the highest-priority ones manually while automation handled the rest.' Specifics signal real experience.

Ignoring edge cases in live technical questions. When an interviewer asks you to design tests for a feature, most candidates cover the happy path and stop. Senior QAs cover empty states, invalid inputs, concurrent access, and failure recovery. Including these unprompted is a strong signal.

Treating automation as the answer to everything. Saying 'I would automate all of this' when asked about a one-off exploratory test, or a UI that changes every sprint, signals poor judgement. Be ready to explain your automation decision, not just your automation ability.

Being passive about bugs and quality. Interviewers notice candidates who say 'I raised the bug' versus candidates who say 'I raised the bug, attached a reproduction video, escalated when it was deprioritised, and re-tested the fix.' Passive language reads as low ownership.

Skipping the result in STAR answers. Many candidates tell a strong Situation, Task, and Action story and then trail off. Always close with what happened: a metric, a team outcome, a release that went smoothly, or a process that stuck.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Mufgub QA Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report two to four rounds, typically starting with a recruiter call, followed by a technical round covering testing concepts and scenario questions, and then a behavioural or managerial round. Some teams add a practical assignment where you write test cases or automation scripts for a sample feature. The exact structure varies by team and level.

What salary can I expect as a QA Engineer at Mufgub?

Based on the knok jobradar data as of mid-2026, QA Engineer salaries in India broadly range from 4-9 LPA at entry level (0-2 years), 9-17 LPA at mid level (3-5 years), 17-30 LPA at senior level (6-9 years), and 28-45+ LPA at lead level. Mufgub-specific numbers are not publicly confirmed, so treat these as a market reference and check Glassdoor or levels.fyi for more recent company-specific reports.

Does Mufgub ask coding questions in QA interviews?

Candidates typically report that competitive coding questions are not the main focus for QA roles, but automation-related coding does come up. You may be asked to write or review test scripts in a language listed on your resume, such as Python or Java. Knowing how to write clean, readable test code matters more than algorithm-style problem solving.

How important is automation experience for this role?

At mid and senior levels, automation experience is expected and will be discussed in depth. At entry level, showing that you understand when and why to automate, and that you have hands-on experience with at least one framework (Selenium, Cypress, Postman), is usually enough. What interviewers care about is your judgement on automation decisions, not just whether you can write scripts.

What tools should I be familiar with before the Mufgub QA interview?

Candidates most commonly mention Selenium, Postman, JIRA, and a test management tool such as TestRail. Basic knowledge of CI/CD tools like Jenkins, familiarity with SQL for database validation, and API concepts (REST, HTTP status codes, request and response structure) also come up frequently. Match what you claim on your resume to what you can walk through confidently in the interview.
