02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly based on what iOS candidates report from Sequoia Connect portfolio company interviews. Expect a mix of Swift fundamentals, mobile architecture, and product judgment.

  1. Walk me through how memory management works in Swift. When would you use 'weak' vs 'unowned' references, and why?
  2. Compare MVC, MVVM, and Clean Architecture. Walk through a decision you made when choosing one over another for a real project.
  3. You notice the app's launch time has degraded after a recent release. How do you investigate and fix it?
  4. How do you build offline-first functionality in an iOS app? Walk through your data sync strategy.
  5. How do you structure unit tests and UI tests in an iOS project? What is your philosophy on test coverage?
  6. Explain Swift concurrency. How have you used async/await or actors, and how does that differ from using Grand Central Dispatch directly?
  7. How would you design a reusable, configurable UI component in SwiftUI that your whole team can use across screens?
  8. Describe the most complex bug you have tracked down in an iOS app. What made it hard and how did you find it?
  9. How do you approach App Store submissions? Walk through certificates, provisioning profiles, and entitlements for a new app.
  10. Tell me about a time a product manager pushed for a feature you felt was technically risky. What happened?
  11. How do you decide which new iOS APIs or Swift language features to adopt in a production app?
  12. What have you done to improve engineering practices or the developer experience on a team you worked on?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format for every behavioural and technical experience question: Situation, Task, Action, Result. These three examples cover common Sequoia Connect interview themes.

Q: You noticed the app's launch time had degraded. How did you handle it?

*Situation:* Our app's cold launch time had crept up to over four seconds after we added a new home feed, and users were leaving before the first screen loaded.

*Task:* I was responsible for diagnosing the regression and bringing cold launch time back under two seconds without removing the feature.

*Action:* I profiled the app using Xcode Instruments, specifically the App Launch and Time Profiler templates. I found two issues: we were loading a large local SQLite database synchronously on the main thread at startup, and a third-party analytics SDK was initialising too early. I moved the database load to a background thread, deferred the analytics init to after the first screen rendered, and switched several non-critical modules to lazy loading.

*Result:* Cold launch time came down to under two seconds, measured across a sample of test devices. We monitored it in our performance dashboard and maintained the improvement through three subsequent releases.

---

Q: Tell me about a disagreement you had with a PM over a feature.

*Situation:* A product manager wanted to ship a real-time chat feature in a two-week sprint. After reviewing the scope, I believed we could not do it safely in that time without cutting corners on reliability.

*Task:* I needed to give honest technical input without blocking the team or appearing obstructive.

*Action:* I wrote a short breakdown showing exactly which parts of the feature were at risk: message delivery guarantees, push notifications in background states, and edge cases around connectivity loss. I proposed two options: a reduced scope version we could ship confidently in two weeks, or the full feature in five weeks. I walked the PM through both in a focused meeting rather than just saying no over chat.

*Result:* We agreed on the reduced scope for the first version, shipped on time, and got positive feedback. We delivered the full feature the following sprint. The PM said later they appreciated having structured options rather than a flat refusal.

---

Q: Describe how you built offline-first functionality into an app.

*Situation:* I worked on a field-service app where technicians operated in basements and warehouses with poor connectivity for most of their shift.

*Task:* The app needed to let users create, edit, and complete work orders with no internet connection, then sync reliably when connectivity returned.

*Action:* I designed a local-first architecture using Core Data as the source of truth. Every write went to Core Data first. A background sync manager observed network reachability using NWPathMonitor and batched pending changes when the connection came back. I handled conflicts using server timestamps as the tiebreaker and surfaced genuine conflicts to the user as a simple 'which version do you want to keep' screen.

*Result:* Technicians could complete full shifts offline without data loss. Sync failures dropped to near zero after the first two weeks in production, and support tickets about lost work orders stopped completely.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For iOS technical questions, structure your answer in three parts: the 'what' (define the concept clearly), the 'why it matters' (the real-world consequence), and the 'trade-off' (when you would choose something different). Interviewers at Sequoia Connect portfolio companies want to see you understand consequences, not just syntax.

For system design questions, start by clarifying scope. Ask about the target iOS version, expected data size, offline requirements, and team size. Then walk through your architecture choice, data layer, networking strategy, and how you would test the component. Thinking out loud matters as much as the final answer.

For behavioural questions, keep Situation and Task tight. Spend the most time on Action, since that is where your judgment and ownership show. For Result, include something observable: a user response, a follow-up from a stakeholder, or a metric from your monitoring setup, even if the sample size was small.

For 'why us' questions, be specific to the portfolio company you are interviewing with, not just Sequoia Connect in general. Mention a feature or product challenge you find genuinely interesting and connect it to your own experience. Generic answers about growth and learning are forgettable.

When you do not know an answer, say 'Let me think through this' rather than guessing immediately. If you do not know a specific API, describe how you would research and evaluate it. Interviewers respect candidates who are honest about gaps and show a clear learning process.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Deep Swift fluency, not just syntax recall. Interviewers want to see you understand why Swift works the way it does: ARC, value vs reference semantics, the ownership model, and how the compiler enforces safety. Expect to be pushed beyond the surface-level answer.

Product thinking alongside engineering judgment. Sequoia Connect portfolio companies tend to be product-led. Interviewers notice when a candidate connects a technical decision to user impact. Saying 'this approach reduced perceived load time, which mattered because our users were on older devices' lands better than a purely theoretical explanation.

Clean communication under pressure. iOS rounds often involve live coding or design discussions. Interviewers watch whether you explain your thinking as you go, handle feedback without getting defensive, and ask clarifying questions before diving in.

Evidence of personal ownership. 'We built this' is weaker than 'I owned this module, made this specific trade-off, and here is what I learned.' Personal accountability in your stories signals you will take the same ownership on their team.

Pragmatic quality standards. Startups at growth stage need engineers who care about quality but can also ship. Candidates who have never had to make a speed-quality trade-off can raise concerns. Show you understand when to be rigorous and when to move fast.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Foundations and story-building

Reread the Swift documentation on memory management, concurrency, and value types. Go through your past projects and pick three that best demonstrate your depth: one where you improved performance, one where you solved a hard architecture problem, and one where you navigated a cross-functional challenge. Write the STAR story for each before you start applying.

Week 2: iOS system design practice

Practice designing common iOS systems out loud: a photo upload flow with offline support, a real-time messaging screen, a paginated feed with local caching. Time yourself and aim to cover architecture, data layer, networking, error handling, and testability within each session. Ask a peer to listen and push back on your choices.

Week 3: Company and role research

Identify the specific portfolio company you are interviewing with through Sequoia Connect. Read their recent product updates, app store reviews, and any engineering content they have published. Pinpoint one product problem you find interesting and think through how you would approach it technically. This preparation often shows clearly in the 'why us' conversation.

Week 4: Mock interviews and logistics

Do at least two mock technical interviews with live coding. Use LeetCode for Swift-specific practice on trees, graphs, sliding window, and two-pointer patterns. Confirm your Xcode setup and internet connection for remote rounds. Prepare two or three thoughtful questions to ask at the end of each round.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Reciting definitions instead of showing experience. When asked about weak references or async/await, candidates often give textbook answers. The question is an invitation to share a real example. Always anchor to something you built.

Skipping the trade-off in design questions. Every architectural choice has a cost. Candidates who only describe the benefits of their chosen approach, without acknowledging what they gave up, come across as less experienced.

Treating all rounds identically. A coding round and a behavioural round need different energy. Candidates who try to give STAR stories in a coding round, or write out code during a behavioural question, are not reading the room.

Under-preparing the 'why this company' answer. With Sequoia Connect, the portfolio company you are joining matters a lot. Vague answers about exciting startups signal low interest. Research the actual product.

Not asking about the role early enough. Candidates sometimes reach the final round before discovering the team size, tech stack, or product stage is not what they expected. Ask early and ask specifically.

Freezing when stuck during live coding. Silence while thinking can look like being lost. Narrate your thinking: 'I am considering a hash map here because lookups will be constant time, let me check whether memory constraints rule that out.' Interviewers want to see your process, not just your answer.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Sequoia Connect iOS Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically runs 3-5 rounds, though this varies by the specific portfolio company you are interviewing with. You can expect a screening call, one or two iOS technical rounds covering coding and architecture, and a final behavioural or culture conversation. Ask your recruiter for the exact structure after the first round.

Is SwiftUI knowledge required, or is UIKit experience enough?

Both matter, but the balance depends on the company's codebase. Many newer Sequoia Connect portfolio startups are SwiftUI-first or SwiftUI-heavy, while older products may still be UIKit-dominant. Candidates report being asked about both in technical rounds, with deeper SwiftUI questions becoming more common in 2025 and 2026. Knowing how to bridge UIKit components into a SwiftUI project is a strong differentiator.

Does prior startup experience matter for these roles?

It helps but is not required. Sequoia Connect portfolio companies tend to be early-to-mid stage startups, so interviewers pay attention to whether a candidate is comfortable with ambiguity, fast iteration, and wearing multiple hats. If you come from a large company, prepare examples that show you have worked in fast-moving, resource-constrained environments, even if the company itself was large.

What salary can I expect as an iOS Engineer at a Sequoia Connect portfolio company?

Sequoia Connect covers a wide range of companies at different stages, so compensation varies significantly. Glassdoor and industry surveys show a wide band for iOS Engineers in India depending on company stage, city, and seniority level. Delhi, which currently has the most iOS openings in this data, may have different benchmarks from Bangalore. Ask the recruiter for the band early in the process to avoid a late-stage mismatch.

How important is system design for an iOS Engineer role here?

Very important, especially for mid-level and senior roles. Candidates report that system design rounds focus on mobile-specific concerns: offline sync, caching strategies, efficient networking, state management, and component architecture. Unlike backend system design, you are unlikely to be asked about distributed databases, but you should be comfortable walking through the full data flow of an iOS feature end to end.

How long does the full process take from application to offer?

Candidates typically report 2-4 weeks for the full process, though this varies by company urgency and interview scheduling. Some portfolio companies in active hiring mode move faster. It is reasonable to ask your recruiter for an expected timeline after the first round, and to mention any competing offers so they can plan accordingly.
