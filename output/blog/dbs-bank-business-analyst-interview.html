02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly in DBS Bank BA interviews based on what candidates report. Have a concrete, specific example ready for each one.

  1. Walk me through a time you turned a complex business problem into a clear requirement document.
  2. DBS is a digital-first bank. How have you used data to drive a business decision in your previous role?
  3. How comfortable are you with SQL or BI tools? Give a specific example of how you have used them.
  4. How do you handle conflicting requirements from multiple stakeholders who each want different things?
  5. Describe a process improvement you identified and led. What was the measurable impact?
  6. Are you familiar with RBI guidelines or other banking regulations? How have they shaped your work?
  7. How do you bridge the gap between business teams and technology teams when requirements get lost in translation?
  8. If you were improving a feature on DBS's digibank app, how would you approach it as a BA?
  9. Tell me about a project that did not go as planned. What happened and how did you recover?
  10. How do you prioritise features or requirements when resources or timelines are tight?
  11. Describe your experience working in Agile or Scrum environments. What was your specific role in the team?
  12. How would you define and measure the success of a new digital banking product?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format for every behavioural question. Here are three worked examples.

Q: How do you handle conflicting requirements from multiple stakeholders?

*Situation:* At my previous company, the compliance team and the product team had completely opposite views on a customer onboarding feature. Compliance wanted a longer KYC flow; product wanted fewer steps to reduce drop-off.

*Task:* My job was to find a solution that satisfied both teams without delaying the launch.

*Action:* I set up a joint workshop, mapped each requirement to a specific regulation or user pain point, and created a prioritised list with clear rationale. I proposed a phased approach where the minimum compliance checks ran upfront and optional fields moved to a later screen.

*Result:* Both teams signed off, the launch happened on time, and the product team reported an improvement in onboarding completion in their post-launch review.

---

Q: Describe a process improvement you identified and led.

*Situation:* Our team was spending hours each week manually pulling data from three different internal systems to produce a weekly sales report.

*Task:* I was asked to find a way to reduce time spent on this task so the team could focus on analysis instead of data collection.

*Action:* I documented the current process, identified each data source, and worked with the data engineering team to build an automated pipeline. I also redesigned the report format based on what stakeholders actually used.

*Result:* The report that used to take a full working day now ran overnight and was ready each Monday morning. The team redirected those hours to deeper analysis work.

---

Q: Tell me about a project that did not go as planned. How did you recover?

*Situation:* I was the BA on a loan origination system migration. Midway through, we discovered that legacy data had quality issues that were not flagged during initial scoping.

*Task:* I needed to re-baseline the project scope and reset stakeholder expectations without derailing the entire timeline.

*Action:* I ran a rapid data audit, categorised issues by severity, and presented the steering committee with three options: fix everything now, fix only critical issues and defer the rest, or launch with documented limitations. The committee chose the middle option.

*Result:* We launched one sprint later than planned, with known limitations clearly documented, and a follow-on sprint addressed the deferred items. The project stayed alive and delivered value rather than being cancelled.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions. Every 'tell me about a time' question deserves a Situation, Task, Action, and Result. Keep Situation and Task brief, spend most of your time on Action, and always end with a measurable or observable Result. Vague endings like 'the project was successful' weaken an otherwise strong story.

Structured problem-solving for case questions. If DBS gives you a product or process case, use a clear structure: clarify the goal, state your assumptions, break the problem into parts, prioritise, then recommend with trade-offs stated openly. Saying 'let me take a moment to structure this' before answering signals maturity and is perfectly acceptable.

The Pyramid Principle for presenting findings. Lead with your conclusion, then support it with two or three key points, each backed by evidence. Interviewers at banks respond well to this because it mirrors how internal memos and business cases are written across most financial institutions.

Stakeholder mapping for process questions. When asked how you manage stakeholders, describing a simple influence-versus-interest grid shows that you think in frameworks, not just on instinct. You do not need to draw it out; explaining the concept verbally is enough to demonstrate the habit.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Digital mindset over legacy banking thinking. DBS consistently positions itself as a technology company that holds a banking licence. They want BAs who are comfortable with APIs, data pipelines, and digital product thinking, not only with traditional requirements documentation and sign-off processes.

Current domain knowledge. Interviewers expect you to know the difference between UPI, NEFT, and IMPS, to have a view on open banking, and to be aware of recent RBI policy directions. Arriving without this context is a commonly cited reason why candidates do not progress past the first business round.

Communication that simplifies, not complicates. BAs at DBS work with engineers, risk teams, and senior leadership in the same week. Interviewers assess whether you can explain a technical constraint to a non-technical stakeholder and a business constraint to a developer.

Ownership and follow-through. Stories where you noticed a gap and fixed it without being asked resonate strongly. DBS's culture rewards proactivity over waiting for direction.

Comfort with ambiguity. Candidates report being given deliberately vague or incomplete case scenarios. The point is not to find the single right answer but to demonstrate how you structure your thinking when key information is missing.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Company and domain research. Read DBS Bank India's recent press releases and product announcements. Understand their key offerings: digibank for retail customers, SME banking, and treasury products. Note any recent RBI regulatory changes that might affect them. Being able to reference a specific DBS product or recent news in your answers signals genuine interest, not a rehearsed pitch.

Technical preparation. Refresh your SQL skills, focusing on joins, aggregations, and window functions. If you use BI tools like Power BI or Tableau, prepare one or two examples of dashboards or analyses you built and the business question they answered. Review Agile and Scrum basics if you have not worked in those environments recently.

Build a story bank. Write out at least six STAR stories covering: a conflict you resolved, a process you improved, a project that faced a setback, a time you worked with a technology team, a decision you made using data, and a time you influenced an outcome without having formal authority over the decision.

Practise out loud. Answering questions in your head is not the same as saying them clearly under pressure. Ask a friend or colleague to play interviewer. For case questions, practise structuring your answer before you speak. Cut any answer that runs longer than two minutes.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Not researching DBS specifically. Answers that could apply to any bank signal low engagement. Know DBS's products, their digital strategy, and at least one recent headline. Generic answers are among the fastest ways to get screened out at the business round.

STAR stories with no results. 'The project was successful' tells an interviewer nothing useful. Even approximate, honest results (such as the team saving roughly a day of manual work each week) are far stronger than vague summaries.

Overselling technical skills you cannot back up. If SQL appears on your resume, expect a question or a take-home test. Be honest about your current level from the start rather than creating an expectation you cannot meet.

Treating the case study as a quiz with one right answer. DBS interviewers typically want to see how you think, not whether you land on a specific conclusion. Candidates who rush to an answer without structuring their approach often score lower than those who think out loud and show their reasoning step by step.

Asking no questions at the end. Leaving the 'do you have any questions for us' moment blank signals low interest or low preparation. Prepare two or three genuine questions about the team's current roadmap, what success looks like in the first few months, or how the BA function collaborates with product and engineering.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the DBS Bank BA interview typically have?

Candidates report anywhere from two to four rounds, though this varies by team and seniority level. A typical path includes an initial HR screening call, one or two business or technical interviews, and sometimes a take-home assignment or live case study. DBS has not published an official interview structure publicly, so treat every round as your best opportunity to make a strong impression.

Is there a case study or written assignment in the DBS Bank BA interview?

Several candidates report receiving a written assignment or a live case scenario, particularly for mid-to-senior roles. These typically involve analysing a business problem, drafting requirements, or proposing a process improvement. Practise structuring your response clearly: state your understanding of the problem, your assumptions, your approach, and your recommendation with trade-offs acknowledged.

What technical skills does DBS Bank look for in a Business Analyst?

SQL proficiency comes up frequently in job descriptions and candidate feedback for DBS BA roles. Familiarity with BI tools such as Power BI or Tableau, and basic knowledge of APIs or data pipelines, is an advantage given the bank's digital focus. You do not need to write production code, but being able to read a data model or write a basic query is expected at most experience levels.

Do I need prior banking experience to get a BA role at DBS Bank?

Prior banking experience is preferred but not always required, especially at junior levels. What matters more is showing you have done your homework: understand key products like digibank and SME lending, know the regulatory context around KYC and AML, and connect your past work to a banking use case. Candidates who demonstrate genuine curiosity about the industry often perform well even without direct sector experience.

How long does the DBS Bank hiring process take?

Candidates report that the process typically runs two to six weeks from first contact to offer, though internal approvals and team availability can extend this. If you have not heard back after a round, a polite follow-up email to your recruiter after about a week is acceptable and sometimes helpful.

What salary can I expect as a Business Analyst at DBS Bank India?

DBS Bank does not publish salary bands publicly. Glassdoor and levels.fyi list community-reported figures for BA roles at private sector banks in India, though sample sizes vary and figures shift over time. When negotiating, research publicly reported ranges for your experience level and city, and be ready to discuss your current and expected CTC directly with the HR team.
