01 Overview

Overview

Asana is a work management platform used by teams globally to track projects, tasks, and goals. The Technical Program Manager (TPM) role at Asana sits at the intersection of engineering, product, and operations, requiring you to drive complex, cross-functional initiatives from planning through delivery.

As of July 2026, knok jobradar tracked 313 TPM openings across India, with Bangalore leading at 41 roles. Asana itself had 164 open roles listed, reflecting active hiring across levels. Candidates report that the interview process typically spans multiple rounds covering program management fundamentals, cross-functional collaboration, and alignment with Asana's mission.

Asana's stated mission is 'enabling the world to do great work together,' and interviewers typically look for candidates who embody that through structured communication and data-driven decision-making. Expect a mix of behavioral, situational, and technical depth questions shaped around how you have driven clarity on complex programs.

Most Asked Questions

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk me through a complex, multi-team program you managed from kickoff to launch. What did you do when timelines slipped?

*Situation:* I was leading a platform migration involving four engineering teams and two external vendor dependencies.
*Task:* Deliver the migration within the committed quarter to unlock a downstream product launch.
*Action:* I set up a weekly cross-team sync with a shared RAID log (Risks, Assumptions, Issues, Dependencies). When one vendor delivered an integration several weeks late, I ran a trade-off session with engineering leads to identify which features could be descoped for the initial release versus deferred to a fast-follow. I rebaselined the plan in writing and shared it with senior leadership promptly rather than waiting for the next steering committee.
*Result:* We shipped the core migration on the revised date with the agreed descope, and the downstream product launched as planned the following month. The rebaselining process became a template for future programs on the team.

---

Q: How do you build alignment between engineering leads and product managers who have competing priorities?

*Situation:* On a data infrastructure program, the engineering lead wanted to invest heavily in reliability work while the PM was pushing for new feature velocity.
*Task:* Build a shared plan both sides could commit to without losing trust.
*Action:* I facilitated a prioritization session where I asked both sides to state their top concerns in terms of user or business impact, not just engineering or roadmap preference. I mapped those concerns against the program's OKRs to find the overlap. We agreed on a split where reliability work was time-boxed to the first sprint of each month, keeping feature work predictable for the PM.
*Result:* The team held the reliability-feature balance for two quarters. Engineering reported fewer incidents, and the PM hit their feature commitments. Both leads cited the structure as something they wanted to continue even after the program closed.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you had to push back on scope creep from a senior leader. How did you handle it?

*Situation:* Mid-program, a VP asked our team to absorb an adjacent workstream that had just lost its PM. The request came informally in a meeting.
*Task:* Protect the program timeline while being respectful of the VP's authority and the urgency of the adjacent work.
*Action:* I followed up in writing the same day with a trade-off document. I outlined three options: absorb the work fully with a significant timeline extension, absorb a scoped-down version with a few additional engineers for a defined period, or find a dedicated owner for the adjacent workstream. I presented the options without advocating for one, letting the data speak.
*Result:* The VP chose the scoped-down option and approved the additional headcount. Our core program stayed on track, and the VP later told me the options framework helped her explain the decision to her own stakeholders.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

The STAR method (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the baseline for all behavioral questions at Asana. Keep the Situation and Task brief, spend the most time on Action, and always close with a concrete Result.

For cross-functional alignment questions, use a structure candidates call 'Context, Conflict, Resolution.' First establish what each party wanted and why. Then name the specific friction point. Then describe the exact steps you took, not just the outcome.

For risk and escalation questions, frame your answer around a clear decision rule: what threshold triggers escalation, who the right escalation path is, and what information you bring when you escalate. Asana interviewers respond well to candidates who can describe this as a repeatable process rather than a one-time judgment call.

For data-driven decision questions, name the metric first, explain why that metric mattered to the business, and then describe how you used it to shift a specific decision. Avoid vague phrases like 'I looked at the data.' Be specific about what the data showed and who changed their mind because of it.

For 'why Asana' questions, connect your TPM background to a specific Asana product area or challenge you find genuinely interesting. The more specifically you can tie your past experience to Asana's product context, the stronger your answer will land.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Asana interviewers typically look for candidates who demonstrate three qualities above all else.

Clarity in communication. Asana's product is built on making work legible. Interviewers notice candidates who structure their answers with a clear beginning, middle, and end. Rambling or context-heavy answers without a clear point are a common flag.

Genuine cross-functional influence. TPMs at Asana are not project coordinators. Interviewers want evidence that you have changed an engineering or product decision, not just tracked it. Think about moments where your input shifted an outcome, and prepare to name the specific people you influenced and how.

Comfort with ambiguity and iteration. Asana ships iteratively, and programs frequently change scope or direction. Candidates who describe rigid, waterfall-style programs without acknowledging pivots can come across as inflexible. Frame your examples to show how you adapted without losing team trust.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week one: Build your story bank. List six to eight programs from your career. For each one, note the scale, the stakeholders involved, the biggest blocker you hit, and the outcome. These become the raw material for all your STAR answers.

Week two: Asana product deep dive. Use Asana as a tool if you have not already. Read their engineering blog and product release notes from 2024 to 2026. Candidates report that Asana interviewers appreciate when you can connect your TPM experience to specific Asana product decisions or workflows.

Week three: Practice out loud. Run mock answers to the questions above with a peer or record yourself. Focus on keeping your Situation and Task tight, and making your Result specific and concrete.

Week four: Logistics and final prep. Research your panel if you know who is interviewing you. Prepare two to three questions for each interviewer that are specific to their team's work. Candidates report that Asana values thoughtful, curious questions over generic ones.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Being too vague about your own contribution. In cross-functional programs, it is tempting to say 'we' throughout. Interviewers want to know what *you* specifically did. Practice naming your own actions clearly.

Skipping the result. Candidates often spend so long on Situation and Action that they rush or skip the Result. A STAR answer without a clear Result reads as incomplete.

Over-engineering the process story. Describing every meeting and every document you created signals process-heaviness, not program leadership. Pick the two or three most important decisions you made and focus there.

Not knowing Asana's product. Candidates who have not used Asana or read about its product direction can struggle when interviewers ask how they would apply their TPM skills to Asana's specific context. This is an easy gap to close with preparation before the interview.

Underselling stakeholder complexity. Asana TPM roles involve deeply cross-functional work. If your examples involve only a handful of stakeholders, they may not reflect the scale the role requires. Choose examples with broad stakeholder maps.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does Asana's TPM interview process typically have?

Candidates report a process that typically includes a recruiter screen, a hiring manager conversation, and a panel of interviews covering behavioral, cross-functional, and sometimes technical depth areas. The exact number of rounds varies by level and team. It is common to see three to five total conversations before an offer decision, though this can shift based on the specific role and hiring team.

Does Asana ask coding or system design questions for TPM roles?

Candidates report that Asana's TPM interviews are not heavy on coding. However, technical depth questions are common, covering topics like how you work with engineering on architecture trade-offs, how you read technical risks, or how you have managed programs with significant infrastructure complexity. Being able to speak credibly about technical concepts without necessarily coding them is typically sufficient for most TPM levels.

What salary can I expect for a TPM role at Asana in India?

Asana does not publicly disclose band data for India-based TPM roles. Publicly reported figures on Glassdoor and levels.fyi for senior TPM roles at comparable US-listed product companies in Bangalore suggest a wide range depending on level and years of experience. Check current listings on those platforms for the most recent data points, as compensation shifts frequently and sample sizes for India-specific Asana data are limited.

How should I research Asana before the interview?

Start with Asana's product: use the tool, read their engineering blog, and follow their product releases from 2024 to 2026. Then review their investor materials and publicly stated company goals to understand their current priorities. Candidates who connect their TPM experience to Asana's specific product challenges tend to stand out more than those who offer generic answers about admiring the company.

What does a TPM at Asana actually own day to day?

Based on publicly available job descriptions, Asana TPMs typically own end-to-end program delivery for complex, cross-functional initiatives. This includes defining program structure, managing dependencies and risks, aligning engineering and product stakeholders, and driving accountability through clear communication. The role is closer to a senior program owner than a coordinator, and candidates are expected to influence decisions, not just track them.

How do I handle the 'why Asana' question without sounding generic?

Be specific. Generic answers about culture or market position are easy to spot in interviews. Instead, connect your TPM background to a specific Asana product area or challenge you find genuinely interesting. For example, if you have managed platform programs, you could speak to how Asana's infrastructure work creates cross-functional complexity that matches your experience. Authenticity and specificity matter more than polish here.
