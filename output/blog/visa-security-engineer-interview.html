01 Overview

Overview

Visa is one of the world's largest digital payments networks, and its security engineering team sits at the heart of protecting hundreds of millions of card transactions. As of July 2026, knok's job radar shows 194 open roles at Visa across India and 628 Security Engineer positions in the broader market. Bangalore leads with 69 openings, followed by Delhi and Pune with 12 each, Hyderabad with 10, Mumbai with 7, and Chennai with 6.

Visa's security interviews pair deep technical knowledge with real-world payments context. Candidates typically face a recruiter screen, two or three technical rounds covering PCI-DSS, cryptography, threat modelling, and SIEM operations, and a behavioural round that tests how you communicate risk across teams. Round count and order vary by team and level, so candidates report treating every stage as a chance to show both hands-on skill and business understanding.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions appear repeatedly in candidate reports and align with Visa's focus on payment security, compliance, and scale.

  1. How would you secure a high-volume payment processing pipeline against injection and data-tampering attacks?
  2. Walk us through how PCI-DSS requirements shape the security controls you design or review.
  3. A card transaction is flagged as anomalous in real time. How do you triage it, and who do you loop in?
  4. Visa handles transactions at massive scale. How do you approach threat modelling for distributed, high-throughput systems?
  5. Describe your hands-on experience with TLS, certificate management, or PKI in a production environment.
  6. How would you run a third-party vendor risk assessment for a new payment-gateway integration?
  7. Tell me about a time you found and fixed a critical vulnerability before it reached production.
  8. How do you stay current with CVEs and decide which patches need immediate action in a payments environment?
  9. Describe how you would write or improve a security incident response runbook for a payments system.
  10. How would you explain a cryptographic vulnerability to a product manager who has no security background?
  11. What SIEM tools have you worked with, and how have you reduced false positives while keeping alert coverage high?
  12. Visa operates in many countries. How does data-residency regulation factor into your security architecture decisions?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a time you found and fixed a critical vulnerability before it reached production.

*Situation:* During a code review at my previous company, I noticed an API endpoint for processing refund requests was not binding the merchant ID to the authenticated session.

*Task:* I had to confirm the severity, document the blast radius, and get it patched before the scheduled production release.

*Action:* I built a proof-of-concept in staging showing a logged-in attacker could issue refunds on behalf of other merchants. I escalated to the tech lead with a one-page risk brief, proposed server-side session binding as the fix, and pair-programmed the patch with the backend developer to cover all edge cases. I also requested a targeted review of similar endpoints.

*Result:* The patch shipped before the release. A follow-on review found two related issues in adjacent endpoints, both fixed in the same sprint. The finding was added to the API security checklist for all future reviews.

---

Q: Describe how you would write or improve a security incident response runbook for a payments system.

*Situation:* When I joined a fintech startup, the existing runbook was a generic template with no payments-specific steps. During a card-data exposure drill, the team spent valuable time figuring out basic escalation paths.

*Task:* I was asked to rewrite the runbook so any on-call engineer could follow it under pressure without guessing.

*Action:* I interviewed engineers, compliance, and legal to map every stakeholder notification requirement, including PCI-DSS breach timelines. I wrote step-by-step playbooks for the top five incident types, added a severity matrix with clear ownership at each tier, and scheduled quarterly tabletop exercises to keep the team sharp.

*Result:* In the next drill, mean time to containment dropped noticeably. Compliance signed off on the runbook as meeting PCI-DSS incident-response requirements, reducing audit risk for the organisation.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you had to explain a security risk to a non-technical stakeholder and get their buy-in for a fix.

*Situation:* A penetration test found that session tokens on our merchant portal were not expiring after logout, creating a token-reuse risk.

*Task:* The product manager was reluctant to delay the release and needed to understand the risk without a technical deep-dive.

*Action:* I skipped the jargon and framed it as: 'If a merchant logs out on a shared computer and someone else opens that browser tab, they can access the account as if they were still logged in.' I then showed a quick fix estimate alongside the reputational and regulatory cost of a real breach.

*Result:* The PM approved a short hold on the release. The fix was deployed the next morning, and the PM later asked me to join all major feature security reviews going forward.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use STAR for every behavioural question. Visa interviewers are trained to probe for specifics. A vague answer like 'we improved security posture' will trigger follow-up questions until you give concrete details. Structure every story as: what was happening (Situation), what you personally owned (Task), exactly what you did step by step (Action), and a measurable or observable outcome (Result).

For threat modelling questions, candidates report success using a structured walkthrough: identify assets and trust boundaries first, list likely threat actors and their goals, map attack paths using a framework like STRIDE or PASTA, then prioritise mitigations by likelihood and impact. Visa interviewers value the reasoning process, not just a final list of controls.

For PCI-DSS questions, anchor your answer to a specific requirement number or control family rather than speaking in generalities. Tying a network segmentation decision to Requirement 1 (network controls) or a key management practice to Requirement 3 (protect stored account data) signals real hands-on familiarity with the standard.

For 'how would you' hypotheticals, open with your first-principles reasoning, state any assumptions you are making, and invite the interviewer to adjust the scenario. This signals structured thinking and a collaborative approach, two qualities Visa consistently highlights in job descriptions.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Payments domain fluency. Generic security knowledge is table stakes. Visa wants to see that you understand what makes payments infrastructure different: low-latency transaction authorisation, chargeback fraud patterns, tokenisation, and the regulatory web of PCI-DSS, RBI guidelines for Indian operations, and cross-border data rules.

Scale thinking. Visa's systems handle peak loads most engineers never encounter. Interviewers look for candidates who instinctively consider throughput, failure modes at scale, and how security controls perform under load, rather than treating security as a feature bolted on at the end.

Clear risk communication. Security engineers at Visa work closely with product, legal, and business teams. Interviewers consistently probe whether you can translate a technical finding into a business risk and recommend a proportionate response.

Ownership and follow-through. Candidates report that interviewers dig into what happened after you identified an issue. Did you raise a ticket and move on, or did you see the fix through to production and verify the control worked? Visa values engineers who treat security as an outcome, not just a process.

Collaborative mindset. Security at a payments company is never a solo effort. Expect questions about how you partnered with developers, infrastructure teams, or vendors to implement controls without blocking delivery velocity.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Build your payments security foundation.
Review PCI-DSS v4.0 requirements, focusing on Requirement 3 (stored data protection), Requirement 6 (secure development), and Requirement 10 (logging and monitoring). The PCI Security Standards Council publishes free summaries online. Brush up on TLS 1.3, certificate lifecycle management, and common cryptographic pitfalls like weak cipher suites or improper IV reuse.

Week 2: Practice threat modelling and technical depth.
Work through two or three threat model exercises on payment flows: a card-present transaction, an e-commerce checkout, and a refund API. Practice articulating STRIDE categories for each flow out loud. Refresh your knowledge of any SIEM platforms you have used, focusing on alert tuning, correlation rules, and log source integration.

Week 3: Prepare your stories and run mock interviews.
Map at least six personal experiences to the questions listed above using STAR format. Cover at least one vulnerability discovery story, one incident response situation, and one cross-team collaboration example. Ask a peer to run a mock technical round and push back hard on any vague answers.

Ongoing: Read recent security advisories in financial services and card networks. Being able to reference a real, recent incident with your own take on the response shows you stay current with the threat landscape. 07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Staying too generic on PCI-DSS. Saying 'I have experience with PCI compliance' without citing specific requirements or controls tells the interviewer very little. Name the requirement, describe the control you implemented, and explain the business reason behind it.

Treating threat modelling as a memorised checklist. Visa interviewers want to see your reasoning, not a rehearsed list. Walk through your logic: why you are prioritising a particular attack path, what assumptions you are making, and where you would revisit if the scenario changed.

Underplaying soft skills. Security engineers at Visa spend significant time working with non-security teams. Candidates who focus only on technical work and skip the 'how you influenced others' part of a story often miss the mark at the behavioural round.

Not asking clarifying questions on hypotheticals. Jumping straight to an answer without stating assumptions or asking one scoping question looks like tunnel vision. A brief 'Can I confirm the scope?' signals structured thinking.

Ignoring the scale dimension. Describing a security control without considering how it performs at Visa's transaction volume is a missed opportunity. Always address whether your proposed solution holds up under high load, or acknowledge where you would need to validate that.

Leaving STAR results vague. 'We improved security' is weak. 'Mean time to detection improved and the finding was embedded in our security checklist' is far stronger, even without quoting a precise number.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Visa Security Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report a process that typically runs three to five rounds. This usually starts with a recruiter screen, followed by two or three technical rounds covering network security, cryptography, and threat modelling, and ends with a behavioural round. Some teams add a system design round for senior roles. Confirm the exact structure with your recruiter once you are in the process.

Is PCI-DSS knowledge mandatory, or can I learn it on the job?

Visa's business is built on card payments, so PCI-DSS literacy is expected from day one, not a nice-to-have. You do not need to have memorised every requirement, but you should be comfortable discussing the major control families: network segmentation, stored data protection, access control, and logging. Candidates who treat PCI-DSS as a new topic at the interview stage typically struggle in technical rounds.

What programming or scripting skills does Visa look for in security engineers?

Job descriptions commonly list Python for automation and scripting, with some roles referencing Java or Go for platform work. Candidates report being asked to review small code snippets for security flaws during technical rounds rather than writing full programs from scratch. Strong comfort with command-line tools, log parsing, and scripted automation is more consistently valued than deep software engineering depth.

Does Visa hire for security roles outside Bangalore?

Yes. Job data as of July 2026 shows Visa security openings in Delhi (12 roles), Pune (12), Hyderabad (10), Mumbai (7), and Chennai (6), alongside the largest cluster in Bangalore (69). Some roles specify office attendance requirements, so check the individual job listing for location flexibility before applying.

How long does the Visa hiring process take from application to offer?

Candidates publicly report timelines ranging from a few weeks to around six weeks for most roles, with senior or specialist positions sometimes running longer. Response time between rounds can vary. Following up with your recruiter after several business days if you have not heard back is generally considered acceptable and signals continued interest.

What salary can I expect as a Security Engineer at Visa in India?

Visa does not publish salary bands publicly. Glassdoor and levels.fyi show a wide range depending on level, city, and specialisation in areas like cloud security or PCI compliance, and figures change frequently, so check those platforms directly for the most current data. Going in with a number anchored in your current CTC and benchmarks from those sources gives you the strongest negotiating position.
