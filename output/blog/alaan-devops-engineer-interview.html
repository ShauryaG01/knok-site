01 Overview

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly in Alaan DevOps interviews, based on patterns from fintech and SaaS infrastructure roles that candidates report.

  1. Walk us through how you would design a CI/CD pipeline for a payment or spend-management application from scratch.
  2. How have you handled secrets management in Kubernetes or a cloud environment? What tools did you use and why?
  3. Describe a production outage you dealt with. What was your runbook and how did you manage the rollback?
  4. How do you approach auto-scaling for services with unpredictable traffic spikes, for example at month-end when expense submissions surge?
  5. Walk us through a Terraform or infrastructure-as-code module you have written. What decisions did you make and what would you change now?
  6. How do you ensure zero-downtime deployments for a service that handles live financial transactions?
  7. Explain how you would set up observability, covering logs, metrics, and distributed traces, for a microservices-based platform.
  8. How do you manage multi-environment setups (dev, staging, production) and prevent configuration drift between them?
  9. What is your experience with compliance requirements like PCI-DSS or SOC 2 from an infrastructure perspective?
  10. How would you reduce cloud spend without affecting reliability? Walk us through something you have actually done.
  11. Describe your Kubernetes cluster management experience. How have you handled node failures or pod evictions in production?
  12. How do you collaborate with developers to shift security left in the deployment process?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through how you designed a CI/CD pipeline for a production service.

*Situation:* At my previous company, we had a Node.js microservice for processing expense claims. Deployments were done manually by SSHing into servers, and a bad push once took the service offline for a significant part of a working day.

*Task:* I was asked to design and implement a fully automated CI/CD pipeline that would let the team ship frequently and roll back safely.

*Action:* I built a GitHub Actions workflow with three stages: lint and unit tests on every pull request, an automatic staging deployment on merge to main, and a gated production deployment requiring explicit approval from a senior engineer. I containerized the service with Docker, pushed images to ECR using immutable tags tied to the git commit SHA, and deployed to ECS using blue-green deployments. Automated smoke tests ran immediately after each deploy, and a failed smoke test triggered an automatic rollback to the previous image.

*Result:* Rollback went from a stressful manual process to an automatic one that completed in minutes. The team started shipping multiple times per week with confidence, and unplanned production incidents dropped significantly.

---

Q: Describe a production outage you dealt with and how you managed it.

*Situation:* Our Kubernetes cluster started showing elevated error rates on a payments microservice shortly after a routine release. Customers were seeing failed transactions.

*Task:* As the on-call engineer, I needed to identify the root cause quickly and restore service.

*Action:* I correlated the error spike with the deployment timestamp on our alerting dashboard and confirmed the issue started within minutes of the new release going live. I ran 'kubectl rollout undo' to revert to the previous image, which brought error rates back to normal. I then pulled logs from the failed pods and found that a missing environment variable was causing the service to crash on startup. I corrected the configuration in our secrets manager, re-deployed with the fix, and monitored through two full request cycles before closing the incident. I followed up with a post-mortem and added an automated config validation step to our CI pipeline.

*Result:* Service was restored quickly, and the same class of misconfiguration has not reached production since the validation step was added.

---

Q: How do you set up observability for a microservices platform?

*Situation:* When I joined my previous team, we had basic server-level monitoring but no visibility into individual microservice behaviour. Debugging a slow API call meant guessing and manually trawling through unstructured logs.

*Task:* I was asked to implement a full observability stack so the team could identify and fix issues faster.

*Action:* I deployed the OpenTelemetry collector as a DaemonSet on our Kubernetes cluster and configured each service to export traces to Jaeger. I set up Prometheus with Grafana dashboards covering the three RED method signals: request rate, error rate, and duration. Logs were centralized in Loki with structured JSON format so engineers could search by trace ID across services. I wrote runbook-linked alerts so the on-call engineer would know the immediate next steps when an alert fired.

*Result:* Mean time to identify the source of an issue dropped noticeably. Engineers stopped guessing which service was responsible and could trace a slow request from the API gateway to the database call in a matter of minutes.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For system design questions: Start with requirements, covering scale, latency tolerance, and uptime expectations. Then walk through the architecture left to right, from source code to production. Name the specific tools you would choose and explain why you picked them over alternatives. Always close with failure modes and recovery. Because Alaan is a fintech product, bring in security and compliance naturally, covering secrets rotation, audit logging, and network segmentation.

For behavioural questions: Use the STAR structure. Keep Situation and Task to one or two sentences each so the majority of your answer sits in the Action section. Frame actions as 'I did X because Y' so the interviewer hears both what you did and the reasoning behind it. End with a concrete Result, even if expressed qualitatively.

For tool-specific questions: Show real experience, not documentation recall. Mention a specific module you wrote, a problem you debugged, or a trade-off you made. If asked about Terraform, describe a specific state management challenge or a time you handled drift. If you have not used a particular tool, say so clearly and explain how you would approach learning it.

For 'how would you' questions: Think out loud. State your assumptions, propose a solution, name its weaknesses, then offer an alternative or mitigation. This signals engineering maturity far more than a confident but shallow answer.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Alaan interviewers look for more than tool familiarity. Candidates report that the team consistently evaluates three qualities.

Ownership mindset. Fintech infrastructure must be reliable at all hours. Stories where you proactively found a problem before it became an incident, or where you went beyond your assigned scope to make the system safer, carry significant weight. Passive answers like 'I followed the process' are weaker than 'I noticed X and fixed it before it caused Y.'

Security awareness by default. Alaan handles corporate card transactions and financial data. Any answer about deployments, access control, or secrets management that ignores security signals a blind spot. Bring up least-privilege IAM, secrets rotation, audit trails, and network policies naturally in your answers, not only when asked directly.

Clear communication across teams. DevOps at a product company means working alongside developers, product managers, and compliance teams. Show that you can translate a technical decision into business terms and that you have done this in practice. Interviewers pay attention to how clearly you explain things, not just whether your answers are technically correct.

Practical depth over breadth. Mentioning many tools is less impressive than explaining one in depth, including why you chose it, how you configured it for your specific situation, and what you would change in hindsight. Expect follow-up questions that go one level deeper than your initial answer.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Core technical areas (start here)

Focus on the topics Alaan's job descriptions emphasize most: Kubernetes (cluster management, RBAC, networking, persistent volumes), CI/CD pipelines (GitHub Actions, GitLab CI, or Jenkins), and infrastructure-as-code with Terraform. For each topic, identify a real project you can speak to in depth. If you have gaps, spend time on hands-on labs rather than just reading documentation.

Fintech and security specifics

Review what PCI-DSS means for infrastructure teams in practical terms: how it shapes secrets management, network segmentation, logging, and access control. Practise explaining these concepts as if you are onboarding a developer who has never worked in a regulated environment. This preparation helps with both direct compliance questions and security topics that surface during system design rounds.

System design and behavioural prep

Practise designing a deployment pipeline or observability stack out loud, as if you are on a whiteboard call. Record yourself to check your pacing and clarity. For behavioural prep, write out three to five STAR stories covering an incident, a cross-team project, a process improvement, and a time you disagreed with a decision but ultimately aligned with the team. Keep each story under three minutes when spoken.

The week of the interview

Read Alaan's LinkedIn posts, any engineering content they have published, and recent product announcements. Know their product well enough to ask a specific, informed question about their infrastructure scale or a technical challenge they have mentioned publicly. This signals genuine interest and tends to stand out.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating DevOps as a tooling checklist. Listing Kubernetes, Docker, Terraform, and Prometheus in your answers is not enough. Interviewers want to know why you chose a tool, what trade-off you made, and what went wrong. If your answers sound like a resume read aloud, you will not clear the technical rounds.

Ignoring security in every answer. At a company handling corporate cards and financial data, security is not an add-on topic. Candidates who describe CI/CD pipelines without mentioning secrets management, or who design Kubernetes setups without discussing RBAC and network policies, signal a significant blind spot that is difficult to recover from in a round.

Vague STAR stories. Saying 'I improved deployment reliability' without explaining what you changed, why it worked, and what the observable outcome was gives the interviewer nothing to evaluate. Be specific about your actions even if you keep the result qualitative.

Not asking questions. Candidates who ask nothing at the end of a round signal low curiosity. Prepare two or three genuine questions about Alaan's infrastructure scale, on-call culture, or a recent engineering challenge. This also helps you assess whether the role is the right fit for you.

Over-engineering a practical task. Candidates report that reviewers value clean, readable, production-worthy solutions over clever but unreadable ones. Document your trade-offs in a README, comment where the logic is not obvious, and err on the side of simplicity.
