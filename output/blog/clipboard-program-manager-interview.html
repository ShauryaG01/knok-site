01 Overview

Overview

Clipboard Health is a US-based healthcare staffing marketplace that connects per-diem nurses and allied health workers with hospitals and nursing facilities on demand. As of mid-2026, Clipboard has 2 Technical Program Manager openings, which signals active investment in engineering coordination as the platform scales.

A TPM at Clipboard typically operates at the intersection of product, engineering, and operations. Candidates report a multi-stage process that commonly includes a recruiter screen, a technical discussion, and a panel interview with engineering managers and cross-functional leads. Expect questions that test your ability to manage ambiguity, align distributed teams, and ship reliably in a marketplace with real-time demand.

This guide covers the questions Clipboard interviewers commonly ask, how to frame your answers, and what separates candidates who move forward from those who do not.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time two engineering teams had conflicting priorities on a shared dependency.

*Situation:* At my previous company, the payments team and the notifications team both needed changes to a shared user-identity service before their Q3 launches.

*Task:* I owned the cross-team program and had to find a sequencing that did not block either team without adding headcount.

*Action:* I facilitated a joint planning session where both teams mapped their exact dependency points on the shared service. We found that only one endpoint overlapped. I worked with the identity-service lead to time-box a focused sprint on that endpoint, then set up a shared channel for real-time visibility and a weekly sync so blockers surfaced before they became critical-path issues.

*Result:* Both teams shipped within the original quarter. The identity-service team reported fewer interruptions from ad-hoc requests, and we carried the same coordination model into the next planning cycle.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you pushed back on an unrealistic deadline.

*Situation:* A product director asked my team to deliver a new vendor-onboarding flow in three weeks. Engineering estimated six weeks for a reliable build.

*Task:* My job was to broker a realistic commitment without derailing the business goal.

*Action:* I prepared a scope matrix showing which features were launch-critical versus nice-to-have. I brought engineering leads into the conversation directly so the director heard constraints from the source, not filtered through me. I proposed a phased release: a manual-assisted MVP in three weeks for a pilot group, then the fully automated flow in the following sprint.

*Result:* The director agreed to the phased plan. The pilot launched on time, gathered real user feedback, and the final automated version shipped in week five, one week ahead of the revised estimate.

---

Q: Describe a process you identified as broken and improved.

*Situation:* My team was spending the first day of every sprint triaging tickets that had incomplete acceptance criteria, which regularly caused mid-sprint blockers.

*Task:* I needed to fix the upstream process without adding bureaucratic overhead for product managers.

*Action:* I introduced a lightweight 'definition of ready' checklist with three items: user story, acceptance criteria, and a design link or API spec. I ran a short workshop with PMs explaining why incomplete tickets created downstream cost for engineers, then built a simple filter that flagged tickets missing any of the three items before they could enter sprint planning.

*Result:* Within two sprints, mid-sprint blockers from incomplete tickets dropped noticeably. Engineers reported spending more time on delivery and less time on clarification, and the PMs found the checklist easy to maintain.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the baseline for behavioral questions at Clipboard. Keep your Situation and Task brief, two to three sentences combined, and put the weight on Action and Result.

For technical communication questions, use a three-part structure: (1) what the technical problem was in plain terms, (2) what trade-offs you considered, (3) what you recommended and why. Adapt your language to the audience in the room, and show you can shift register between an engineer and a business lead.

For prioritization questions, anchor to business impact and user harm. Clipboard is a marketplace where a system outage means nurses cannot find shifts and facilities go understaffed. Frame your prioritization decisions around that kind of real-world consequence, not just engineering effort or ticket severity labels.

For conflict and alignment questions, show that you default to bringing parties together rather than escalating upward. Interviewers want evidence that you resolve most conflicts at the team level before they reach leadership.

Quantify wherever honest. If you reduced a cycle time or improved a delivery rate, say so. If you do not have a precise number, use a directional description ('roughly halved', 'cut by about a third') rather than inventing a figure.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Clipboard interviewers typically look for three things above all.

Ownership without authority. A TPM at Clipboard does not manage engineers directly. Interviewers want evidence that you have driven outcomes through influence, clarity, and trust rather than positional power. Be specific about moments when you had no formal authority and still got teams aligned.

Comfort with ambiguity. Healthcare staffing is a volatile domain. Shift demand can spike overnight, and regulatory changes can reorder priorities without warning. Candidates who need perfectly defined requirements before they can move tend not to do well here. Show examples where you made reasonable decisions with incomplete information and adapted as new data arrived.

Clear, structured communication. TPMs at Clipboard sit between fast-moving engineering pods and business stakeholders who may have limited technical context. Interviewers listen for whether you can explain a complex dependency or a technical trade-off in terms that a non-engineer can act on. Practice summarising technical situations in two to three sentences before going deeper.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Company and domain research. Read everything publicly available about Clipboard Health's product and marketplace model. Understand how supply (healthcare workers) and demand (facilities) interact on the platform. Think about the technical programs that would exist at this scale: shift-matching logic, worker onboarding, compliance and credentialing workflows, and mobile app reliability.

Story inventory. Write down eight to ten TPM stories from your own experience covering cross-team conflict, scope change, deadline pressure, process improvement, stakeholder communication, and a production or quality incident. Map each story to the STAR format before your interview.

Practice behavioral responses out loud. Aim for ninety seconds to two minutes per answer before you invite follow-up questions. Record yourself once to check for filler words and pacing.

Prepare thoughtful questions to ask. Good questions signal genuine interest and reveal how you think. Ask how engineering planning works across teams, how the TPM role interacts with product managers, and what a successful first ninety days looks like. Avoid questions whose answers are on the company website.

Day before: review your story inventory once more and get a good night's sleep. The interview tests judgment and communication, not recall of memorised scripts.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Being vague about your own contribution. Candidates often describe 'the team' doing things without clarifying what they personally decided, initiated, or changed. Clipboard interviewers are assessing your individual impact specifically. Use 'I' statements for your actions even when the outcome was a team win.

Skipping the Result. Incomplete STAR answers that end after the Action leave interviewers guessing whether the approach actually worked. Always close the loop, even if the result was partial or included a setback you learned from.

Over-indexing on process tools. Mentioning Jira or Confluence is fine as context, but it is not an answer by itself. Interviewers want to understand how you think, not which software you have used.

Treating every conflict as someone else's fault. Candidates who frame the other party as entirely unreasonable rarely land well. Show that you understood the other team's constraints and worked to address them, not just overcome them.

Failing to ask about the role. Candidates who ask no questions, or only ask about compensation, signal low interest. Prepare two to three thoughtful questions about team structure, current challenges, or how success is measured in the first six months.
