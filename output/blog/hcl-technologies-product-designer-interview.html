02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up frequently in HCL Product Designer interviews, based on what candidates report across job forums and review sites.

  1. Walk me through a product you designed end to end. What was your process from research to final delivery?
  2. How do you handle conflicting feedback from multiple stakeholders who each want something different?
  3. HCL often works with enterprise clients. How do you design for complex, data-heavy dashboards or workflows?
  4. Describe a time you had to design for a user group you had never met. How did you build empathy and understanding?
  5. How do you balance business requirements with user needs when they point in opposite directions?
  6. What design tools do you use for wireframing, prototyping, and developer handoff? How do you decide which tool to use?
  7. Tell me about a design decision you were confident about that turned out to be wrong. What did you do next?
  8. How do you collaborate with developers to make sure the final product matches your design intent?
  9. Describe your experience with design systems. Have you built one, contributed to one, or worked within one?
  10. How would you approach redesigning an internal enterprise tool that employees find frustrating to use?
  11. What does good UX mean to you specifically in a B2B or enterprise software context?
  12. When you have several design problems to solve and limited time, how do you decide what to work on first?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk me through a product you designed end to end.

*Situation:* My team was asked to build a new onboarding flow for a logistics company's internal tracking tool. The existing flow had a well-documented drop-off problem, with new users frequently abandoning setup before completing it.

*Task:* I was the sole designer responsible for user research, wireframes, and the final prototype. I had to deliver within a tight project timeline before a client review.

*Action:* I started by running several user interviews with warehouse supervisors who were the primary users. I found they were confused by the number of steps and by technical jargon in the UI. I mapped a simplified flow, cut unnecessary steps, replaced jargon with plain language, and built a mid-fidelity prototype in Figma. I ran multiple rounds of usability testing with the same users and iterated on the design before final handoff to developers.

*Result:* The client approved the redesign. After launch, the team reported that new users completed onboarding more consistently, though I want to be honest that this was a small pilot group and not a large-scale measurement.

---

Q: Tell me about a design decision you made that turned out to be wrong.

*Situation:* I was designing a filter panel for a reporting dashboard. I was confident a sidebar filter layout was the right choice based on common patterns I had studied in industry surveys and competitive analysis.

*Task:* I needed to deliver a working prototype for a client demo with a fixed deadline.

*Action:* I moved the sidebar design into prototype without running any usability tests first. During an internal review, a developer flagged that the sidebar would collapse on mobile and break the layout entirely. I had not tested on smaller screens because I assumed most users would be on desktop.

*Result:* I had to redesign the filter as a collapsible top bar in a short turnaround, which was stressful but workable. Since then I always test on multiple screen sizes before any design goes to review. The lesson: assumptions about user context need to be verified, not assumed.

---

Q: How do you handle conflicting feedback from multiple stakeholders?

*Situation:* During a redesign project for a healthcare client, the product manager wanted to add more data fields to a form, while the compliance officer wanted fewer fields to reduce risk. Both had valid reasons.

*Task:* I needed to resolve the conflict and move the design forward without losing either stakeholder's trust.

*Action:* I scheduled a joint call with both stakeholders and presented the user research I had already done, which showed that longer forms caused users to abandon midway. I used that data to reframe the conversation: instead of 'more fields vs. fewer fields,' I asked both of them to agree on which fields were truly essential for the user to complete the task. This shifted the discussion from preference to evidence.

*Result:* We landed on a shorter form with an optional 'add more details' section that satisfied compliance needs without overwhelming users. Both stakeholders signed off, and the design moved to development without further changes.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For portfolio and process questions: Use a four-part structure: Problem (what was the challenge and why it mattered), Process (what steps you took and why), Output (what you delivered), and Impact (what changed as a result). Keep it concrete. Interviewers at enterprise companies like HCL typically care more about your reasoning than about the visual finish of your work.

For stakeholder or conflict questions: Use a 'Listen, Align, Decide' approach. Show that you heard both sides, found common ground using data or user evidence rather than personal opinion, and made a clear recommendation. Avoid framing it as 'I convinced them I was right.' Frame it as 'we agreed on what the user needed.'

For redesign or whiteboard questions: Start with discovery, not solutions. Say out loud: 'Before I jump to wireframes, let me ask a few questions about users and business goals.' Interviewers want to see that you do not skip straight to solutions before understanding the problem. A short discovery phase followed by a structured approach signals seniority.

For tool or process questions: Be specific and honest. Name the tools you actually use (Figma, Maze, Miro, etc.), explain when you use each, and mention the handoff process you follow with developers. If you have never used a tool they mention, say so clearly and note how you would pick it up.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Enterprise mindset: HCL builds for large clients, so interviewers look for designers who understand real constraints: legacy systems, multi-stakeholder approvals, accessibility requirements, and the reality that a strong design sometimes has to be simplified for technical or business reasons. Show that you have worked within these kinds of limits.

Clear communication: You will work with developers, business analysts, and client PMs who are not designers. Interviewers want evidence that you can explain your decisions in plain language without relying on design jargon. Practice saying 'I chose this layout because users told me X' rather than 'I chose it because it follows certain design principles.'

Structured process: Candidates report that HCL interviewers pay close attention to whether you follow a user-centred process: research, define, design, test, iterate. Even if a past project was rushed, be able to name what step you skipped and what you would have done differently.

Collaboration and ownership: Enterprise design teams value designers who take full ownership of their work but also collaborate well across functions. Come ready to talk about how you worked with a specific developer or PM, not just about projects you completed independently.

Portfolio depth over polish: A case study that shows your thinking clearly, including dead ends and pivots, is typically valued more than a beautifully formatted deck with no story behind it.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know your own work. Pick your strongest portfolio projects and prepare clear answers for each: What was the problem? Who were the users? What research did you do? What did you try that did not work? What was the final design and what changed because of it? If you cannot answer all of those, choose different projects.

Week 2: Research HCL's product context. Look at HCL's publicly available product and engineering case studies. Understand the kinds of enterprise clients they serve. Read the specific job description carefully and note whether it mentions B2B, SaaS, healthcare, fintech, or another domain. Tailor your portfolio to lead with the most relevant case study.

Week 3: Practice out loud. Work through the questions listed in the most-asked section by speaking your answers rather than just thinking them. Record yourself if you can. Watch for filler words, vague statements like 'I did some research,' and answers that run too long. For the portfolio walkthrough, prepare a tight short version and a detailed longer version so you can adapt to the time available.

Week 4: Prepare your own questions. Interviewers typically expect you to ask questions at the end. Prepare at least three: one about the team structure, one about the kind of products you would work on, and one about how design decisions are made when engineering and design disagree. These signal genuine interest and help you judge whether the role is right for you.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

1. Showing only final designs without process. If your portfolio only shows polished screens, interviewers have no way to assess how you think. Include sketches, early wireframes, and notes on what you changed and why.

2. Claiming sole credit for team work. Enterprise companies know that large projects involve multiple designers, PMs, and developers. If you say 'I designed this entire product,' interviewers often probe harder, and vague answers damage your credibility. Be specific: 'I owned the onboarding flow, while another designer handled the dashboard.'

3. Not knowing your own data. If you say a redesign improved the experience, be ready for 'by how much?' If you do not have data, say so clearly and explain what you would have measured. Do not invent metrics.

4. Skipping the problem definition. Jumping straight to 'here is what I designed' without explaining the problem and the users signals that you are output-focused rather than problem-focused. Always set context first.

5. Misreading the seniority level. If you are applying for a mid-level role (3-5 years of experience), avoid answering every question as if you were a fresh graduate who followed instructions. Show independent judgment. On the other hand, do not overclaim leadership if you were an individual contributor.

6. Ignoring the enterprise context. HCL is not a consumer app startup. Answers that only reference consumer product experience, without connecting to the complexity of enterprise workflows, can feel off-target. Always ask yourself whether your answer makes sense for a company that builds products for banks, hospitals, and large manufacturers.
