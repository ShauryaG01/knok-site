02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

  1. Walk us through a portfolio project where you had to balance user needs with business constraints. What trade-offs did you make?
  2. How do you approach a design problem when you have very little time for research?
  3. Describe your process for user research. What methods do you choose and why?
  4. Give us an example of a design decision you pushed back on. How did you handle the disagreement with the stakeholder?
  5. How do you decide when a design is 'done'? What does 'good enough' look like for you?
  6. Tell us about a time a product you designed did not perform as expected after launch. What did you do next?
  7. How do you collaborate with engineers when your design hits a technical constraint?
  8. Walk us through how you would design an onboarding flow for a first-time user of a new mobile app. (Candidates report this appears frequently as a live brief prompt.)
  9. How do you prioritize features when you have limited design bandwidth and multiple stakeholders pushing different directions?
  10. Describe a time you used data or analytics to challenge an assumption in your design process.
  11. How do you maintain design consistency across a product that has grown quickly?
  12. What does great design look like to you, and how do you explain your standard to a non-designer?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through a portfolio project where you balanced user needs with business constraints.

*Situation:* I was the sole designer on a mobile checkout flow for an e-commerce platform. User research showed customers wanted a detailed product comparison view before completing their purchase.

*Task:* The business needed to reduce the number of steps to payment to improve conversion, which put the two goals in direct tension.

*Action:* I ran a quick card-sorting session with a small group of users to find out which comparison points mattered most. I condensed the view to the top three attributes they consistently cited and placed them in a collapsible panel, so the detail was available but not required to proceed.

*Result:* The redesign kept the research-backed content while meeting the business goal of fewer taps to payment. Candidates in similar roles report that a 'collapse and reveal' pattern is a recurring solution interviewers respond to well when the rationale is clearly explained.

---

Q: Tell us about a time a design you shipped did not perform as expected.

*Situation:* I redesigned a dashboard for a B2B SaaS product. I felt confident in the new layout based on usability testing with recruited participants.

*Task:* After launch, analytics showed users spending more time on sections we had de-prioritised in the new design, which was the opposite of what we expected.

*Action:* I ran a follow-up diary study with a small group of users over two weeks. The key insight was that our test participants were new users, while the actual product base was mostly power users with a very different mental model.

*Result:* I lobbied for a segmented view: one layout for new users and a separate one for power users. The experience permanently changed how I think about matching research participant profiles to the real user base before drawing conclusions.

---

Q: How do you collaborate with engineers when your design hits a technical constraint?

*Situation:* I had designed an animation-heavy onboarding experience for a fintech app.

*Task:* The engineering lead flagged that the animations would add significant load time on lower-end Android devices, which made up a large share of our users.

*Action:* I joined two sprint planning sessions to understand what was actually feasible within the release timeline. Together we agreed on a reduced-motion variant that activated based on device performance, and I documented both variants in Figma with clear handoff notes for each.

*Result:* The product shipped on time with no quality compromise. That collaboration also led to a standing weekly sync between design and engineering that caught similar issues earlier in future sprints.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For portfolio walkthroughs: Structure your answer as Problem, Process, Decision, Outcome. Always name the constraint you were working within (time, technology, data, stakeholder pressure) and explain why you chose your approach, not just what you did. Interviewers want to see your thinking, not just your execution.

For case study or live brief questions: Candidates report that interviewers at product-focused companies respond well to a four-step approach: clarify the goal, understand the user, map the key flows, then propose. Do not jump to screens before you have discussed the user's context and needs. Silence followed by a polished answer is less impressive than a messy, collaborative working session.

For 'how do you handle conflict' questions: Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) and keep the focus on the outcome for the user or the product, not on proving you were right. Interviewers want to see that you can disagree respectfully and commit to a shared decision.

For design metrics questions: Connect your answer to a user behavior, not just a UI metric. 'Time on task dropped after the redesign' is a much stronger answer than 'the screen looked cleaner.'

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Clarity of thinking over visual polish. Interviewers at product companies typically care more about how you arrive at a decision than how refined your final screens look. Be ready to explain your reasoning at every step of the process.

User empathy with business awareness. The strongest candidates show they understand the user deeply and can also speak the language of product goals and business metrics. Neither alone is enough at a product design role.

Collaboration signals. Because designers work closely with product managers and engineers, interviewers listen carefully for how you describe cross-functional work. Using 'I designed everything' throughout your answers raises flags. Saying 'we aligned with engineering before moving to high-fidelity' signals maturity.

Opinionated but open. Candidates report that strong performers express a clear point of view on their work but show they can update that view when new information arrives. Avoid either extreme: do not present every decision as a committee choice, and do not come across as resistant to feedback.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Two weeks out: Review your portfolio and pick three to five projects that demonstrate different skills: user research, interaction design, cross-functional collaboration, and shipping under constraint. For each project, prepare a two-minute verbal walk-through using the Problem, Process, Decision, Outcome structure.

One week out: Practice answering the 12 questions listed above out loud, ideally with someone who can give honest feedback. Record yourself at least once. Most candidates underestimate how long their answers run when nerves kick in.

Three days out: Look closely at Berg Group's products or any publicly available work from their design team. Form your own opinion on what is working and what could be improved. Interviewers typically appreciate candidates who have engaged with the actual product rather than just reading the job description.

Day before: Prepare two questions for the interviewer. Strong options: 'What does the design team's relationship with product management look like here?' and 'What is a recent design decision the team is especially proud of?' Avoid asking about salary in early rounds unless the interviewer raises it first.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping to UI without talking about the user. In case study questions, many candidates say 'I would design a screen that...' before discussing who the user is and what they actually need. Interviewers typically notice this and mark it down. Start with the user's context and goal.

Vague ownership language. Saying 'the team did this' for every decision makes it hard for the interviewer to evaluate your individual contribution. Be specific about what you personally owned, decided, and delivered.

Not knowing your own portfolio numbers. If you mention that a redesign improved something, be ready to say what the improvement was and how it was measured. 'It tested well' is not a satisfying answer in a design interview.

Treating the live brief as a solo exercise. Candidates report that interviewers often want you to think out loud and ask clarifying questions throughout the brief. A polished, silent answer delivered at the end is typically less impressive than a messy, collaborative working session.

Skipping the 'why' in design decisions. Every screen, flow, or pattern choice has a reason. If you cannot explain it, the interviewer will assume there was none.

Asking zero questions at the end. Candidates who skip their questions leave a weaker impression. Curiosity is a core design skill, and asking thoughtful questions signals genuine interest in the role.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Berg Group Product Designer interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically spans two to four rounds. These commonly include a portfolio review, a design exercise or case study, and a final conversation with a senior stakeholder or hiring manager. Round count and structure can vary by team and city, so confirm the details with your recruiter after you apply.

What kind of portfolio does Berg Group look for?

Candidates report that interviewers respond best to portfolios that show the full design process, not just polished final screens. Include your research, the decisions you made along the way, and the measurable or qualitative outcome. Three to five well-explained projects are typically stronger than ten projects with thin context and no rationale.

Is there a live design exercise or take-home assignment?

Candidates report that product designer interviews at companies of this type often include either a live brief or a take-home case study. For a live brief, think out loud and ask clarifying questions rather than delivering a silent polished answer at the end. For a take-home, scope your response to what is realistic within the time provided rather than over-engineering a submission.

What salary can I expect as a Product Designer at Berg Group?

Berg Group does not publicly disclose its pay bands. Based on knok jobradar data as of July 2026, mid-level Product Designers (3-5 years experience) are commonly cited at 14-24 LPA in the broader Indian market, while senior roles (6-9 years) are commonly cited at 26-40 LPA. Use these as a market anchor and negotiate based on your specific experience and the level of the role you are being offered.

How important is domain knowledge for this role?

Interviewers typically value a strong design process over deep domain expertise, especially at mid and senior levels. That said, if you have relevant industry experience, highlight it clearly in your portfolio walk-through. Coming in with an informed, specific opinion about Berg Group's actual product, based on your own use of it, is a strong signal regardless of your domain background.

How long does the process take from application to offer?

Candidates report timelines ranging from two to six weeks from first contact to offer, though this varies by team urgency and the number of rounds involved. Following up politely with your recruiter after each round is normal and expected. If you have a competing offer with a deadline, communicate it early so the team can adjust their timeline if they want to move faster.
