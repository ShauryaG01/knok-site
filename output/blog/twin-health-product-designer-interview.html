02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

  1. Walk us through a product you shipped end-to-end. What was the user problem, how did you approach it, and how did you know you had solved it?
  2. Twin Health's primary users are patients managing a chronic illness every day. How do you design for someone with low digital literacy or high health anxiety?
  3. Describe a time you used user research or product data to challenge a stakeholder's assumption about what users actually needed.
  4. How would you approach designing a screen that shows a patient their metabolic health trends over the past month? Walk us through your first steps.
  5. Tell us about a time when clinical accuracy and a simple, friendly UI were in direct conflict. What did you decide, and why?
  6. How do you collaborate with medical or clinical experts who have strong opinions about how a feature should look or work?
  7. Tell us about a design you shipped that did not perform as expected. What happened, and what would you do differently?
  8. When you have several competing user problems and time for only one fix, how do you decide what to tackle first?
  9. Walk us through your prototyping process. How do you choose fidelity, and when do you know you are done iterating?
  10. Twin Health sells to employers and health plans, not only to end patients. How does that B2B2C context shape your design decisions?
  11. How do you make sure your designs are accessible, especially for older adults or users with visual or motor impairments?
  12. What does a healthy designer-to-engineer handoff look like, and how do you keep it working when timelines are tight?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Twin Health's users are patients managing a chronic illness. How do you design for someone with low digital literacy or high health anxiety?

*Situation:* At my previous company, we launched a glucose-tracking feature for patients in a corporate wellness programme. Our initial design used clinical terminology and dense multi-line trend charts.

*Task:* After release, support contacts spiked and session drop-off on the dashboard was high. I was asked to redesign the experience for a broader range of users, including first-time smartphone users and older adults.

*Action:* I ran moderated usability sessions with a small group of participants who matched our highest drop-off segment, and reviewed support call transcripts to find the exact labels causing confusion. I rewrote all field labels in plain language, replaced the dense charts with a single 'how you are doing today' status indicator, and added contextual tooltips tied to specific readings. I tested three iterations before finalising.

*Result:* In the next cohort, support contacts related to the dashboard dropped noticeably according to the team's internal tracking, and coaches reported spending less time explaining the UI on calls. The pattern became the team's standard template for new health metric screens.

---

Q: Describe a time you used research or data to push back on a stakeholder's assumption.

*Situation:* A senior product manager wanted to add a fixed daily reminder notification for all users, convinced it would improve logging rates.

*Task:* I was responsible for the notification experience and had access to our engagement analytics and a recent user survey.

*Action:* I pulled the survey data and found that a large portion of users had already turned off all app notifications, citing 'too many alerts' as the reason. I mapped this against our analytics and confirmed that the users most likely to disable blanket reminders were already-engaged power users, while lapsed users barely opened the app at all. I put together a short deck showing the risk and proposed a contextual nudge instead, triggered by a missed log window rather than a fixed daily time.

*Result:* The PM agreed to run a test with the contextual approach. Industry surveys commonly cite contextual nudges as outperforming fixed ones on re-engagement. Our internal pilot showed a directional improvement, and the feature shipped with the contextual logic.

---

Q: Tell us about a design decision where clinical accuracy and a simple, friendly UI were in direct tension.

*Situation:* We were building a food logging screen that needed to display caloric and macronutrient data per meal. Our clinical team required gram-level breakdowns because accuracy was critical for patients on a monitored programme.

*Task:* My responsibility was to make that data feel accessible to patients who were not nutrition experts and who already found the app stressful.

*Action:* I proposed a layered disclosure model. The default view showed a coloured progress ring for each macro with a plain label such as 'Good on protein today', and a single tap expanded to the full gram-level detail for users who wanted it. I documented the clinical rationale for each data point and got sign-off from the clinical lead, who confirmed that accessibility of the data, not its front-and-centre placement, was the actual requirement.

*Result:* The layered design passed clinical review, and user testing showed participants felt much less overwhelmed by the screen. The clinical team later adopted the same pattern for other metric screens.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For portfolio walkthroughs, use Problem, Process, Decision, Outcome. Open with the user problem in one sentence. Describe your research or discovery steps briefly. Call out one or two key design decisions and explain the trade-offs you weighed. Close with a concrete outcome, even if qualitative, such as a drop in support contacts or a team adopting your pattern for future screens.

For hypothetical design questions, use a lightweight Double Diamond approach out loud. Name who the users are, say what you would learn first (existing analytics, a quick interview, secondary research on the health condition), frame the problem before opening a design file, and identify constraints (clinical, technical, accessibility) before proposing a solution direction.

For behavioural questions, use STAR. Keep Situation to one or two sentences of context. Task should state your specific responsibility. Spend most of your time on Action, and be concrete about what you personally did rather than what the team did. Close Result with what actually changed. If hard numbers are thin, a qualitative signal such as coach feedback or a simplified clinical review process is a valid and honest outcome.

For prioritisation questions, name a framework and immediately ground it in a real example. RICE (Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort) or a simple impact-versus-effort grid are both widely understood. The goal is to show that you have a repeatable process, not that you memorised a framework name.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Clinical empathy, not just user empathy. Twin Health's users are managing real health conditions with real emotional weight. Interviewers want to see that you understand why a patient might feel anxious about a particular reading, not just that you know how to run a usability test.

Systems thinking across a B2B2C product. Your design decisions affect patients, clinicians, and the employer or health plan paying for the service. Candidates who only speak to the patient layer often get caught out when the B2B discussion starts.

Research-led decisions. Twin Health is a data-driven company. Expect to be asked how you validated a design choice. Vague answers like 'we did user testing' are far less convincing than specifics: who you tested with, what you learned, and what you changed as a result.

Comfort working inside constraints. Health tech design lives within clinical, regulatory, and engineering constraints. Interviewers look for designers who treat constraints as inputs to the design problem, not as blockers to route around.

Cross-functional fluency. You will work with clinicians, data scientists, and engineers. Show that you can hold a meaningful conversation in a clinical setting without needing to be the clinical expert in the room.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know the product. Download the Twin Health app or watch demo videos on their website. Map the main user flows: onboarding, daily logging, coach interaction, and metric review. Note design decisions you would question or improve, and be ready to explain your reasoning in the interview.

Week 1: Prepare your case study list. Select three or four portfolio projects for short walkthroughs. At least one should involve data-heavy or health-adjacent UX. Prepare a short verbal walk-through for each using the Problem, Process, Decision, Outcome structure described in the answer frameworks section.

Week 2: Practise the design exercise. Candidates report that Twin Health typically includes a design exercise, either take-home or live. Practise with health-related prompts such as 'Design a screen that helps a patient understand why their blood sugar spiked.' Narrate your thinking out loud as you work, since interviewers are evaluating your reasoning, not just the final output.

Week 2: Review accessibility and inclusive design. Go through WCAG guidelines and be ready to explain how you have applied them in a real project. Twin Health serves older adults and patients with varying abilities, so this is not a checkbox topic for them.

Before every round: Prepare your questions. Ask about the clinical review process for design decisions, how the design team collaborates with clinical staff, and what the biggest unsolved UX challenge in the product is right now. Strong questions signal genuine curiosity about the domain.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating it like a consumer app interview. Twin Health is a clinical-grade product. Candidates who lead with visual polish and skip the clinical logic often do not pass the portfolio round. Show that you understand why design decisions matter for patient outcomes, not just engagement metrics.

Making vague research claims. Saying 'we did user research' without specifics reads as thin. Be concrete about who you spoke to, what you found, and how it changed the design.

Ignoring the B2B2C layer. Many candidates focus entirely on the patient experience and forget that an employer or health plan is also a key stakeholder. At least one of your case studies should touch on multi-stakeholder or multi-persona design.

Over-investing in the mock-up during a design exercise. Candidates sometimes spend most of their time on a polished visual and too little time on problem framing. Interviewers at health tech companies typically want to see how you define the problem before they evaluate a single screen.

Not asking questions. Twin Health operates in a specialised domain. Asking thoughtful questions about clinical workflows or regulatory constraints signals that you understand the complexity of the work and are genuinely interested in it.

Underselling qualitative outcomes. Not every health UX improvement has a clean metric attached to it. A drop in support contacts, positive coach feedback, or a simplified clinical review process are all valid results. Do not discard a strong story just because you cannot attach a percentage to it.

You do not need a clinical background, but you do need to show you have thought seriously about designing for patients managing chronic conditions. Read about behaviour change design principles and look at how health apps handle sensitive data presentation. Be ready to go deep on one project rather than skimming across many.
