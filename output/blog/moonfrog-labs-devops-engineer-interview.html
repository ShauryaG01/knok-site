03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a production incident where your deployment caused downtime. What was your rollback strategy?

*Situation:* At my previous company, a backend service update introduced a memory leak that caused game servers to crash under load within minutes of deployment.

*Task:* I was the on-call engineer and needed to restore service quickly while preventing data loss for active player sessions.

*Action:* I immediately triggered a rollback to the previous stable image using our Kubernetes rollout undo command, then isolated the faulty pods. I set up a temporary feature flag to disable the affected feature while the team investigated the root cause. I posted a status update to our internal incident channel every few minutes to keep stakeholders informed.

*Result:* Service was fully restored within minutes. We added a memory profiling step to our pre-deployment checklist and introduced a canary release policy so future changes roll out to a small subset of servers first. Candidates report that structured incident narration lands well in Moonfrog interviews.

---

Q: How would you design auto-scaling for game servers handling unpredictable player surges?

*Situation:* Our gaming platform ran weekly tournaments that could multiply concurrent connections several times over in minutes.

*Task:* I was asked to redesign our scaling strategy to eliminate the manual intervention that was common during these events.

*Action:* I configured horizontal pod autoscaler in Kubernetes based on custom metrics, specifically active WebSocket connections per pod rather than CPU alone. I set up predictive scaling rules that pre-warmed server capacity well before scheduled tournaments, and paired this with a queue-based load shedding mechanism for unscheduled spikes.

*Result:* We eliminated manual on-call interventions during tournaments. The pre-warming approach meant players experienced smooth joins at peak times rather than connection errors during cold-start periods.

---

Q: How have you used Infrastructure as Code in a real project?

*Situation:* Our team was managing cloud resources manually through the console, which led to configuration drift and made disaster recovery slow.

*Task:* I led the migration of our full staging and production infrastructure to Terraform.

*Action:* I started by importing existing resources into Terraform state, wrote modular configurations for networking, compute, and database layers, and set up a remote state backend with state locking. I introduced a pull-request review process for all infrastructure changes so no one could push without peer approval.

*Result:* We could recreate our full environment from code in under an hour. The review process also caught several misconfigurations before they reached production.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use STAR for incident and experience questions. Moonfrog interviewers typically ask 'tell me about a time' questions to assess real ownership. Structure your answer as: Situation (brief context), Task (your specific responsibility), Action (what you personally did, not 'we'), Result (measurable or observable outcome). Keep Situation and Task short, and spend most of your time on Action.

Use 'Diagnose, Design, Trade-off' for system design questions. When asked how you would build or redesign something, first state your assumptions and constraints (player scale, latency targets, budget), then walk through your design step by step, then explicitly call out trade-offs (cost vs. resilience, simplicity vs. flexibility). Interviewers at product-driven companies like Moonfrog value engineers who think about real constraints, not just textbook setups.

Use 'Current state, Gap, Fix' for cost and optimisation questions. Start by describing how you would audit the current setup (cloud billing reports, unused resources, oversized instances), then identify the gap (what is wasting money or slowing delivery), then explain your proposed fix and how you would validate it. This shows structured thinking rather than jumping straight to solutions.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Candidates report that Moonfrog interviewers look for a few specific traits beyond technical knowledge.

Ownership under pressure. Gaming companies deal with real-money transactions and real-time player sessions. Interviewers want to see that you have personally owned incidents, not just helped from the sidelines. Use 'I' not 'we' when describing your actions.

Gaming-context awareness. You do not need prior gaming industry experience, but you should understand why gaming infrastructure is different: always-on availability, burst traffic patterns, low-latency requirements, and the cost of a bad player experience. Mentioning these trade-offs shows you have thought about the domain.

Practical tooling depth. Moonfrog candidates report technical questions that go beyond 'have you used Kubernetes?' to 'walk me through a real configuration decision and why you made it.' Prepare to discuss specific flags, trade-offs, and failure modes in the tools you list on your resume.

Cost consciousness. As a growth-stage company, Moonfrog interviewers typically ask about cloud cost optimisation. Be ready to discuss spot instances, reserved capacity, right-sizing, and how you have balanced cost against reliability in real projects.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week one: shore up your core skills. Review your strongest cloud platform (AWS, GCP, or Azure) with a focus on compute scaling, managed Kubernetes, and networking. Revisit Terraform or Ansible by writing a small module from scratch. Candidates report that being able to write IaC live during an interview is a clear differentiator.

Week two: prepare your incident stories. Write out a few real incidents or complex deployments you have handled using the STAR format. Focus on ones where you personally drove the resolution. Practice saying them out loud so they sound natural, not rehearsed.

Week three: study the gaming context. Read about common game backend architectures: matchmaking services, leaderboard systems, real-time event pipelines. Understand why WebSocket connection management and always-on uptime matter differently in gaming than in an e-commerce or fintech backend.

Before your interview. Look at Moonfrog's public engineering blog or any published talks by their team. Glassdoor and similar platforms sometimes carry interview experience reports from candidates. Prepare two or three specific questions to ask your interviewer about their deployment cadence and on-call culture.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Saying 'we' instead of 'I'. In behavioural rounds, candidates often describe team actions rather than their own. Interviewers want to know your specific contribution. Practice reframing your stories around what you personally decided and did.

Listing tools without depth. Writing Kubernetes, Terraform, and Prometheus on your resume invites deep follow-up questions. If you have only used a tool at a surface level, be honest about your depth rather than claiming expertise you cannot demonstrate under questioning.

Ignoring the gaming context. Treating Moonfrog like a generic SaaS company in your answers signals that you have not thought about why you want to work there. Reference always-on requirements, player experience impact, or traffic burst patterns to show domain awareness.

Skipping the 'why' in design answers. Saying 'I would use Kubernetes' without explaining the trade-offs makes your answer sound rote. Candidates report that explaining your reasoning matters as much as the technical choice itself.

Not asking questions at the end. Candidates who ask nothing about on-call load, deployment frequency, or team structure come across as disengaged. Prepare at least two specific questions before you walk in.
