02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly in CommIT Frontend Engineer interviews, based on what candidates report:

  1. Walk us through the most complex UI feature you have built. What were the key decisions you made?
  2. How do you manage state in a large React application? Compare your experience with Redux, Zustand, and React Context.
  3. Explain the difference between server-side rendering and client-side rendering. When would you choose one over the other?
  4. How do you diagnose and fix a slow-loading frontend? Walk us through your performance debugging process step by step.
  5. CommIT works across multiple client projects. How do you adapt when you switch between very different codebases and tech stacks?
  6. Describe how you approach accessibility (a11y) in your UI work. Give a concrete example from a project you owned.
  7. How do you structure a reusable component library so that other engineers can pick it up without confusion?
  8. Tell me about a time you found a critical bug in production. How did you identify it, and what did you do?
  9. How do you collaborate with designers when their mockups are technically difficult to implement exactly as drawn?
  10. What is your approach to writing unit and integration tests for frontend code? What tools do you prefer and why?
  11. How do you keep TypeScript types useful and clean without making them overly complex for the team?
  12. Describe a situation where you disagreed with a technical direction your team was taking. What happened and what did you learn?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for every behavioural question. Here are three worked examples.

---

Q: Tell me about the most complex UI feature you have built.

*Situation:* My team was building a real-time data dashboard that needed to show live updates across hundreds of rows without freezing the browser on mid-range devices.

*Task:* I owned the entire frontend: data layer, rendering logic, and the performance budget.

*Action:* I used react-window for row virtualisation so only visible rows stayed in the DOM, debounced the WebSocket updates to avoid triggering a re-render on every incoming tick, and memoised expensive calculations with useMemo. I added skeleton loaders so the page felt responsive before data arrived.

*Result:* The dashboard went from a noticeable stutter to smooth scrolling even on older machines. The client flagged it as one of the smoother handoffs the team had delivered that quarter.

---

Q: Describe a time you found a critical bug in production.

*Situation:* A payment confirmation screen was silently failing for a subset of users on iOS Safari after a routine release.

*Task:* I picked up the bug report and owned the investigation end to end.

*Action:* I reproduced the issue using BrowserStack, traced it to a Promise chaining behaviour that Safari handled differently from Chrome, and confirmed the fix locally before raising a PR. I also added a Sentry alert for that specific error path so future regressions would surface before users noticed.

*Result:* The fix went out within a few hours of the first report. The Sentry alert has caught two similar cross-browser issues since, both silently, before any user complaint came in.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you disagreed with a technical direction.

*Situation:* My team was about to adopt a custom in-house state management solution on a project that already had Redux working well.

*Task:* I felt the change added risk without a clear benefit and needed to raise that constructively without derailing the discussion.

*Action:* I put together a short comparison covering migration cost, learning curve, and long-term maintenance, and shared it during the design review. I asked clarifying questions rather than just objecting, which kept the conversation collaborative.

*Result:* The team agreed to stay with Redux for the current project and revisit the custom solution for the next greenfield build, avoiding multiple sprints of migration work mid-delivery.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For technical 'how do you' questions: Start with your default approach, then explain the trade-offs that would make you choose something different. Interviewers want to see that you think in context, not that you have memorised a single correct answer.

For system design questions: Think out loud. State your assumptions first (for example: 'I am assuming this is a consumer-facing app with moderate traffic'), then work through component breakdown, data flow, and performance considerations. Candidates report that CommIT interviewers value the reasoning process as much as the final output.

For behavioural questions: Use STAR consistently. Keep Situation and Task to two or three sentences and spend most of your time on Action and Result. Quantify results where you honestly can, but never invent numbers.

For live coding rounds: Narrate your thinking as you write. If you hit a wall, say what you are trying and why, rather than going quiet. Interviewers typically value communication and collaboration alongside correctness.

For culture and 'why CommIT' questions: Research their client sectors and any recent news before your interview. Connect your answer to something specific rather than giving a generic response about culture or growth.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates report from CommIT Frontend Engineer interviews, a few qualities come up consistently.

Solid React fundamentals, not just surface familiarity. Interviewers probe how well you understand the rendering lifecycle, hooks, and performance patterns, not just whether you have used React before.

Practical problem-solving under real constraints. CommIT works across client projects with real deadlines and existing codebases. They look for engineers who can make pragmatic decisions and communicate trade-offs clearly.

Cross-functional collaboration. Frontend work at CommIT touches designers, backend teams, and clients directly. Showing that you communicate well across these boundaries matters alongside your technical score.

Ownership and accountability. Stories where you took a problem end to end, including debugging, testing, and release, land better than stories where you wrote the feature and handed it off.

Clean, readable code. Candidates report that interviewers pay attention to naming, component structure, and whether the code would make sense to a new team member coming in cold.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Two to three weeks out:
Revise React core concepts: reconciliation, the virtual DOM, hooks (useEffect, useMemo, useCallback, useRef), and common performance pitfalls. Brush up on JavaScript fundamentals including closures, the event loop, prototypes, and async patterns.

One to two weeks out:
Practise two or three medium-difficulty frontend coding problems each day. Build or rebuild a small project using TypeScript so you can explain your type decisions out loud. Review browser APIs you have used, especially around storage, events, and the fetch lifecycle.

Final week:
Prepare four to five STAR stories covering a complex feature, a production bug, a disagreement, a collaboration challenge, and a performance improvement. Do at least one mock interview out loud, not just in your head. Research CommIT: their client sectors, the tech stacks they commonly work with, and any recent public news.

Day before:
Re-read the job description and map your experience to each listed requirement. Prepare two or three thoughtful questions to ask the interviewer. These signal genuine interest and help you evaluate fit from your side too.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping to code without thinking out loud. Interviewers want to follow your reasoning. Stay quiet for too long and they assume you are stuck, even if you are not.

Giving vague answers to behavioural questions. 'I worked on a team project' is not a story. Name the problem, your specific role, and what changed because of what you did.

Memorising definitions rather than understanding them. CommIT interviewers typically follow up on everything you say. If you have recited something without really understanding it, a single follow-up question will make that clear.

Ignoring non-functional concerns. Purely functional code that is untested, inaccessible, or unoptimised will cost you points even if it works. Mention testing, a11y, and performance proactively rather than waiting to be asked.

Not asking questions. Candidates who ask nothing come across as uninterested. Prepare at least two genuine questions about the role, the team structure, or the day-to-day tech environment.

Overstating your experience. If you have not used a tool recently or only touched it lightly, say so. Interviewers appreciate honesty far more than a confident claim that unravels under follow-up questioning.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does CommIT typically have for Frontend Engineer roles?

Candidates report three to four rounds in most cases: a recruiter screening, one or two technical stages, and a final round with a senior stakeholder or team lead. The exact number can vary by team and seniority level. Some senior roles include a take-home assignment or a live coding session alongside the other stages. Ask your recruiter early so you can plan your preparation accordingly.

What salary can I expect as a Frontend Engineer at CommIT?

Based on knok jobradar data for Frontend Engineers across India, indicative ranges are 5-11 LPA at entry level (0-2 years), 12-22 LPA at mid level (3-5 years), 24-40 LPA at senior level (6-9 years), and 38-58+ LPA for Lead or Staff roles. CommIT-specific figures are not publicly reported at scale, so treat these as market benchmarks rather than CommIT guarantees. Cross-check with Glassdoor and levels.fyi before your salary discussion.

Does CommIT ask DSA (data structures and algorithms) questions for frontend roles?

Candidates report that CommIT Frontend Engineer interviews focus more on practical frontend skills such as React, performance, and browser APIs than on pure DSA. That said, some teams include light algorithmic problems, especially at the mid to senior level. It is safer to revise core JavaScript problem-solving patterns alongside your frontend prep rather than skipping algorithms entirely.

Is there a take-home assignment in CommIT's interview process?

Some candidates report receiving a take-home task, typically a small React application or a UI feature build, while others go straight to a live coding round. Whether you get one depends on the team and the specific role. If you do receive a take-home, focus on clean component structure, meaningful tests, and a clear README that explains your decisions rather than just the setup steps.

How should I answer the 'why CommIT' question?

Avoid generic answers about 'great culture' or 'learning opportunities.' Research CommIT's client sectors, the scale of the products they build, and their engineering practices if any are publicly shared. Connect your answer to something specific, such as the type of client problems they solve or a tech challenge that genuinely interests you. Interviewers remember specific, honest answers far more clearly than polished rehearsed ones.

More open slots mean more real opportunities, but high volume also attracts more applicants, so the bar for each individual role does not drop.
