03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How have you optimised a slow API or database query in a production system?

*Situation:* At my previous company, our player-history endpoint was taking several seconds to respond during peak hours, and users were dropping off mid-session.

*Task:* I was asked to bring the response time under an acceptable threshold without changing the API contract.

*Action:* I started by profiling the query and found a full-table scan on a large events table. I added a composite index on player_id and created_at, rewrote the query to use a date-range filter, and introduced a Redis cache for the most recent events per player. I also set up a simple alert so the team would know if latency spiked again.

*Result:* Response time dropped significantly at peak load. Player session length improved noticeably in the week after the fix, based on our analytics dashboard.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you had to fix a critical production bug under time pressure.

*Situation:* Our in-app purchase confirmation webhook started failing silently on a weekend, and players were not receiving their purchased items even though payments had gone through.

*Task:* I was the on-call engineer and needed to restore correct behaviour quickly and communicate clearly with the support team.

*Action:* I checked the error logs and found a null-pointer exception triggered by a new optional field our payment provider had added to their webhook payload. I wrote a targeted null-check fix, tested it locally against a sample payload, deployed behind a feature flag, and verified several live transactions before fully rolling out. I also wrote a post-incident summary so the team could add better payload validation going forward.

*Result:* The webhook was restored within the hour. Affected players received their items via a manual backfill the next day, and we had zero recurrence after the fix.

---

Q: Describe a feature you built end-to-end. What tradeoffs did you make?

*Situation:* Our casual game needed a seasonal leaderboard that reset every two weeks and showed players their rank among friends as well as globally.

*Task:* I owned the feature from backend design through to the API that the mobile client consumed.

*Action:* I chose Redis Sorted Sets for real-time rank lookups because they give fast updates and fast rank reads. For the friends leaderboard I used a separate sorted set scoped to each player's friend list, rebuilt on friend-add events. I deliberately avoided a relational database join approach because the write volume would have caused lock contention. The tradeoff was higher memory usage, which I offset by expiring old season keys automatically.

*Result:* The feature shipped on schedule, handled load comfortably, and the friends leaderboard became the most-used social feature in that release cycle according to product analytics.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

The STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the most reliable structure for behavioural questions. Keep Situation and Task brief, spend most of your time on Action (what you specifically did, not what the team did), and always close with a concrete Result.

For system design questions, use a four-step structure. First, clarify scope: ask about expected load, read/write ratio, and must-have features before drawing anything. Second, sketch the high-level components: clients, API layer, database, cache, and any async queues. Third, deep-dive on one or two critical parts, for example the ranking algorithm or the payment webhook handler. Fourth, discuss tradeoffs: what you chose, what you left out, and why.

For coding questions, narrate your thinking out loud. Interviewers at product companies care as much about how you reason as about whether you reach the optimal solution on the first attempt. State the brute-force approach first, then optimise with the interviewer's input.

For 'tell me about a time' questions, pick examples where you made a decision, not just where your team succeeded. Interviewers want to see individual ownership and accountability, not shared team credit.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Gaming companies look for a few qualities that pure enterprise shops do not always emphasise.

Product empathy. Can you talk about features from a player's point of view? Interviewers notice when a candidate connects engineering decisions to the player experience, not just to metrics. You do not need to be a hardcore gamer, but showing that you care about how the product feels goes a long way.

Comfort with scale spikes. Unlike steady-traffic SaaS products, games can go from quiet to very high concurrent users in hours if a video goes viral or a new event launches. Interviewers will probe whether you have thought about horizontal scaling, queue-based decoupling, and graceful degradation under unexpected load.

Speed of shipping. Casual gaming companies move fast. Interviewers want to see that you can make pragmatic decisions, ship something working, and iterate, rather than spend many sprints designing a perfect architecture.

Ownership under pressure. The on-call and production-bug questions are not only about technical skill. They are checking whether you stay calm, communicate clearly with non-engineers, and take full responsibility for the outcome.

Clean, readable code. Candidates report that reviewers at gaming companies care about naming, modularity, and test coverage, especially because game codebases can grow complex quickly. Show that you write code other people can maintain.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Foundations
Review core data structures (arrays, hash maps, heaps, trees, graphs) and the algorithms that appear most in interviews: sorting, BFS/DFS, and dynamic programming basics. Solve a mix of easy and medium problems daily on any coding practice platform.

Week 2: System Design
Study how to design leaderboards with Redis Sorted Sets, notification pipelines with pub/sub or message queues, and player-profile stores weighing relational versus NoSQL tradeoffs. Read about horizontal scaling patterns and caching strategies. Practise drawing system diagrams out loud, explaining your choices as you go.

Week 3: Behavioural Prep
Write out five to seven stories from your past work using the STAR format. Cover: a production incident, a feature you owned end-to-end, a technical disagreement, and a time you had to learn something fast. Practise saying them aloud so they feel natural, not rehearsed.

Week 4: Company-Specific Prep
Play Timepass Games' titles so you understand the product firsthand. Think about the engineering behind features you use: how the leaderboard updates, how purchases are validated, how notifications are triggered. Prepare two or three thoughtful questions for the interviewer about the team's technical challenges and upcoming roadmap.

Before each round: Re-read the job description, refresh your STAR stories, and do one or two warm-up coding problems to get into thinking mode. 07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping straight to code. In system design rounds, candidates who skip the clarification step often solve the wrong problem. Always confirm scope, load expectations, and must-have features before you start drawing or coding.

Saying 'we' instead of 'I'. Interviewers are evaluating you, not your team. Describe what you personally did and decided, even when the overall success was shared.

Stopping at the answer. After you solve a coding problem, interviewers typically ask follow-ups: what is the time complexity, what would break under load, how would you test this? Anticipate these and address them before being asked.

Ignoring the gaming context. Candidates who give purely generic system design answers without connecting them to game-specific scenarios (player sessions, in-app purchases, leaderboards) miss a chance to show product fit. Tie your examples back to the gaming domain wherever it feels natural.

Not asking questions at the end. Asking nothing signals low interest. Prepare at least two specific questions about the team's technical stack, biggest scaling challenges, or upcoming product areas.

Underestimating the behavioural round. Candidates often over-prepare for coding and under-prepare for 'tell me about a time' questions. These rounds carry significant weight at product companies, especially for evaluating ownership and communication skills.
