01 Overview

Overview

Shavish Group currently has 3 open Solutions Engineer roles. The Solutions Engineer sits at the crossroads of technical depth and customer-facing communication. Your job is to help prospects and clients understand how the product solves their specific problems, support the sales team with demos and proofs of concept, and ensure a smooth handoff after a deal closes.

Candidates typically go through multiple rounds covering technical knowledge, product-fit scenarios, and live communication exercises. 02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up frequently in Solutions Engineer interviews, based on what candidates report:

  1. Walk me through how you would prepare and deliver a technical demo to a client with limited technical background.
  2. Describe a time you identified a gap between what a client asked for and what they actually needed. How did you handle it?
  3. How do you manage live technical objections during a product presentation?
  4. Tell me about a situation where you worked closely with a sales team to win a competitive deal.
  5. How do you manage multiple client proof-of-concept (POC) engagements running at the same time?
  6. Describe a time you had to learn a new technology quickly to support a client requirement.
  7. How do you respond when a client's requirements go beyond what the product can currently do?
  8. Tell me about a time you explained a complex technical concept to a non-technical executive or decision-maker.
  9. Walk me through your approach to responding to an RFP or RFI.
  10. How do you maintain strong relationships with clients after the initial deal closes?
  11. Describe a situation where the sales team made a promise to a client that was technically difficult to fulfill. What did you do?
  12. What questions would you ask in a discovery call to understand a client's core pain points?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format for all behavioral questions. Here are three examples tailored to Solutions Engineer scenarios.

Q: Describe a time you bridged a gap between a client's expectations and what the product could actually deliver.

*Situation:* A large enterprise client had seen a competitor demo and expected our product to replicate a specific workflow exactly.

*Task:* My job was to show the client how our solution could meet their underlying business goal, even if the interface looked different.

*Action:* I set up a working session with their operations lead to understand the core pain point behind the request. I then built a customized demo using their own data format to show an alternative workflow that achieved the same outcome.

*Result:* The client agreed to move forward with a pilot. The account became one of our strongest relationships that year.

---

Q: Describe a time you had to learn a new technology quickly for a client.

*Situation:* A financial services prospect asked us to demonstrate an integration with a payment gateway our team had no prior experience with.

*Task:* I had only a short window before the scheduled demo to get up to speed.

*Action:* I went through the gateway's official documentation, set up a sandbox environment, and arranged a quick knowledge transfer with our backend team. I built a working prototype that showed the integration in action.

*Result:* The demo went smoothly, the prospect's technical team was impressed, and we moved to the next stage of their evaluation.

---

Q: How did you handle a situation where the sales team over-promised a feature to a client?

*Situation:* A salesperson told a client that a specific reporting feature would be available 'in the next release' before checking with the product team.

*Task:* I needed to reset the client's expectations without damaging the relationship or the deal.

*Action:* I requested a call with the client and the salesperson together. I explained the actual roadmap timeline honestly, showed the client workarounds available today, and offered to build a custom report as a short-term solution.

*Result:* The client appreciated the transparency, the deal closed, and we used the incident to improve how our sales and engineering teams coordinate on product promises.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For behavioral questions: STAR is the standard. Every answer needs a clear Situation, Task, Action, and Result. Interviewers lose interest when candidates describe what they 'usually do' in general terms. Ground your answer in a specific past event.

For technical questions: Use a problem-first approach. Start by restating the problem in your own words. Then propose a solution, walk through the trade-offs, and tie the recommendation back to a business outcome. Interviewers are not just testing if you know the right answer; they are evaluating how you think through ambiguity.

For product-fit questions: Use a discovery-then-solution structure. Ask clarifying questions before jumping to an answer. Saying 'Can I ask a couple of questions to better understand the use case?' signals maturity and reflects how a good Solutions Engineer actually operates in the field.

For objection-handling questions: Acknowledge, clarify, then respond. Never dismiss an objection. Show you understand the concern before you address it. Interviewers want to see that you stay calm and methodical when a client pushes back.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Interviewers at the Solutions Engineer level typically look for five things.

Technical credibility. You do not need to know everything, but you must be able to hold a meaningful conversation with a client's engineering team. Show that you can read documentation, build a POC, and explain system concepts clearly.

Communication that scales. The same concept needs to land differently with a developer, a product manager, and a CFO. Give examples of how you have adapted your communication style for different audiences in past roles.

Sales alignment. Solutions Engineers exist to support revenue. Interviewers want to see that you understand how your work connects to pipeline and deal closure. Avoid framing yourself purely as a technical resource.

Customer empathy. Show that you think about the client's long-term success, not just closing the current POC. Candidates who mention post-sales outcomes and client satisfaction tend to stand out.

Composure under pressure. Expect a scenario-based question where something goes wrong during a demo or a client asks a question you cannot answer on the spot. Show that you stay calm, acknowledge the gap, and have a clear plan to follow up.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Understand Shavish Group's business. Visit their website and read any available case studies, product pages, or press releases. Understand who their typical clients are and what problems the product solves. Knowing the company's market position helps you frame your experience relevantly.

Step 2: Practice demos out loud. Record yourself walking through a product explanation or a technical scenario. Review for clarity, pacing, and whether a non-technical listener could follow along. Most candidates underestimate how different speaking out loud feels compared to thinking through a solution silently.

Step 3: Build a bank of STAR stories. Prepare specific examples covering: a difficult client situation, a missed or misaligned expectation, a cross-team collaboration, a fast learning curve, and a competitive deal. Having these ready lets you adapt quickly to any behavioral question.

Step 4: Brush up on core technical concepts. For most Solutions Engineer roles this means API integrations, enterprise software architecture basics, and data flow concepts. Practise whiteboarding a simple system design and explaining it in plain language.

Step 5: Prepare strong closing questions. At the end of each round, a thoughtful question leaves a positive impression. Good options include: 'What does success look like in this role after the initial ramp-up period?' or 'How does the Solutions Engineering team collaborate with the Product team here?'

Step 6: Research the interviewers on LinkedIn. If you know who you are meeting, look at their background. A shared industry or technical interest is a natural conversation anchor and shows genuine interest in the team.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating it as a purely technical interview. Solutions Engineers are evaluated equally on communication and empathy. Candidates who only talk about systems and skip the client-facing dimension often do not advance past the first technical round.

Jumping to solutions without asking questions. Skipping discovery is one of the most common mistakes. Before answering any scenario question, ask a clarifying question. It mirrors the real job and signals maturity to the interviewer.

Vague STAR answers. 'We improved the process and the client was happy' is not a result. Name a specific observable outcome: the deal closed, the client renewed, the escalation was resolved, the POC was approved on schedule.

Badmouthing previous employers. If asked about a difficult stakeholder or a conflict with a sales team, keep your tone neutral. Focus on what you did and what you learned, not on how wrong the other party was.

Ignoring the business context. Every technical answer should connect back to a business value: cost, speed, reliability, or client retention. Candidates who stay only in the technical layer often miss this connection.

Not preparing questions for the interviewer. Showing up with no questions signals low interest. Prepare at least two thoughtful questions for every round, and make them specific to the team or the product.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many interview rounds does Shavish Group typically have for a Solutions Engineer?

Candidates report anywhere from three to five rounds. The process typically includes an HR screen, one or two technical rounds, and a final round with a business or leadership stakeholder. Some candidates also report a practical exercise such as a live demo or a written case study. Confirm the exact structure with your recruiter before your first round so you can prepare accordingly.

Is there a take-home assignment or demo exercise in the interview process?

For Solutions Engineer roles, a practical component is common across companies, and candidates at similar firms report receiving a scenario to present or a mock demo exercise. The goal is usually to assess how you communicate technical information to a non-technical audience. Treat any exercise as seriously as a live round, since the quality of your explanation typically matters as much as the technical content itself.

What salary can I expect for this role at Shavish Group?

Shavish Group has not publicly disclosed salary bands for Solutions Engineer roles. For current benchmarks, check Glassdoor and levels.fyi listings for Solutions Engineers in India, filtering by city and years of experience. Industry surveys suggest compensation for this role varies significantly based on company stage, candidate background, and city. Come prepared with a range based on your own research before the negotiation stage.

How deeply should I know Shavish Group's products before the interview?

You are expected to have done basic homework before the first round. Review their website, read any available case studies, and watch any recorded demos or webinars you can find publicly. You do not need to know every feature, but being able to articulate what the product does and who it serves shows genuine preparation. Candidates who ask informed product questions tend to make a stronger impression than those who ask things easily answered by a quick web search.

What technical topics should I prepare for the technical round?

Focus on areas most common in Solutions Engineering: API integrations, REST concepts, enterprise software architecture, and data flow between systems. Be ready to whiteboard a simple integration scenario or walk through a technical problem out loud. Interviewers are not only checking if you get the right answer; they are watching how you reason through uncertainty and whether you ask good clarifying questions before proposing a solution.

When is the right time to discuss salary during the Shavish Group interview process?

Salary discussions typically happen during the HR screen or after a formal offer is made. Avoid raising compensation in a technical or business round unless the interviewer brings it up first. Come to any salary discussion prepared with a range backed by your research from Glassdoor and publicly reported industry surveys, and be ready to connect that range to your specific experience and the value you bring.
