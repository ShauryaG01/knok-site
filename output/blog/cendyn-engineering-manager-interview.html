01 Overview

Overview

Cendyn builds CRM, marketing automation, and data analytics products for the global hospitality industry. As an Engineering Manager at Cendyn, you lead teams that ship SaaS products used by hotel chains and resorts around the world.

With Cendyn currently listing 24 open roles, the company is actively expanding its engineering organisation. The interview process typically spans multiple rounds: candidates report an initial HR screen, a technical or case discussion with a senior engineering leader, and a final round with cross-functional stakeholders. Round names and sequencing vary, so treat each conversation as an opportunity to show both leadership depth and hospitality domain awareness.

This guide covers 12 company-specific questions to practise, three full STAR answers, the frameworks that typically matter to Cendyn interviewers, and a prep plan you can follow in the week before your interview.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

1. How do you build and scale an engineering team that delivers SaaS features on a predictable release cadence?

2. Cendyn's products serve hotel groups with very different technical maturity levels. How have you managed integrations or APIs for clients with legacy systems?

3. Walk us through how you balance technical debt against new feature requests when product pressure is high.

4. Describe how you have worked with a Product Manager to shape a roadmap when engineering constraints pushed back on the original plan.

5. Cendyn operates across multiple time zones and markets. How have you led a distributed or cross-geography team?

6. Tell us about a time a production incident affected a major client. What did you do, and what did the team change afterward?

7. How do you measure the health and velocity of your engineering team beyond just story points?

8. Hospitality is a 24/7 industry. How do you set up on-call rotations and incident response without burning out the team?

9. How have you contributed to hiring decisions and built a strong engineering bench in your previous roles?

10. Give an example of a significant architectural decision you made. How did you align stakeholders and manage the risk?

11. How do you keep engineers motivated and growing when the work is largely incremental maintenance and improvement?

12. Cendyn invests in data-driven personalisation for hotels. Have you led any data platform, analytics, or ML-adjacent engineering work?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How do you balance technical debt against feature delivery when product pressure is high?

*Situation:* At my previous company, we had a core booking-engine module that had not been refactored in several years. Product wanted three major features shipped in a single quarter.

*Task:* I needed to deliver the features on schedule without making the codebase worse and risking stability for existing clients.

*Action:* I introduced a 'debt tax' policy: for every sprint, the team reserved a fixed portion of capacity for targeted refactoring, chosen by the engineers closest to the code. I presented this to the product lead with a concrete risk register showing what could break if we skipped it. We agreed on a phased feature rollout that gave us breathing room.

*Result:* All three features shipped within the quarter. Production incidents in that module dropped over the following sprints, and engineers reported higher confidence in touching that area of the code.

---

Q: Tell us about a time a production incident affected a major client. What did you do?

*Situation:* A data pipeline feeding our reporting dashboard failed silently over a weekend. A large hotel-chain client noticed incorrect revenue figures on a Monday morning.

*Task:* I had to manage client communication, coordinate the engineering fix, and confirm root cause before declaring the incident resolved.

*Action:* I set up a war-room call immediately, assigned a dedicated incident lead from the team, and personally joined the client call with our Customer Success lead to give a transparent status update every couple of hours. I kept non-incident work paused for the engineers working on the fix.

*Result:* The pipeline was restored and validated within the same day. We introduced automated data-quality checks that caught similar gaps in staging before they could reach production. The client acknowledged the response quality in their quarterly review.

---

Q: How have you worked with a Product Manager to shape a roadmap when engineering constraints pushed back?

*Situation:* Our PM had committed to a major CRM integration feature by a date that assumed our team had bandwidth it did not have, given an ongoing platform migration.

*Task:* I needed to reset expectations without damaging trust with the PM or the sales team that had already mentioned the feature to prospects.

*Action:* I prepared a capacity model showing actual engineer availability week-by-week, with two delivery options: a phased MVP on the original date and the full feature on a later date. I brought the PM into the trade-off conversation early rather than simply saying 'no'. We jointly presented both options to the sales lead.

*Result:* The team delivered the MVP on the original date, which satisfied the prospect conversations in progress. The full integration followed the next month. The PM described it as the clearest capacity discussion she had experienced with an engineering counterpart.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the backbone of behavioural answers at Cendyn. Keep your Situation brief (one or two sentences), make your Action the longest part (what specifically you did, not what 'we' did), and always land on a measurable or clearly observable Result.

The Constraint-Trade-off-Outcome frame works well for technical or roadmap questions. State the constraint clearly, name the trade-offs you considered, explain the choice you made, and describe what happened.

The People-Process-Product frame suits leadership philosophy questions. When asked how you build teams or manage delivery, cover how you develop people, what processes you put in place, and how those choices affect the product quality your clients experience.

For hospitality-specific questions, anchor your answer in client impact. Cendyn's customers are hotel operators, so phrases like 'this affected guest-facing booking data' or 'the hotel revenue team depended on this report' signal domain awareness even if your background is not in hospitality.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Cendyn interviewers typically look for competencies beyond standard engineering leadership.

Client-first thinking. Because Cendyn's products are used by hotel operations teams, interviewers want to see that you understand the downstream impact of engineering decisions on non-technical end users.

SaaS delivery discipline. Candidates who can speak to release cadence, feature flags, backward-compatible APIs, and multi-tenant architecture tend to stand out. You do not need hospitality experience, but you need SaaS experience.

Calm under operational pressure. The hospitality industry runs around the clock. Interviewers probe for incident management maturity and the ability to keep teams steady during outages.

Cross-functional collaboration. Cendyn sits at the intersection of engineering, sales, and customer success. Candidates who work fluidly with non-engineering stakeholders without losing technical rigour are valued.

Team growth ownership. Beyond delivery, interviewers want to know how you hire, mentor, and retain engineers. Expect at least one question on how you have handled an underperforming team member or an unexpected departure.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Days 1-2: Company and product research. Read Cendyn's public product pages and press releases from 2024-2026. Understand their CRM, marketing automation, and data products. Check Glassdoor and LinkedIn reviews to get a sense of the engineering culture, keeping in mind that sample sizes are small.

Days 3-4: Story inventory. Write out six to eight stories from your career using STAR format. Cover: a technical debt decision, an incident response, a cross-functional conflict, a hiring or team-building moment, and a time you pushed back on an unrealistic deadline. Practise saying each one aloud in under three minutes.

Day 5: Frameworks and questions. Review the STAR, Constraint-Trade-off-Outcome, and People-Process-Product frames in this guide. Practise mapping your stories to the 12 questions above. Prepare two or three thoughtful questions to ask your interviewers, for example about the team's current delivery rhythm or how engineering and product planning are structured at Cendyn.

Day 6: Mock interview. Do at least one timed mock with a peer or record yourself. Check that you are not saying 'we' when you mean 'I', and that every answer closes with a concrete result.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Saying 'we' instead of 'I'. Interviewers are evaluating your contribution specifically. When you say 'we delivered' without explaining your personal role, the interviewer cannot assess your individual impact.

Skipping the Result. Many candidates give a rich Situation and Action but trail off before stating what actually happened. Always close with a concrete outcome: something changed, improved, or was learned.

Treating hospitality as irrelevant. Even if you come from fintech or e-commerce, connect your experience to Cendyn's context. Saying 'my previous clients were banks, but the operational uptime requirement maps directly to hotel booking systems' shows awareness the interviewer will notice.

Over-explaining technology to non-technical interviewers. If a recruiter or HR partner is on the call, calibrate your language. Save architecture depth for technical rounds.

Not preparing questions to ask. Arriving without questions signals low interest. Ask about team size, current engineering challenges, or how success is measured in the first few months.

Underselling the people side. Engineering Manager roles at product companies like Cendyn weigh people leadership heavily. If your answers focus entirely on code and architecture with nothing on mentoring, hiring, or conflict resolution, you leave a significant gap in the interviewer's picture of you.
