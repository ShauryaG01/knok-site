Most Asked Questions

  11. Tell me about a trade-off you made between system performance and data correctness. What did you choose and why?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How would you implement idempotency in a payment API?

*Situation:* At my previous company, the mobile app had intermittent network drops. Users would tap 'Pay', the request would time out on the client, and they would tap again. Because we had no idempotency mechanism, both requests would sometimes go through, resulting in duplicate charges reaching production.

*Task:* I was asked to design and ship a solution that would make our payment initiation endpoint safe to retry without creating duplicate transactions.

*Action:* I designed an idempotency key system. The client generates a unique key per payment attempt and sends it as a request header. On the server side, before processing any request, the service checks a Redis cache for that key. If the key exists, it returns the stored result immediately without executing the payment logic again. If the key is new, it processes the request and stores the result in the cache with a time-based expiry. I also added a database unique constraint on the key as a hard safeguard for cache misses.

*Result:* Duplicate transaction complaints dropped to zero within the first week after rollout. The pattern became the standard for all write endpoints in our payment service.

---

Q: Describe a production bug you debugged under pressure.

*Situation:* On a Friday evening, our alerting system fired because a significant portion of transactions were failing silently. Users saw 'Payment failed', but no exception appeared in our logs.

*Task:* I was the on-call engineer that evening. I needed to identify the root cause and restore normal operation as quickly as possible, with real money transactions affected.

*Action:* I pulled the API response logs and compared them against records from our payment gateway. I found that our response parser was treating any successful HTTP status from the gateway as a completed transaction, without reading the response body. The gateway had recently introduced a new field indicating a 'soft decline', which our code did not handle. I patched the response handler to read the body correctly, wrote a regression test for the new case, and deployed a hotfix through our standard release process.

*Result:* Transaction success rates recovered within minutes of the hotfix going live. We added a monitoring rule that alerts whenever the gateway body status and the HTTP-level status disagree, which has since caught similar issues early.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you improved the performance of a slow database query.

*Situation:* Our transaction history page was timing out for users who had many months of payment records. The backend query was taking several seconds on average according to our query profiler, and users were noticing.

*Task:* I was assigned to bring the query time down without changing the data model or requiring a migration that would lock the table during peak hours.

*Action:* I ran EXPLAIN ANALYZE on the query and found it was doing a full table scan on a large transactions table. The filter columns 'user_id' and 'created_at' were indexed separately but not together. I added a composite index on both columns. I also rewrote the pagination logic to use keyset pagination instead of OFFSET, which avoids scanning all rows before the current page on large datasets.

*Result:* Query time dropped to a fraction of the previous baseline according to our query monitoring dashboard. The transaction history page stopped timing out entirely, and the approach was adopted as the standard pagination pattern across the service.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the most reliable structure for behavioral questions at any company. Keep each component tight: one or two sentences for Situation and Task, the bulk of your answer in Action (what you personally did, not what the team did), and a concrete Result with a measurable outcome where possible.

For system design questions, use a structured approach: clarify requirements and scale constraints first, sketch the high-level architecture, then drill into the components your interviewer focuses on. At a payments company, proactively address what happens when a service goes down or a request fails mid-flight. Interviewers here commonly probe the consistency and failure-handling parts of any design, so bring those up without waiting to be asked.

For debugging and investigation questions, narrate your thought process as a structured elimination: what signals you checked first, what you ruled out, and how you confirmed the root cause before applying the fix. Avoid jumping straight to the answer without showing the reasoning.

On quantifying results: use real numbers from your own experience where you have them. If you do not recall an exact figure, say something like 'the query time dropped significantly according to our profiler' rather than inventing a number. Interviewers value intellectual honesty over inflated metrics.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Freecharge handles real money for a large user base, so the engineering bar for correctness and reliability is high. Candidates report that interviewers pay close attention to whether you understand why consistency and failure handling matter, not just whether you can name the right design pattern.

Ownership and follow-through. Interviewers typically want to see that you take responsibility end to end, from detecting a problem through fixing it to monitoring it afterward. Stories that stop at 'I wrote the code' rarely score as well as stories that continue to 'and here is how I knew it was working.'

Practical fintech awareness. You do not need prior payments experience, but knowing terms like idempotency, distributed transactions, eventual consistency, and reconciliation signals that you understand the stakes of financial software.

Clean code under time pressure. Coding rounds typically involve standard data structures and algorithm problems. Candidates report that reviewers notice whether the code is readable and whether the candidate tests edge cases before declaring a solution complete.

Communication. Fintech roles involve regular coordination with product, compliance, and operations teams. Interviewers often check whether you can explain a technical decision clearly to someone who is not an engineer.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Weeks one and two: Core technical skills. Refresh data structures and algorithms with a focus on arrays, hashmaps, trees, graphs, and recursion. Then shift to system design for payments: read about distributed consistency, idempotency, event-driven architecture, and how UPI and payment gateways work at a high level. Practice designing systems out loud, explaining trade-offs as you go.

Week three: Behavioral prep and company research. Write out several STAR stories from your career covering at least one production incident you handled, one performance improvement you drove, one disagreement you navigated with a colleague, and one project you owned from start to finish. Look up freecharge's current products and the Axis Bank partnership context so you can speak to why you want to work there specifically.

Week four: Mock interviews and weak-spot review. Do at least two full mock interviews, one coding and one system design. Review what you got wrong or found difficult, and practice those areas before the real interview. Prepare two or three specific questions to ask your interviewer about the team's current technical challenges.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping the 'why' in system design. Candidates who list components without explaining why they made each choice lose points quickly. Always connect each design decision back to the requirements, especially reliability and consistency for a payments system.

Designing only the happy path. A very common gap is building out the flow that works but not addressing what happens when a downstream service is slow, unavailable, or returns an unexpected response. Interviewers at payments companies probe this specifically. Build failure handling into your answer without waiting to be asked.

Vague STAR answers. Saying 'I improved performance' without explaining what you measured, what you changed, and what the outcome was gives the interviewer nothing concrete to evaluate. Be specific about your own contribution, not your team's.

Ignoring edge cases in code. Many candidates produce a working solution for the typical input but do not think through null inputs, empty collections, or integer overflow. Walk through at least one edge case before saying your solution is complete.

Over-engineering the design. Proposing a complex multi-region architecture for a problem that calls for a simple cache or queue signals poor judgment. Match the complexity of your solution to the scale described in the question.

Not asking clarifying questions. Starting a design or coding problem without confirming the constraints is a red flag for experienced interviewers. Ask about expected scale, consistency requirements, and acceptable trade-offs before you begin.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the freecharge Software Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates typically report a process of 3-4 rounds. This usually includes a recruiter or HR screening call, one or two technical rounds covering coding and system design, and a final round with a hiring manager or senior engineer. The exact number of rounds can vary by team and seniority level, so it is worth confirming with the recruiter after your first call.

What coding topics should I focus on for a fintech company like freecharge?

Standard data structures and algorithms form the base: arrays, hashmaps, trees, graphs, and recursion. Beyond that, fintech interviews often include questions about concurrency, database design, and API reliability. Understanding idempotency and distributed consistency at a conceptual level will help you stand out, even in rounds that do not ask a direct question on those topics.

Does freecharge ask system design questions at the entry level?

Candidates report that system design becomes a heavier focus from the mid level upward. At the entry level (0-2 years of experience), interviewers typically focus on coding, data structures, and object-oriented design. That said, even junior-level interviews at a fintech company can include scenario-based thinking, so a basic understanding of how a payment flow works end to end is worth having.

How important is fintech domain knowledge for the interview?

You do not need to be a payments expert, but basic familiarity with how digital payments work in India (UPI, payment gateways, reconciliation) provides helpful context. More important is that you understand the engineering challenges these systems create: the need for idempotency, strong consistency, and reliable failure handling. Demonstrating that you understand why these properties matter will signal readiness for the domain without requiring years of payments experience.

What is the best way to prepare for behavioral questions at freecharge?

Write out several stories from your career using the STAR format before your interview. Try to cover a range of scenarios: handling a production incident, disagreeing constructively with a team member, owning a project from start to finish, and improving something that was already working. Practice saying these stories out loud so your answers feel natural. Interviewers at product-led companies like freecharge are looking for evidence of ownership and clear thinking, not polished scripts.
