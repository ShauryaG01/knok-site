01 Hiring Overview

Hiring Overview

Amazon is one of the largest and most active tech employers in India, with roles spanning AWS cloud, e-commerce, logistics, advertising, and corporate functions. As of July 2026, Amazon has 64 open roles actively tracked by knok. These cover software development, product management, data science, business analysis, and operations, across experience levels from fresher to senior leader.

Getting hired at Amazon is competitive but very doable if you understand their process. Unlike most companies, Amazon does not just test your skills. It tests how you think, how you make decisions, and whether you align with the way they operate. That entire framework is built on their Leadership Principles, a set of values that every interviewer is trained to assess in every round.

03 Interview Process

Interview Process

Amazon's process has several distinct stages. Each stage tests something different, and clearing every one requires different preparation.

Stage 1: Application and resume screen. Apply on Amazon's careers page or through job aggregators. Recruiters filter by skills, experience, and keyword match. Tailor your resume to the specific job description rather than sending a generic one.

Stage 2: Online Assessment (OA). For software, data, and some business roles, shortlisted candidates receive a timed online test, usually coding problems or work-style simulations. Complete it promptly because the invite window is short.

Stage 3: Recruiter screen. A brief call with an Amazon recruiter to confirm your background, explain the role, and walk you through what comes next. Prepare a concise intro and a few highlights from your past work.

Stage 4: Technical phone screen. For engineering and data roles, expect a one-on-one video call with a current Amazon employee. You will likely be asked to write code live and walk through your reasoning as you go.

Stage 5: The Loop (virtual onsite). This is the main event. The loop is a series of back-to-back video interviews with different Amazon employees, commonly reported as five or more separate sessions in a single day or across two days. Each interviewer is assigned specific Leadership Principles to probe, alongside role-specific questions. One person in every loop is the 'Bar Raiser,' a trained independent evaluator whose job is to uphold Amazon's hiring standard across the company, not just fill your team's open position.

Stage 6: Debrief and decision. After the loop, all interviewers meet to share written feedback. The Bar Raiser holds veto power. A decision is usually communicated within a few weeks.

Stage 7: Offer. If selected, your recruiter shares the offer. Amazon offers typically include base salary, a signing bonus, and an RSU grant that vests over a multi-year schedule. The offer is negotiable.

04 What They Look For

What They Look For

Amazon's interview process is designed to surface a specific kind of candidate: someone who takes ownership, thinks clearly, and acts decisively. Here is what interviewers are actually evaluating.

Structured, specific STAR answers. Every behavioural question expects a Situation, Task, Action, Result format. Vague answers like 'we improved the process' will not work. Interviewers want to know exactly what you personally did and what changed because of it. Saying 'I' instead of 'we' matters here.

Real ownership. Amazon values people who go beyond their job description without being asked. The strongest candidates share examples where they spotted a problem, took charge, and solved it, even when it was not strictly their responsibility.

Technical depth for engineering roles. For software roles, you need solid skills in data structures, algorithms, and system design. Expect to write working code during interviews, not pseudocode. For senior roles, architectural trade-offs are a central part of the assessment.

Customer-first thinking. Amazon will ask how you have prioritised the end user in past decisions. Prepare examples where you chose the harder internal path because it was better for the customer.

Comfort with ambiguity and speed. They are looking for people who can make sound decisions with incomplete information and correct course quickly. Candidates who wait for perfect conditions before acting tend to struggle in Amazon interviews.

05 How To Prepare

How To Prepare

Preparing for Amazon is a two-track effort: the technical side and the behavioural side. Both matter equally, and both take time.

Map your stories to Leadership Principles. Go through Amazon's published Leadership Principles one by one. For each, prepare at least one strong STAR story from your actual work history. Priority areas include ownership, delivering results, inventing and simplifying, and disagree and commit. Write these stories down and practise them out loud so they feel natural, not memorised.

Practice coding consistently. For tech roles, build a daily habit of solving data structures and algorithm problems on platforms like LeetCode. Focus on medium difficulty problems and work up to harder ones. During the actual interview, narrate your thinking as you code.

Prepare for system design. If you are applying for a mid to senior engineering role, expect system design rounds. Practise designing scalable systems and be ready to articulate trade-offs clearly, such as consistency versus availability, or build versus buy.

Tailor your application. Read the job description carefully and mirror its language in your resume. Understand what the team builds and how it connects to Amazon's broader business goals.

Do mock interviews. Practise LP questions with a friend or record yourself answering out loud. The Bar Raiser is listening for genuine, specific stories. Over-polished, corporate-sounding answers are easy to spot and tend to score lower.

06 Culture And Values

Culture And Values

Amazon's culture is principle-driven in a way that is more literal than at most companies. The Leadership Principles are not a poster on the wall. They are the actual framework used for hiring, performance reviews, and day-to-day decisions.

Customer obsession above everything. Amazon employees are expected to start every decision with the customer's need, not a competitor's move or internal convenience. This shows up in interviews constantly, and it shows up on the job too.

High bar and frugality. Amazon sets high standards and expects teams to meet them with lean resources. This affects how roles are scoped, how decisions get made, and how performance is evaluated. 'Do more with less' is taken seriously at every level.

Disagree and commit. Amazon encourages honest disagreement before a decision is made. Once the decision is final, full commitment is expected from everyone, including those who disagreed. This can feel unusual if you are used to consensus-heavy or hierarchical workplaces.

Data over opinion. Claims without evidence carry less weight here. Whether in an interview or a team meeting, grounding your points in outcomes and numbers is the norm.

Demanding and fast-paced. Amazon is publicly reported as one of the more intense tech work environments in the industry. Goals are clear, expectations are explicit, and the pace is high. If you thrive in structured, outcome-oriented settings, you will likely find Amazon motivating. If you prefer slower, more exploratory cultures, it is worth being honest with yourself before applying.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How long does the Amazon hiring process take from application to offer?

The timeline varies by team and role, but many candidates publicly report the full process taking several weeks from application to final decision. The loop is usually scheduled quickly once you clear the earlier screening stages. After the loop, the debrief and offer stage typically adds another week or two.

What is the Bar Raiser and should I be worried about them?

The Bar Raiser is a trained Amazon employee from outside your target team who joins the loop to maintain a consistent hiring standard across the company. Their job is not to fail you. It is to make sure every new hire is genuinely strong and not just a good fit for one team's immediate need. The best approach is the same as any other round: be honest, be specific, and let your real examples do the work.

Can I apply to multiple Amazon roles at the same time?

Yes, you can apply to multiple roles simultaneously, and many candidates do. It is generally more effective to focus on a small number of roles that genuinely match your background rather than applying broadly and hoping something sticks. Amazon recruiters can see multiple active applications in their system, so a targeted approach also signals that you know what you are looking for.

Does Amazon hire freshers in India?

Yes, Amazon actively recruits freshers in India through campus placements and off-campus drives, particularly for software development engineer and operations roles. The online assessment is typically the first filter for entry-level candidates. Strong coding fundamentals and a few solid project examples from college or internships carry significant weight at this stage.

How do I negotiate my Amazon offer?

Amazon offers commonly include base pay, a joining bonus, and RSUs that vest over a multi-year schedule. The RSU component is often where the most flexibility exists. Come to the negotiation with data from publicly reported sources like Glassdoor or levels.fyi to anchor your counter-offer. Be direct about what you are asking for and explain why, without issuing ultimatums.

If I get rejected, how soon can I reapply?

You can reapply after a waiting period that is commonly cited as several months following a rejection. Use that time to genuinely improve in the areas where you struggled, whether that is coding, STAR storytelling, or domain knowledge. A fresh application is treated as a fresh attempt, so real improvement in your preparation can make a meaningful difference the second time around.
