01 Overview

Overview

Because CashKaro handles live cashback transactions and affiliate redirects, expect targeted questions on deep linking, network reliability, and secure data handling.

Most Asked Questions

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for all experience-based questions. Here are three worked examples:

Q: Tell us about a time you improved app performance.

*Situation:* Our shopping app had a noticeable cold-start delay, and users were dropping off before the home feed loaded.

*Task:* I was asked to bring the startup time down significantly without removing any features.

*Action:* I profiled the app with Android Studio's CPU profiler and found three heavyweight SDKs initialising on the main thread at launch. I moved them to a background coroutine using a lazy-init pattern and replaced synchronous SharedPreferences calls with DataStore reads.

*Result:* Firebase Performance traces showed a significant drop in cold-start time. Crash-free sessions also improved because the main thread was no longer blocked during busy periods.

---

Q: How have you handled deep linking in an app with affiliate redirect flows?

*Situation:* At my previous company, we had a cashback flow where users left the app, completed a purchase on a partner site, then returned. Many users did not come back at all.

*Task:* I owned the re-engagement flow and needed to make the return-to-app experience seamless and verified.

*Action:* I implemented App Links with a Digital Asset Links file hosted on our domain, so the redirect opened directly in the app with no browser chooser prompt. I also stored the pending cashback state in a local database before the user left, so on return we could resume the correct confirmation screen even if the app had been killed.

*Result:* Return-to-app rate improved noticeably, and support tickets about 'missing cashback' dropped in the following sprint cycle.

---

Q: Describe a time you worked across teams to ship a feature under a tight deadline.

*Situation:* Our team had a flash-sale feature that had to go live before a major shopping festival. The backend team hit a delay.

*Task:* I needed to keep the Android side ready so we could integrate quickly the moment the API was available.

*Action:* I built the complete UI and local state management against a mock API using a repository abstraction. I wrote integration tests against the mock so the team could review the end-to-end experience early. When the real API shipped, swapping in the live endpoint was straightforward.

*Result:* We hit the release date. QA signed off the same day the backend merged, because most testing had already happened against the mock.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For technical questions, lead with your reasoning before the code. Interviewers at product companies like CashKaro want to see you understand the 'why', not just the 'how'. A strong pattern: state the constraint you were working under, name the approach you chose, explain one trade-off you considered, then describe the outcome.

For architecture or design questions, draw on the layered approach familiar from modern Android: UI layer (Jetpack Compose or XML), domain layer (use cases), data layer (repository pattern). CashKaro's app is transaction-heavy, so show you think about data consistency and error states, not just the happy path.

For past-experience questions, use STAR. Keep the Situation brief, one to two sentences. Spend most of your time on Action, because that reveals your actual skills. Always close with a concrete Result, even a qualitative one ('reduced support tickets', 'cut load time noticeably').

For design questions, ask one clarifying question first, for example: 'Is this for a single partner or will it need to handle multiple affiliate configurations?' This signals product thinking. Then walk your answer through layers: data model, API contract, Android architecture, edge cases.

Pace yourself. A brief pause to think reads as confidence, not confusion. Candidates who rush often skip the trade-off discussion, which is where strong answers separate themselves.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

CashKaro's Android team, like most growth-stage startups, looks for engineers who can own a feature end to end, not just implement tickets.

Ownership instinct is the most commonly reported theme from candidates. Can you spot a bug outside your scope and fix it anyway? Did you write the spec or wait for one? Come with concrete stories that show this.

Production mindset. CashKaro handles real money and affiliate commissions. Interviewers want evidence you think about failure modes: what happens if the network drops mid-redirect? What if a duplicate transaction fires? Defensive coding and error-state design matter here more than at a typical utility app.

Android depth. You should be fluent with the modern Android stack: Kotlin, coroutines and Flow, Jetpack (ViewModel, Navigation, Room, WorkManager), and at least one architecture pattern (MVVM or MVI). Interviewers probe edge cases, not just textbook definitions.

Clear communication. Mid-level and senior candidates are expected to explain trade-offs. If you chose an approach in a past project, say what you gave up and why it was worth it. Vague answers without context tend to flag as surface-level knowledge.

Comfort with speed. CashKaro moves quickly. Interviewers often ask how you handle changing requirements or shifting priorities. Show flexibility without suggesting you cut corners on code quality.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Core Android revision

Revisit the Android lifecycle in depth: activity, fragment, and ViewModel interactions. Practice coroutines and Flow with real async scenarios. Review RecyclerView optimisation (DiffUtil, multiple view types, item animations). Make sure you can explain MVVM vs MVI from first principles, not just a diagram.

Week 2: CashKaro-specific topics

Install the CashKaro app and use it as a test user. Observe the cashback redirect flow, the deals listing screen, and how notifications behave. Think about how you would build each screen. Study App Links and deep link handling. Review payment SDK integration patterns and Android Keystore usage for secure token storage.

Week 3: System design and mock interviews

Practice designing the CashKaro home feed (pagination, caching, offline state). Design a cashback tracking system (what events do you fire, how do you handle duplicates?). Run at least two timed mock coding sessions. Record yourself answering a behavioural question and check whether your STAR answers are crisp and outcome-focused.

Before the interview

Check the job description for any specific libraries or tools mentioned. Prepare two to three questions for the interviewer that show genuine product curiosity, for example: 'How does the Android team handle feature rollouts during a high-traffic sale event?'

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping the 'why'. Saying 'I used Room for local caching' without explaining why you chose Room over another option makes the answer feel shallow. Add one sentence of reasoning to every technical choice.

Over-engineering in design rounds. Proposing a microservices or event-streaming architecture for a feature that needs a simple API and a local database is a common trap. Match your solution's complexity to the actual problem scale.

Ignoring error states. Many candidates design only the happy path. For a cashback app, the redirect failing or the transaction timing out is not an edge case, it is a core flow. Show you handle it from the start.

Vague results in STAR answers. 'The feature went live successfully' is not a result. Tie outcomes to something concrete: launch time improved, crash rate dropped, support tickets fell. Even rough qualitative comparisons are better than a non-answer.

Not asking clarifying questions. In design rounds, jumping straight into an answer without confirming scope leads you off track. One good clarifying question impresses more than rushing into a detailed but wrong-direction answer.

Underselling personal contribution. Candidates often say 'we did X' for everything. Interviewers want to know your specific role. Saying 'I designed the data layer, and the team shipped it together' is accurate and confident, not boastful.
