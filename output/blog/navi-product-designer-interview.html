01 Overview

Overview

Navi Technologies is a Bangalore-based fintech building lending, insurance, and mutual fund products for everyday Indian users. The design team carries a clear brief: make complex financial decisions feel safe, simple, and fast for people who may be managing money digitally for the first time.

Candidates report that the interview process typically has three to five rounds. It usually begins with a portfolio review call, followed by a design challenge (take-home or live), and then one or two rounds with senior designers or product leads. Some candidates also report a brief culture or values discussion. There are no publicly confirmed round names, so treat any structure you read online as a rough guide, not a guarantee.

Navi currently has 60 open Product Designer roles (per knok jobradar, as of July 2026), which signals active team growth across multiple product areas. Expect interviews to cover both craft (visual quality, interaction details) and thinking (how you frame problems, how you use data).

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Navi-specific questions candidates report seeing most often:

  1. Walk me through a project where you had to simplify a complex or jargon-heavy user flow. What decisions did you make?
  2. How do you design for trust in a financial product, specifically for users who are nervous about sharing personal data?
  3. Describe a time you used analytics or user research to change a design decision you had already made.
  4. How would you approach redesigning the loan application experience in the Navi app to improve completion?
  5. Tell us about a conflict you had with a PM or engineer over a design. How did it get resolved?
  6. How do you balance business goals (conversion, revenue) with user needs (ease, transparency) in a single design?
  7. What metrics would you define to measure the success of a new onboarding flow for first-time investors?
  8. Walk me through your process from problem brief to final handoff. What steps do you never skip?
  9. How do you design for low-bandwidth or low-literacy users without compromising on the product experience?
  10. Tell us about a design that shipped but did not perform as expected. What did you learn and what did you change?
  11. How would you run a quick usability test if you had only three days and a very small budget?
  12. Where do you see the biggest UX gap in fintech products built for India today?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk me through a project where you simplified a complex user flow.

*Situation:* At my previous company, we had a fixed deposit booking flow built for a web-first audience. When we expanded to mobile, users were dropping off midway through the form. Internal review flagged it as a top conversion priority.

*Task:* I was asked to redesign the mobile flow to reduce drop-off, with a specific focus on users in smaller cities booking a fixed deposit for the very first time.

*Action:* I ran moderated user tests with six participants from Tier 2 cities. Every confusing label ('tenure', 'pre-closure penalty', 'TDS deduction') was flagged. I replaced jargon with plain-language equivalents, added contextual help tooltips, broke a single long form into four shorter steps with a progress bar, and moved the interest payout preview to the first screen so users saw the benefit before they saw the form.

*Result:* Product analytics shared at the next sprint review showed a meaningful improvement in completion. The PM highlighted the redesign as a model for handling similar flows across the app.

---

Q: Tell us about a conflict with a PM over a design decision.

*Situation:* I was designing a loan repayment reminder screen. The PM wanted a strong red warning banner at the top to drive urgency. I felt this would create anxiety for users who were already stressed about debt.

*Task:* My goal was to align on a design that met the business need (timely repayments) without making the user feel punished or shamed.

*Action:* I pulled up research on financial anxiety in borrowing contexts and ran a small preference test with five participants, showing two variants: the red warning version and a calmer 'payment coming up' version with a soft blue tone. I presented both to the PM with the data, acknowledged the business goal, and proposed we A/B test both versions.

*Result:* The PM agreed to the test. The calmer version had a higher tap-through rate on the 'Pay Now' button (reported in our sprint retrospective), and we shipped that version. The PM later used the same framework, test instead of argue, for two other design debates.

---

Q: How do you measure the success of an onboarding flow?

*Situation:* When I joined my last team, the mutual fund onboarding flow had no clear success metric. Different stakeholders tracked different things, so it was impossible to know if changes were working.

*Task:* I proposed defining a shared success framework before we redesigned anything.

*Action:* I ran a workshop with the PM, data analyst, and business lead. We agreed on three primary metrics: activation rate (user completes KYC and links bank account), time to first investment, and first-week retention after onboarding. I also added a secondary metric: drop-off by step, so we could pinpoint exactly where users left.

*Result:* The framework became the team standard for all onboarding-related work. When we shipped the redesigned flow, we had clear before-and-after data to present to leadership instead of relying on gut feel.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For behavioral questions, use the STAR format: Situation (one or two sentences of context), Task (what you were responsible for), Action (specific things you did, not 'we'), Result (a concrete outcome, even if qualitative). Keep each section tight. Interviewers lose interest when the Situation section runs too long.

For design challenge questions, open with this three-part structure before you sketch anything:
1. Reframe the problem: 'Before I jump to solutions, let me clarify what problem we are actually solving and for whom.'
2. Constraints check: 'What are the business, technical, and time constraints I should design within?'
3. Metrics first: 'What does success look like, and how will we measure it?'

For product thinking questions (like 'how would you redesign X'), use a problem-user-metric-idea-tradeoff flow. State the problem, identify the specific user segment, define what good looks like, propose two or three ideas, and honestly discuss tradeoffs.

For disagreement questions, always show that you used data or research rather than personal preference. Candidates report that Navi interviewers respond well to designers who can back a decision with evidence, not just conviction.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates report and Navi's product context, interviewers are typically looking for five things.

Simplicity under complexity. Navi's products touch first-time borrowers and investors. Interviewers want to see that you default to clarity, not cleverness. If your portfolio is full of delightful animations but lacks examples of simplifying hard flows, fix that before your interview.

Data and research fluency. You do not need to be a data scientist, but you should be comfortable reading analytics dashboards, designing experiments, and triangulating between quantitative signals and qualitative insight. Have a real example ready.

Cross-functional fluency. Navi moves fast. Designers are expected to work closely with PMs and engineers, not hand off and disappear. Show that you can push back constructively and ship pragmatically.

Ownership. With 60 open roles as of July 2026 (per knok jobradar), the team is scaling across product areas. They need designers who drive projects forward, not wait for direction. Talk about times you spotted a problem no one asked you to solve.

India-first thinking. Navi's core users are not metro tech-savvy professionals. If your portfolio shows work for Tier 2 or Tier 3 audiences, or if you can articulate what makes India-specific design different, that stands out strongly.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Portfolio and product audit
Review your three to five best projects. For each, prepare a crisp narrative: problem, your role, key decisions, outcome. Then download and use the Navi app for at least a week. Note three flows you think work well and three you would redesign, and be ready to explain why in concrete terms.

Week 2: Practice design challenges
Pick one fintech flow (onboarding, loan application, KYC) and redesign it from scratch. Give yourself a firm time limit and practice staying within it. Then write a short case study explaining your decisions and practice presenting it out loud. This mirrors the live design round candidates typically describe.

Week 3: Behavioral prep and research fluency
Write out STAR answers for the questions listed above. For metrics and data questions, be ready to talk about specific tools you have used (Mixpanel, Amplitude, Hotjar, or SQL basics) and a real example of a data-driven design decision you made.

Day before the interview
Review Navi's recent product announcements or any press coverage you can find. Prepare two or three thoughtful questions for the interviewer, focused on design process, team structure, or product direction. Questions signal genuine interest and give you useful signal on culture fit.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Saying 'we' instead of 'I'. Interviewers want to know what you specifically contributed. Always separate your role from the team's work, even if the project was fully collaborative.

Skipping problem framing. Jumping straight to solutions in a design challenge without stating the problem and the user is one of the fastest ways to lose points. Spend the first few minutes clarifying before you open Figma or start sketching.

Generic design-thinking answers. Saying 'I always put the user first' with no specific example reads as hollow. Every answer needs a real story with a real outcome.

Ignoring constraints. Designs that ignore engineering feasibility, business timelines, or regulatory requirements (especially relevant in fintech) signal that you are not ready for a fast-moving team.

Portfolio without outcomes. If your case studies only show beautiful screens and no results, interviewers have no way to assess your impact. Add a 'what happened after we shipped' section to every case study, even if the outcome is qualitative.

Designing only for metro users. If every example in your portfolio assumes a high-digital-literacy professional with fast internet, you may be misaligned with Navi's actual user base. Show that you have thought about Tier 2 and Tier 3 users.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Navi Product Designer interview typically have?

Candidates report three to five rounds. This typically includes a portfolio review call, a design challenge (take-home or live), and one or two rounds with senior designers or product stakeholders. Some candidates also mention a culture or values conversation. Exact structure can vary by team and role level, so confirm the process at the start of your first round.

What kind of design challenge does Navi typically give?

Candidates describe either a take-home assignment (redesign an existing fintech flow or solve a new problem, typically submitted within a day or two) or a live round where you think through a design problem with the interviewer. In either format, candidates report that framing the problem clearly and defining success metrics matters as much as the final design. Thinking process is weighted more heavily than visual polish.

What salary can a Product Designer expect at Navi?

Based on knok jobradar data, Product Designer salaries in India broadly range from 6-12 LPA at entry level (0-2 years experience) up to 36-55+ LPA at Lead or Principal level. For mid-level designers (3-5 years), the commonly cited band is 14-24 LPA, and for senior designers (6-9 years) it is 26-40 LPA. Navi-specific compensation may vary based on product area and leveling, so check Glassdoor or levels.fyi for Navi-specific data points before you negotiate.

Does Navi value visual design skills or product thinking more?

Candidates report that Navi interviewers care about both, but product thinking tends to be weighted more heavily for mid-to-senior roles. They want to see that you can identify the right problem and measure success, not just deliver polished screens. That said, for consumer-facing product roles, visual quality and interaction detail are still table stakes. Show both clearly in your portfolio.

Is fintech experience required to get a Product Designer role at Navi?

Fintech experience is a plus, but candidates without it do get hired, especially at junior and mid levels. What matters more is demonstrating an understanding of user trust, regulatory constraints, and designing for users with low digital literacy or financial anxiety. If your background is in e-commerce, health tech, or any high-stakes consumer product, draw clear parallels to Navi's user and business context.

Is there room to negotiate the offer at Navi?

Most companies in India, including fintech startups, expect some negotiation. Candidates report that having a competing offer or a clear market benchmark from Glassdoor or levels.fyi gives you the strongest position. Make sure to negotiate the full package: base salary, ESOPs if any, health cover, and joining bonus. First offers are rarely final offers, so ask politely and with data.
