01 Overview

Overview

Keka HR is one of India's well-known HR-tech SaaS companies, handling payroll, attendance, and people operations for thousands of businesses. Because the platform stores sensitive employee records including salary details, the Security Engineer role here carries real weight. As of July 2026, Keka has 3 open Security Engineer positions, so each hire decision matters.

Candidates report the process typically involves two to four rounds: an initial screening call, one or two technical rounds covering application security and cloud infrastructure, and a final conversation about approach and culture fit. There are no publicly confirmed round names, so treat this as a rough guide.

Across India, knok jobradar tracked 628 Security Engineer openings as of July 2026, with Bangalore leading at 69 roles. Keka is headquartered in Hyderabad, so both cities are worth watching if you are open to relocation.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates who have interviewed at Keka and similar HR-tech companies report questions in these broad areas:

  1. Walk us through a real vulnerability you found and fixed from discovery to closure.
  2. How do you secure a SaaS product that stores sensitive payroll and employee data?
  3. Explain the OWASP Top 10. Which three do you see most often in web apps, and how do you remediate them?
  4. How would you set up a security review gate before a new feature ships to production?
  5. What does your cloud security checklist look like for AWS or Azure? Walk us through the first things you lock down.
  6. How do you handle engineering pushback when a team resists a security control?
  7. Describe how you prioritise a backlog of open vulnerabilities when you cannot fix everything at once.
  8. How would you build a security awareness programme for a growing tech company?
  9. Explain the difference between authentication and authorisation, and give a real example of a flaw in each.
  10. How do you stay updated on new CVEs and threat intelligence as part of your regular work?
  11. How would you approach data protection compliance for a product that holds personal employment records, including salary details?
  12. A critical zero-day is found in a third-party library your product depends on. Walk us through what you do on the first day.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR structure (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for every story-based answer.

Q: Tell us about a significant vulnerability you discovered and remediated.

*Situation:* During a routine code review at my previous company, I spotted an endpoint in our HR portal that returned full employee records including salary details without checking whether the logged-in user had permission to view them.

*Task:* I needed to assess the blast radius, fix the flaw, and verify it had not already been exploited, all without disrupting the live service.

*Action:* I documented the issue privately and looped in the engineering lead and my manager the same day. I pulled access logs for the past several months to check for unusual query patterns. I then wrote a patch enforcing role-based access checks on every call to that endpoint, added automated tests to cover the authorisation logic, and coordinated a hotfix deployment.

*Result:* Logs showed no evidence of exploitation. The fix shipped within two days. I also used the incident to propose a lightweight pre-PR security checklist the team adopted, which caught two similar issues in the following quarter.

---

Q: How do you handle pushback from an engineering team on a security control?

*Situation:* I recommended enforcing MFA for all internal admin tools at a previous employer. The engineering manager said it would slow down on-call engineers during incidents.

*Task:* I had to find a way to get the security control in place without creating real friction for the team.

*Action:* I sat down with the on-call lead and mapped the exact flows where MFA felt painful. We agreed on a phased rollout: MFA enforced immediately for production database access, with a 'remember this device for several hours' option for tools engineers used many times a day. I documented the risk trade-off and got sign-off from the security lead.

*Result:* MFA rolled out without a single escalation. A few months later we extended it to all internal tools, and by then the team was used to it and raised no objections.

---

Q: Describe how you prioritise a backlog of open security findings.

*Situation:* When I joined my previous team, there were dozens of open findings in our vulnerability tracker with no clear prioritisation system.

*Task:* I needed to build a system the team would actually use, not just a scoring spreadsheet that gathered dust.

*Action:* I introduced a simple triage framework combining CVSS severity with business context: asset sensitivity (customer-facing or internal?), ease of exploitation, and whether a compensating control already existed. I ran a session with engineering leads to score the top findings together so the framework felt shared, not imposed.

*Result:* Within a few months the team cleared the top critical and high findings, and the backlog dropped by roughly half. We had a repeatable process the next engineer could pick up without needing me to explain it.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For technical 'how would you' questions: Anchor your answer in a real scenario from your experience, even if you then generalise it. Interviewers at product companies want to know you have actually done this, not just read about it.

For vulnerability and incident questions: Follow a clear arc: how you found it, how you assessed severity, what you did, and what you improved afterward. Skip jargon-heavy tool names unless the interviewer asks. Focus on decision-making.

For compliance and data protection questions: Show you understand the controls behind privacy regulations and information security management principles, not just the acronym. Explain why a control matters in terms of what harm it prevents. For a company like Keka, tie your answer to the specific sensitivity of payroll and HR data.

For 'how do you work with engineers' questions: These are as much about communication as security. Show that you understand developers are not your adversaries. Use examples where you made the secure path the easy path.

For staying-current questions: Be specific. Name the feeds, communities, or practices you actually use. A vague 'I read blogs' answer signals shallow engagement.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Deep practical knowledge, not just certifications. Keka is a product company moving fast. Interviewers want to see you can operate independently, find real problems, and fix them without hand-holding.

Business context awareness. The data Keka handles is highly sensitive: employee salaries, personal details, and attendance records. Candidates who connect security decisions to business risk and user trust stand out from those who discuss only technical controls.

Communication and influence skills. Security engineers at product companies spend a significant part of their time convincing others. If your answers show you can explain risk clearly to a non-technical stakeholder, that matters.

Ownership mindset. Candidates report that interviewers probe whether you see a finding through to closure, not just whether you can spot it. Examples where you drove a fix, ran a postmortem, or built a lasting process carry more weight than examples where you filed a ticket and moved on.

Comfort with ambiguity. Smaller security teams in growing companies rarely have fully defined playbooks. Showing you can build process from scratch, or adapt when priorities shift, signals readiness for this environment.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Understand the product and threat model first.

Use Keka HR as a real user if you can get access. Understand what data the platform handles, how authentication works, and where sensitive information flows. Think about what an attacker would target in an HR SaaS product. This context will make your answers specific and credible.

Refresh your core technical areas.

Review the OWASP Top 10 and be ready to give real examples for at least a few of them. Practice explaining common web vulnerabilities (broken access control, injection, insecure direct object references) in plain terms. Brush up on cloud security basics for AWS or Azure, particularly IAM, network segmentation, and secrets management.

Build your story bank.

Write down several stories from your own experience that cover: a vulnerability you found, a time you worked through conflict with engineering, a process you built or improved, and a time you responded to an incident. Structure each story with STAR before the interview so you are not improvising under pressure.

Prepare for compliance and data protection questions.

For a company processing payroll data, expect at least one question on India's data protection regulations. Know the basics of what DPDPA requires and how security controls map to it. You do not need to be a lawyer, but you should be able to speak to data minimisation, access controls, and breach notification in plain language.

Day before: logistics and mindset.

Review Keka's recent product announcements or current job descriptions to pick up active priorities. Prepare two or three specific questions to ask the interviewer about the team's current challenges. This signals genuine interest and helps you assess fit.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Talking about tools instead of thinking. Listing every scanner or SIEM you have used is not a substitute for explaining how you decided what to do with the output. Interviewers want to see your reasoning.

Generic answers for a product-specific role. Saying 'I would implement strong access controls' without connecting it to the specific risks of an HR SaaS platform misses the mark. Tailor your answers to what Keka actually does.

Ignoring the business side. Security engineers who frame every answer only in technical terms often struggle in product-company interviews. Show that you weigh risk against business impact.

Not knowing your own resume deeply. Candidates sometimes list certifications or tools they cannot speak to in depth. If it is on your resume, be ready to go deep on it.

Skipping the 'what changed afterward' part of stories. Stopping your answer at 'I fixed the bug' leaves impact on the table. Interviewers at growing companies want to know what you built or improved so the problem would not recur.

Underselling soft skills. In a small security team, your ability to communicate risk, influence without authority, and work well with product and engineering is just as important as your technical depth. Do not save this for the end as an afterthought.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Keka HR Security Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically involves two to four rounds. This usually includes an initial HR or recruiter screening, one or two technical rounds focused on security fundamentals and practical scenarios, and a final round covering approach, culture fit, and sometimes compensation. Round structures can vary, so confirm the process directly with your recruiter before you begin preparing.

Does Keka HR ask coding questions in security engineer interviews?

Candidates report that coding is not always a major focus, but some technical rounds include scripting tasks, such as writing a small Python script to parse logs or automate a security check. Brushing up on basic scripting and being able to explain your code clearly is worthwhile. The emphasis tends to be on security thinking rather than algorithm-style competitive coding.

What compliance knowledge does Keka HR expect from security candidates?

Given that Keka processes employee and payroll data, interviewers typically probe for familiarity with India's data protection regulations, particularly DPDPA, and general information security management principles. You do not need deep legal expertise, but you should understand how controls like access management, data minimisation, and audit logging connect to regulatory requirements. Framing your answers around protecting the actual people whose data is at risk tends to land well.

Is prior HR-tech or SaaS experience required for this role?

It is not typically listed as a hard requirement, but candidates who demonstrate awareness of the specific risks in HR SaaS products stand out. Think about the sensitivity of payroll data, multi-tenant architecture, and the consequences of a breach for thousands of small businesses and their employees. Showing this context in your answers matters more than having a specific industry background.
