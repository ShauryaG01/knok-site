01 Overview

Overview

EXL Service is a global analytics and digital solutions company known for work in data engineering, cloud operations, and AI-driven business transformation. Cloud Engineers at EXL typically support clients in BFSI, healthcare, and insurance sectors, which means interviews test both technical depth and your ability to explain solutions clearly to non-technical stakeholders.

As of July 2026, knok jobradar shows 102 Cloud Engineer openings across India, with EXL carrying 398 open roles company-wide. Positions are concentrated in Bangalore and Delhi (13 each), followed by Hyderabad (6), Pune (5), and Chennai (2).

Candidates report the process typically spans a few rounds: an HR screen, one or two technical discussions covering cloud architecture and hands-on tools, and a final managerial conversation. Strong candidates come prepared with concrete project examples, cost and availability trade-offs, and awareness of compliance requirements relevant to EXL's client industries.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly in EXL Cloud Engineer interviews, based on candidate reports and the nature of EXL's client work:

  1. Walk me through a cloud architecture you designed from scratch. What were your key decisions?
  2. How do you approach cost optimisation for a cloud workload running at scale?
  3. Explain the difference between IaaS, PaaS, and SaaS with an example from your own experience.
  4. How do you handle cloud security for a client that deals with sensitive financial or health data?
  5. Describe your experience with infrastructure-as-code tools such as Terraform or AWS CloudFormation.
  6. How would you migrate an on-premise application to the cloud with minimal downtime for end users?
  7. What is your approach to monitoring, alerting, and incident response in a cloud environment?
  8. How do you manage deployments across multi-cloud or hybrid cloud setups?
  9. EXL works with BFSI and healthcare clients. How would you design a cloud setup that meets regulatory requirements like RBI guidelines or HIPAA?
  10. Tell me about a time a cloud service failed in production. How did you detect it and what did you do?
  11. How do you design for high availability and disaster recovery for a business-critical workload?
  12. What CI/CD tools have you worked with, and how do they connect to your cloud deployment pipeline?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk me through a cloud architecture you designed from scratch.

*Situation and Task:* My previous team ran a monolithic analytics application on a single on-premise server that hit memory limits every month-end. I was asked to redesign it on AWS so it could scale automatically without manual intervention.

*Action:* I broke the application into compute and storage layers. I moved data ingestion jobs to AWS Lambda with SQS queues for buffering, shifted storage to S3 with lifecycle policies, and used RDS Aurora for transactional queries. I wrote the full infrastructure in Terraform so the team could reproduce any environment on demand. I also set up CloudWatch dashboards and alarms for CPU, queue depth, and error rates.

*Result:* Month-end reporting jobs that previously caused outages completed without manual intervention after the migration. The team could spin up a staging environment on demand, which sped up testing considerably.

---

Q: Tell me about a time a cloud service failed in production. How did you respond?

*Situation and Task:* We had an RDS instance in a single Availability Zone supporting a client dashboard used during business hours. When the AZ experienced a disruption, the dashboard went down and I was the on-call engineer responsible for restoring service and preventing recurrence.

*Action:* I checked CloudWatch metrics and AWS Health to confirm the AZ issue. I promoted a read replica in a different AZ as the new primary, updated the connection string in Secrets Manager, and restarted the application tier. While notifying the client, I drafted a post-incident plan: enable Multi-AZ on the RDS instance, add automated failover tests to the runbook, and set up status-page notifications for similar events.

*Result:* Service was restored within the incident window. The Multi-AZ change went live the following week, and the team adopted the runbook for future on-call rotations.

---

Q: How do you approach cost optimisation for a cloud workload?

*Situation and Task:* A data processing pipeline at my previous company ran EC2 instances around the clock even though jobs only ran during business hours. I was asked to reduce the cloud spend without affecting job completion times.

*Action:* I analysed usage patterns using AWS Cost Explorer and identified that compute was idle for a large portion of each day. I moved batch jobs to Spot Instances with a fallback to On-Demand, scheduled auto-scaling to bring instances up before jobs started and terminate them afterwards, and right-sized instance types based on actual CPU and memory metrics. I also enabled S3 Intelligent-Tiering for data accessed infrequently after processing.

*Result:* The monthly compute bill for the pipeline dropped noticeably by the next billing cycle. The pattern was documented and reused for other pipelines the team owned.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use the STAR structure for every behavioural and situational question. Keep the Situation and Task portions brief, just enough context for the interviewer to follow your story. Spend most of your time on the Action, because that is where you demonstrate your skills and judgment.

Quantify outcomes wherever you can. Even a rough description of impact ('cut deployment time significantly', 'reduced incidents considerably', 'adopted across all projects') is stronger than a vague 'it went well'. If you have exact figures from your experience, use them. If not, describe the direction and scale of the change.

For technical questions, think out loud. EXL interviewers typically want to see your reasoning process, not just the final answer. State your assumptions, name the trade-offs (cost vs. availability, simplicity vs. flexibility), and explain why you chose one approach over another.

Tie answers to client impact. EXL's business is built on client outcomes. Where possible, connect your technical decisions to what they meant for the client or end users: reliability, compliance, faster delivery, or lower cost.

Prepare a 'walk me through your resume' answer. Candidates report this is almost always the opening question. Practice a clear, focused version that highlights cloud-specific projects and skips unrelated roles.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

EXL Cloud Engineer interviewers typically look for a few specific qualities beyond technical knowledge:

Hands-on experience with real cloud platforms. Theoretical knowledge matters less than evidence that you have actually built, deployed, or troubleshot something on AWS, Azure, or GCP. Be ready to name the specific services you used and explain why you chose them.

Cost and compliance awareness. Because EXL's clients are often in regulated industries, interviewers want to see that you think about security, data residency, and compliance as part of your design process, not as an afterthought.

Clear communication. Cloud Engineers at EXL often present solutions to client teams. Interviewers assess whether you can explain a technical decision in plain terms without jargon.

Ownership mindset. Stories where you identified a problem proactively, drove a fix end-to-end, or improved a process without being asked tend to stand out. EXL values engineers who take initiative on client engagements.

Collaboration and documentation habits. Candidates who mention writing runbooks, onboarding teammates, or conducting knowledge-transfer sessions signal that they think beyond their own output.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Start with your own project inventory. List every cloud project you have worked on and note the services involved, the problems you solved, and the results. These become your raw material for every interview question.

Brush up on core services for your primary cloud platform. For AWS: EC2, S3, RDS, Lambda, VPC, IAM, CloudWatch, and Cost Explorer. For Azure: Virtual Machines, Blob Storage, Azure SQL, Functions, VNets, and Monitor. Know how these services interact and when you would choose one over another.

Practice infrastructure-as-code. Terraform is widely used across Indian cloud teams. Review how to write modules, manage state, and handle environment-specific configurations. Be ready to talk through a Terraform project you have built.

Read up on EXL's industries. Spend time understanding the cloud compliance landscape for BFSI (RBI guidelines, data localisation) and healthcare (HIPAA-equivalent standards in India). Knowing these frameworks shows you understand EXL's client context.

Do a mock system design session. Pick a scenario such as 'design a data ingestion pipeline for a bank' and talk through your architecture choices out loud. Record yourself if possible and listen for filler words or vague answers.

Prepare questions to ask the interviewer. Asking about the team's cloud platform mix, typical client engagement structure, or current technical challenges shows genuine interest and helps you assess fit.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Giving textbook definitions instead of project examples. Saying 'Auto Scaling adjusts capacity based on demand' is not an answer. Describe a time you configured it, what triggered the scaling policy, and what happened as a result.

Skipping the 'why'. Naming a service or tool is only half the answer. Interviewers want to know why you chose it over alternatives. Always follow 'I used X' with 'because Y was a constraint and X handled that better than Z.'

Ignoring cost and security in architecture questions. A design that only discusses compute and storage but says nothing about IAM roles, encryption, or cost estimates will feel incomplete to an EXL interviewer.

Being vague about results. Answers that end with 'it worked out' or 'the client was happy' miss an opportunity. Even a rough description of improvement ('deployments went from manual to automated', 'on-call incidents dropped noticeably') is much stronger.

Not researching EXL's domain. Candidates who are unaware that EXL's cloud work is often tied to analytics and data platforms for regulated industries can struggle with contextual questions about compliance or data governance.

Talking over the interviewer. If a question is unclear, ask a clarifying question before answering. This is especially important for system design questions where the scope can shift significantly depending on the interviewer's intent.

Which cloud platform does EXL focus on most, AWS or Azure?
