01 Overview

Overview

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly in knit PM interviews, based on candidate reports and the company's publicly stated focus on user research and AI-driven insights.

  1. Walk me through a product you use daily that handles qualitative data well. What would you improve?
  2. How would you prioritise features if you had a large backlog of user requests and limited engineering bandwidth?
  3. knit's core value is making user research faster for product teams. How would you grow adoption among teams that already use spreadsheets for feedback?
  4. Tell me about a time you had to kill a feature you personally believed in. How did you make that call?
  5. How do you measure success for a B2B SaaS product where the buyer and the end user are different people?
  6. Describe how you would design a feedback-tagging feature that learns from user corrections.
  7. A large enterprise client says your product is too complex for their team. How do you investigate and respond?
  8. How would you approach launching knit in a new vertical, say healthcare or edtech?
  9. Walk me through how you have worked with engineers to scope down a feature without losing its core value.
  10. What metrics would you track to know if knit's AI-generated summary feature is actually useful to PMs?
  11. How do you balance short-term revenue asks from sales with long-term product vision?
  12. Tell me about a time you used qualitative research to change a product decision that data alone would not have supported.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for all behavioural questions. Here are three examples tailored to themes knit typically explores.

Q: Tell me about a time you used qualitative research to change a product direction.

*Situation:* At my previous company, our analytics showed a significant drop-off at step three of onboarding, but the quantitative data did not tell us why.

*Task:* My job was to diagnose the problem and recommend a fix within one sprint cycle.

*Action:* I ran several user interviews over a few days, focusing on first-time users. I found that users did not understand what 'connect your data source' meant in our context. I worked with the content designer to rewrite the tooltip and added a short explainer video. I also flagged a UX confusion to the design team that had been invisible in our funnel data.

*Result:* After shipping the changes, onboarding completion improved noticeably. Publicly reported benchmarks for UX copy improvements suggest double-digit gains are common in similar scenarios, and our support tickets for that step dropped sharply.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you had to prioritise ruthlessly under pressure.

*Situation:* We had a roadmap with several features promised across multiple teams, and then two engineers were pulled to a critical bug fix for a full month.

*Task:* I had to renegotiate the roadmap with stakeholders while keeping the most business-critical feature on track.

*Action:* I scored each feature using a simple impact-versus-effort matrix. I held individual conversations with each stakeholder before the group meeting so no one was surprised. I deferred roughly half the features to the next quarter with a clear written rationale, and kept the two highest-impact features in scope with a reduced spec.

*Result:* We shipped on time, stakeholders felt heard, and the deferred features actually launched faster in the next quarter because the scope was cleaner.

---

Q: Describe a time you worked with engineers to scope a feature down without losing its value.

*Situation:* We were building a bulk-export feature. The original spec would have taken many weeks of engineering time.

*Task:* Sales needed something shippable within a short window to close a deal with a key client.

*Action:* I sat with the lead engineer to identify must-have versus nice-to-have parts. We agreed to ship CSV export first, skip custom column mapping for v1, and queue the PDF version for the next release. I updated the sales team so they could set the right expectations with the client.

*Result:* The feature shipped ahead of the original estimate. The client signed, and the full feature followed in the next sprint with much better engineer confidence because v1 had real user feedback.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

The CIRCLES Method is useful for product design questions ('Design a feature for X'). It stands for: Comprehend the situation, Identify the customer, Report the customer's needs, Cut down priorities, List solutions, Evaluate trade-offs, Summarise. Use it when knit asks you to design a new module or improve an existing one.

RICE Scoring (Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort) works well for prioritisation questions. Assign a rough score to each factor, divide (Reach x Impact x Confidence) by Effort, and rank. When knit asks how you would choose between features, walking through RICE shows structured thinking without requiring you to guess at hard numbers.

The Metrics Tree helps for success-measurement questions. Start with a north star metric (for knit, something like 'insights generated per team per month'), then break it into inputs: sessions created, tags applied, exports used. This shows you understand how leading indicators connect to business outcomes.

User Empathy Mapping is particularly relevant for knit because their product IS a research tool. If you can show that you use empathy maps yourself, not just in theory, you demonstrate you live the product's value proposition.

The 'So What' Test: After every answer, ask yourself whether your result actually mattered. knit interviewers typically push candidates to quantify or qualify impact. If you cannot cite a specific number, describe what changed behaviourally for the user or the business.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

knit PM interviewers, based on candidate reports and their public job descriptions, look for a few specific qualities.

Deep user empathy, not just process. Because knit's product is a research tool, they want PMs who genuinely believe in talking to users, not just scheduling research as a checkbox. Show that you have personally run interviews, not just commissioned them.

Comfort with ambiguity in qualitative data. Unlike metrics-heavy consumer apps, knit deals in open-ended feedback. Interviewers want to see that you can synthesise themes from messy, unstructured input and turn them into a clear product decision.

B2B and enterprise intuition. knit sells to product teams inside larger companies. They want PMs who understand the buyer-user split: the person who signs the contract is often not the person using the tool daily. Show you have navigated this dynamic in past roles.

Cross-functional maturity. Expect scenarios where engineering, design, and sales are pulling in different directions. Interviewers want evidence that you can hold a clear product vision while being genuinely collaborative, not just diplomatic.

Intellectual honesty. knit is a growth-stage company. They typically value candidates who say 'I don't know, but here is how I would find out' over those who bluff through knowledge gaps.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Build your foundation.
Use knit's product yourself if a trial or demo is available. Write down one thing you found intuitive and one thing you would change. Read their blog, LinkedIn posts, and any founder interviews available in plain text. Map out their core user journey from the perspective of a product team using it for the first time.

Week 2: Practice case questions.
Pick a few questions from the list above and answer them out loud, timing yourself. Record your answers on your phone and listen back. Focus on whether your 'Result' section actually answers 'so what'. Practice the RICE framework on a real prioritisation problem from your past work.

Week 3: Sharpen your story bank.
Prepare a set of STAR stories covering: a feature you killed, a time you used data to change your mind, a conflict you resolved, a launch that did not go as planned, and a time you influenced without authority. Each story should take a couple of minutes to tell cleanly.

Week 4 (final prep). Do a mock interview with a peer or mentor. Practise asking clarifying questions before jumping into answers. Prepare a few thoughtful questions for your interviewer about knit's roadmap, their biggest PM challenge, and how success is measured in the role.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping to solutions before understanding the problem. Many candidates hear a product question and immediately start listing features. knit interviewers typically want you to first ask: who is the user, what are they trying to do, and what is the biggest pain point. Take a moment to think out loud before pitching anything.

Treating metrics as the only valid answer. knit's product is built around qualitative insight. If every answer you give leads back to 'I would run an A/B test', you are missing the point. Show that you value user interviews and behavioural observation alongside quantitative data.

Vague results in STAR stories. 'The product improved significantly' is not a result. Say what changed: churn went down, support tickets dropped, a deal closed, a team stopped using a workaround. If you cannot share a specific number, describe the behavioural change clearly.

Not knowing knit's product. Candidates who have never seen the product, even through a demo video or screenshots, stand out negatively. Spend some time understanding what knit does and who their users are before your first round.

Overcomplicating frameworks. Naming CIRCLES or RICE is fine, but walking through every step for a simple question signals process over judgement. Use frameworks as scaffolding, not as the answer itself.

Asking no questions at the end. Candidates who say 'I think you have covered everything' signal low curiosity. Prepare genuine questions about the team, the product direction, or how the company measures PM success.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the knit PM interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically runs 3-5 rounds. These usually include an initial HR or recruiter screen, a take-home product case or live case discussion, one or two rounds with senior PMs or founders, and a final culture or leadership fit conversation. knit has not published an official round structure publicly, so treat this as a general pattern rather than a guarantee.

What salary can I expect for a PM role at knit?

Based on knok jobradar data for Product Manager roles in India, Associate PM roles pay in the 12-20 LPA range, PM roles at the 3-6 year level pay 24-40 LPA, and Senior PM roles pay 40-60 LPA. Actual offers at knit depend on your years of experience, the seniority of the specific role, and how well you negotiate. Glassdoor and levels.fyi can give you additional data points for B2B SaaS companies of similar size and stage.

Does knit give a take-home assignment?

Candidates report that a take-home product case or live case discussion is commonly part of the process, though the format can vary by role and hiring manager. Typical assignments ask you to analyse a user problem, propose a solution, and outline how you would measure success. Treat take-home work as seriously as a live round, since it often carries significant weight in the final decision.

What kind of PM experience does knit value most?

Based on their job descriptions and candidate reports, knit values experience in B2B SaaS, comfort with user research and qualitative data, and the ability to work closely with both enterprise clients and engineering teams. Experience with AI or data-driven products is a plus, as is any background building tools that product or research teams use daily. Consumer PM experience can translate if you frame it around user empathy and structured experimentation.

Should I prepare for technical questions in the knit PM interview?

Technical depth is not typically the focus, but candidates report being asked about how AI features work at a conceptual level, given that knit's product involves machine-generated insights. You do not need to code, but understanding concepts like NLP, tagging models, or how AI suggestions are trained will help you sound credible. Focus more on product judgment and research thinking than on engineering fundamentals.
