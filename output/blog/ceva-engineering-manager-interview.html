01 Overview

Overview

Ceva is a semiconductor IP company specialising in wireless connectivity, signal processing, and AI-powered edge computing. Their IP cores power billions of connected devices across IoT, 5G, and consumer electronics. 02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These are the questions candidates most commonly report from Ceva Engineering Manager interviews. Use them to anchor your preparation.

  1. Tell me about yourself and your engineering leadership journey. Ceva wants to understand how you moved from individual contributor to manager and what shaped your leadership style.
  1. How do you handle underperforming engineers on your team? This tests your coaching ability and willingness to give hard feedback early rather than letting problems fester.
  1. Describe a time you drove a technically complex project to delivery under tight constraints. Ceva ships IP cores on fixed chipset schedules, so delivery discipline matters enormously.
  1. How do you balance technical debt against feature velocity? Expect follow-up questions on how you communicate that trade-off to product stakeholders.
  1. What is your approach to building and retaining strong engineering talent? Hiring and retention are key concerns in semiconductor IP, where domain expertise is scarce.
  1. How have you collaborated with cross-functional teams (hardware, firmware, product) to align on a shared goal? Ceva's work spans hardware and software, so cross-functional fluency is essential.
  1. Describe a situation where your team had a critical production or silicon bug. How did you respond? This probes your crisis management and communication skills under pressure.
  1. How do you set technical direction for a team while staying close enough to the code to make good decisions? Ceva values EMs who remain technically grounded, not just process managers.
  1. Give an example of a time you had to influence a decision without direct authority. Relevant for working with hardware partners or third-party IP vendors.
  1. How do you define and track engineering productivity and quality? Expect a conversation about metrics philosophy, not just tooling choices.
  1. What is your experience managing distributed or multi-site engineering teams? Ceva operates across multiple geographies, so this question comes up frequently.
  1. Where do you see semiconductor IP and edge AI heading over the next few years, and how does that shape your team priorities? A strategic question to test domain awareness and intellectual curiosity.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a situation where your team had a critical bug. How did you respond?

*Situation:* Our team shipped a firmware update for a wireless connectivity module and discovered a handshake failure affecting a subset of devices in a specific RF environment.

*Task:* I had to coordinate a fix across firmware, hardware validation, and the customer success team, while keeping leadership informed without causing panic.

*Action:* I immediately called a cross-functional war room, assigned a dedicated investigator to root-cause analysis, and set up a communication cadence so stakeholders had updates at regular checkpoints rather than silence. I shielded the team from escalation noise so engineers could focus. We isolated the affected configurations quickly and prepared a patched firmware build alongside a workaround for customers already in the field.

*Result:* The issue was contained and a patch shipped within the same sprint. Customer escalations dropped, and the incident led us to add automated RF-environment regression tests to our CI pipeline, preventing a repeat.

---

Q: How do you handle an underperforming engineer?

*Situation:* A senior engineer on my team was consistently missing deadlines and producing code that required heavy review cycles. The rest of the team was quietly absorbing the extra load.

*Task:* I needed to address the performance issue fairly, protect team morale, and either help the engineer improve or make a difficult people decision.

*Action:* I started with a private, honest conversation focused on specific observations rather than judgements. Together we identified that the engineer was unclear on priorities and had a skills gap in a new domain we had moved into. I set up a structured improvement plan with clear milestones, paired them with a senior peer for knowledge transfer, and checked in weekly. I was transparent that continued misses would require escalation.

*Result:* The engineer improved measurably over the following months and went on to own a module independently. The team saw that I handled the situation with fairness and directness, which strengthened trust across the group.

---

Q: Give an example of influencing a decision without direct authority.

*Situation:* Our team wanted to adopt a new hardware abstraction layer approach, but the architecture decision sat with a central platform team that had already committed to a legacy approach.

*Task:* I needed to shift that decision without formal authority, budget, or the ability to mandate a change.

*Action:* I put together a concise analysis comparing the two approaches on integration effort, long-term maintainability, and risk to upcoming tape-out timelines. I shared it informally with key platform engineers first to gather feedback, then requested a working session with the platform team lead. Instead of arguing against their choice, I framed it as 'here is what our team's data shows, and here is how we could pilot this on a low-risk module to validate.' I also brought in a customer reference where the newer approach had worked well.

*Result:* The platform team agreed to a scoped pilot. The pilot succeeded and the new approach was adopted for the next product generation, reducing integration effort across teams.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions. Every behavioural question at Ceva is best answered with a tight Situation, Task, Action, Result structure. Keep Situation and Task brief (two to three sentences combined) so you have room to go deep on Action and Result. Results should be concrete wherever possible, but if you cannot share exact figures due to NDA, describe the qualitative outcome clearly: 'the escalation rate dropped noticeably' or 'the team shipped on schedule for the first time in three cycles.'

The 'Stay Technical' framework. Ceva Engineering Managers are expected to be technically credible. When answering questions about technical direction or debt, use this pattern: (1) state the business context driving the technical decision, (2) describe the options you evaluated, (3) explain how you involved senior engineers in the final call, (4) describe the outcome. This shows you lead engineers rather than override them.

The 'Metrics and Meaning' framework. When asked about productivity or quality metrics, avoid listing tools as your answer. Instead, explain the outcome you were trying to measure and why, the metric you chose, how you collected it, and what decision it drove. This positions you as someone who uses data purposefully rather than tracking for tracking's sake.

Stakeholder communication. For cross-functional or escalation questions, use the 'Inform, Protect, Resolve' pattern: describe how you kept stakeholders informed at the right cadence, how you protected your team from distraction, and how you drove resolution. This maps well to Ceva's multi-site, hardware-software environment.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates typically report, Ceva Engineering Manager interviewers are looking for a specific combination of qualities.

Technical credibility without micromanaging. Ceva ships complex semiconductor IP, so interviewers want to see that you understand the technical domain deeply enough to make sound architectural trade-offs, but that you empower your engineers rather than owning every design decision yourself.

Delivery discipline. Semiconductor product cycles tie to foundry and tape-out schedules that cannot slip. Interviewers probe whether you have instilled a culture of predictable delivery on your teams, and whether you can manage scope and risk without sacrificing quality.

People-first leadership. Domain expertise in wireless and signal processing is rare, so retention matters. Interviewers listen for evidence that you invest in your team's growth, give honest feedback, and build psychological safety.

Cross-functional communication. Ceva's EMs work across hardware, firmware, software, and customer-facing teams. Interviewers look for evidence that you can translate between these worlds without losing either audience.

Intellectual curiosity about the domain. Candidates who have clearly thought about trends in edge AI, 5G, and IoT connectivity make a stronger impression. You do not need to be a researcher, but showing genuine interest in where the technology is heading signals long-term fit.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week one: know the company. Read Ceva's recent product announcements, earnings calls, and any public engineering blog posts. Understand their core product lines: DSP IP, Wi-Fi and Bluetooth IP, and AI/ML inference IP. Form a point of view on how these connect to market trends in edge AI and connected devices.

Week one: audit your own stories. Map at least two strong STAR stories to each of these themes: delivery under pressure, underperformer management, cross-functional influence, technical direction-setting, and team building. Write them out fully so you can compress or expand in the room.

Week two: practise out loud. Behavioural interviews feel very different when spoken versus written. Practise with a peer or record yourself. Focus on keeping the Situation and Task concise and letting the Action carry the weight.

Week two: prepare your questions. Ceva typically gives candidates time to ask questions. Good questions show strategic thinking: ask about the team's current technical challenges, how engineering and product roadmaps are prioritised, or how success is measured for this EM role in the early months.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Talking about leadership in the abstract. Saying 'I believe in empowering my team' without a specific example leaves interviewers unconvinced. Every leadership claim needs a story behind it.

Going too deep on individual contribution. EM candidates sometimes revert to describing their own code or designs in detail. Interviewers want to know how you led the team to the outcome, not what you personally built.

Ignoring the hardware-software context. Ceva is not a pure software company. Candidates who frame every answer in a web or mobile software context without acknowledging the constraints of hardware-coupled development can seem like a poor fit.

Underselling retention and hiring. In a niche domain like semiconductor IP, finding and keeping engineers is a genuine business problem. Candidates who treat talent management as a side topic miss an opportunity to show strategic awareness.

Vague results in STAR answers. 'The project was successful' is weak. Even if you cannot share exact metrics, describe the outcome concretely: 'We shipped on schedule for the first time in three cycles' or 'the escalation rate from the customer dropped significantly in the quarter after the fix.'

Not asking questions. Candidates who have no questions signal low engagement. Prepare at least three thoughtful questions and listen actively in case some get answered during the conversation.

What should I focus on if I am coming from a pure software background applying to Ceva?

Ceva's core business is semiconductor IP, so hardware-software co-design, firmware, and silicon-level constraints come up frequently.
