02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These 12 questions appear repeatedly in candidate reports for Engineering Manager interviews at Instacart. Preparing by theme is more efficient than going question by question.

People and performance

  1. Tell me about a time you turned around an underperforming engineer or team.
  2. How do you approach hiring for a senior engineering role? What do you look for beyond technical skill?
  3. How do you keep engineers motivated through a long reliability or tech-debt cycle with few visible wins?

Cross-functional alignment

  1. Describe a project where shoppers and customers had competing needs. How did you align the engineering approach?
  2. How have you handled disagreements with a product manager over roadmap priorities?
  3. How do you run a roadmap planning cycle and balance business asks against engineering capacity?

Technical leadership

  1. Walk me through a major production incident you managed. How did you run the response and the retrospective?
  2. Describe a build-vs-buy decision you have made. What factors drove your choice?
  3. How do you stay technically credible with your team without writing production code yourself?

Delivery and trade-offs

  1. Instacart's business is seasonal: holidays and peak events drive demand spikes. How would you plan engineering capacity around them?
  2. Describe a time you cut scope under deadline pressure. How did you decide what to drop?
  3. How do you balance feature velocity with technical debt on a platform that ships daily?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for every behavioral question. These three worked examples show how to structure answers for an Instacart EM panel.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you turned around an underperforming engineer.

*Situation:* An engineer on my team was consistently missing sprint commitments over two quarters. The rest of the team was quietly absorbing the extra work, and morale was starting to slip.

*Task:* I needed to close the performance gap and give the engineer a genuine, structured chance to succeed, without burning out the rest of the team.

*Action:* I set up structured weekly 1:1s with written goals agreed between us. I paired the engineer with a senior teammate on a targeted project where the skill gap was most visible. I gave written feedback every fortnight tied to specific, measurable outcomes. I also clarified assignments that had been ambiguous, since that ambiguity had partly masked the problem.

*Result:* Within two quarters the engineer met all sprint targets and later became the primary owner of a core checkout module. Two teammates separately told me the improvement had restored their confidence in the team's culture of accountability.

---

Q: Walk me through a major production incident you managed.

*Situation:* Our order assignment service went down during a peak delivery window. A large number of active shopper sessions were affected and customer SLAs were at risk.

*Task:* As the on-call EM I had to coordinate a fast recovery while keeping leadership and cross-functional stakeholders informed throughout.

*Action:* I opened a bridge call immediately and assigned one engineer to triage and one to maintain a live timeline document. I gave the product and ops leads a short, regular update cadence so they could manage customer communications without pulling engineers off the incident. I kept the team focused on restore-first, root-cause-second. After recovery I ran a blameless retrospective and turned the action items into a tracked project with owners and deadlines.

*Result:* We restored service within the target SLA window. The retrospective surfaced three systemic gaps. All three were closed in the next sprint and the same failure mode has not recurred since.

---

Q: Describe a time you cut scope under deadline pressure.

*Situation:* We were mid-sprint on a checkout revamp when a regulatory deadline moved up by three weeks, leaving no buffer.

*Task:* I had to decide what to ship, what to defer, and how to communicate the changes without damaging trust with product and legal stakeholders.

*Action:* I mapped every in-flight work item to one of three buckets: must-ship for compliance, high-impact for the launch experience, and deferrable. I ran a short prioritisation session with the PM and legal team to agree the cut list. I was transparent with engineers about why scope was changing and made sure no one's contribution was quietly dropped without acknowledgement.

*Result:* We shipped the compliant version on the new deadline. The deferred features were sequenced into the next cycle and two of them shipped within a month. No stakeholder was surprised by what changed.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Beyond STAR, these frameworks help you structure answers consistently across different question types.

For people and performance questions: Lead with your observation method (how you spotted the issue early), then your intervention (what you specifically changed), then the outcome for the individual and for the team. Avoid framing the story as 'I fixed a broken person.' The stronger framing is 'I removed the barriers that were preventing someone from doing their best work.'

For technical trade-off questions (build vs buy, debt vs velocity): Use a three-part structure: the constraints you were operating under, the criteria you applied to make the decision, and the result including what you learned or would do differently. Interviewers want structured thinking, not a single 'correct' answer.

For cross-functional alignment questions: Name the stakeholders, describe the competing incentives at play, and specify the exact mechanism you used to reach agreement: a written document, a working session, a shared metric. Vague answers like 'I aligned everyone' carry no weight.

For incident and reliability questions: Follow a clear arc: detect, respond, restore, learn. Show that you separate the 'war room' phase (restore first) from the investigation phase (root cause second). Include how you communicated upward during the incident and what changed structurally afterward.

For goal-setting and OKR questions: Describe how you connect team goals to company-level priorities, how you involve engineers so they feel genuine ownership, and how you track progress without micromanaging.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Instacart's engineering teams run a complex, real-time logistics platform. Interviewers are typically evaluating you across five dimensions.

Ownership and judgment under pressure. Delivery windows are short and expectations are high on both sides of the marketplace. Interviewers want evidence that you make clear, reasoned decisions when things go wrong, not only when everything is running smoothly.

People development with specifics. Saying you 'care about growth' is table stakes. Bring concrete stories: an engineer you promoted, a performance issue you resolved, a team dynamic you improved. Candidates who generalise tend to score lower than those who narrate specific, verifiable situations.

Technical credibility without hands-on coding. You are not expected to solve algorithmic puzzles, but candidates report that technical depth checks do appear, typically through discussion of a system you have built or a past incident. Being able to evaluate architecture trade-offs and ask sharp questions in a design conversation matters here.

Cross-functional fluency. Engineering decisions at Instacart touch shoppers, customers, retail partners, and internal ops simultaneously. Interviewers want to see that you can hold multiple stakeholder perspectives and make principled trade-offs across them.

Structured, confident communication. Every answer is partly an assessment of whether you can represent engineering clearly in a leadership meeting. Clarity and conciseness signal readiness for the role.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused two-to-three week approach that candidates report works well at this level.

Week 1: Build your story bank

Write down eight to ten stories from your career covering: a people issue you resolved, a technical decision you owned, a cross-functional conflict you navigated, a major incident you managed, and a time you cut scope under pressure. Each story needs a concrete result. Practise narrating each one in under two minutes.

Week 2: Go deep on Instacart

Read Instacart's publicly available engineering blog posts (search 'Instacart Tech Blog'). Understand how their fulfillment and assignment systems work at a high level. Note any architecture patterns or reliability investments they have written about publicly. This gives you specific hooks to reference in your answers rather than speaking in the abstract.

Also review publicly reported or Glassdoor-shared interview experiences for Engineering Manager roles at Instacart and similar marketplace companies. Look for patterns in what interviewers probed on most.

Week 3: Practise and refine

Do at least four mock interviews covering the question themes in this guide. Record yourself or ask a peer to give feedback. Focus on trimming filler, tightening your stated results, and making the 'what I specifically did' part of each story crisper.

Prepare three to four questions for the end of each round. Strong questions for an EM role ask about how engineering and product share accountability for outcomes, how the team handles post-mortems, and what the path from Manager to Director looks like in practice at Instacart.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

These patterns come up most often in publicly reported candidate feedback for Engineering Manager loops at Instacart.

Staying too high-level. Answers that describe your general management philosophy rather than a specific situation, action, and result tend to score low. Every answer should be a real story with a named outcome.

Skipping the result. Candidates often describe good actions and then stop. Always close with what changed because of what you did. Even 'the team's delivery rate improved over the following two quarters' is far better than trailing off without an outcome.

Assuming technical questions do not apply to you. Some EM candidates prepare only for behavioral rounds. Candidates report that Instacart interviewers do probe technical depth, typically through architecture discussions or incident walkthroughs. Skipping technical preparation is a common way to fail the loop.

Generic cross-functional answers. Saying you 'worked closely with product' without explaining the specific disagreement, the mechanism you used to resolve it, and the outcome tells the interviewer nothing useful. Be precise: what document did you write, what session did you run, what decision did you unblock?

Underprepared questions for the interviewer. Interviewers notice when candidates ask generic questions that show no research into Instacart's specific platform challenges. Ask questions that reference something specific: their fulfillment architecture, their approach to reliability at peak demand, or how they think about EM career growth internally.

How many rounds does the Instacart Engineering Manager interview typically have?

Does Instacart ask coding questions for Engineering Manager roles?

Candidates report that standard algorithmic coding exercises are not typical for EM-level interviews at Instacart. However, technical depth is still assessed. Expect conversations about system design trade-offs, architecture decisions you have owned, and how you review technical work as a manager. Being able to talk credibly about distributed systems and reliability patterns is an advantage.
