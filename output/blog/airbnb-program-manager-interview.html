01 Overview

Overview

Airbnb had 242 open roles tracked on knok jobradar as of July 2026, making it one of the more actively hiring global tech companies this year. Technical Program Manager roles are among the more competitive positions at Airbnb, and the broader market shows 313 TPM openings tracked across India, with Bangalore leading at 41, followed by Delhi (14), Pune (13), Hyderabad (12), and Chennai (5).

Airbnb's TPM interview process is typically multi-round and covers program management depth, technical judgment, cross-functional leadership, and behavioral fit. Candidates report going through a recruiter screen, a hiring manager conversation, and two to four panel rounds before an offer. The company has a strong culture around belonging and community, and this shows up in the interview, not just in HR messaging.

TPMs at Airbnb are expected to own complex, ambiguous programs across product, engineering, design, legal, and operations, often spanning multiple countries and time zones. The interview bar for structured communication, data-driven thinking, and stakeholder management is high.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly in Airbnb TPM interviews, based on what candidates report:

  1. Tell me about a complex, cross-functional program you owned end to end. How did you align stakeholders who had conflicting priorities?
  2. Airbnb operates in many countries with different regulations. Describe a time you managed a program with significant compliance or legal constraints.
  3. How do you handle a situation where an engineering team tells you a key deliverable cannot meet the committed deadline?
  4. Walk me through how you build and maintain a program roadmap when multiple teams depend on it.
  5. How have you used data to identify a risk or change the direction of a program mid-flight?
  6. Airbnb's mission is about belonging. How have you actively created an inclusive environment within a program or across teams you did not directly manage?
  7. Describe a time a program you owned was at serious risk of failure. What early signals did you catch, and how did you course-correct?
  8. Tell me about a time you had to push back on a senior stakeholder or say no to scope creep. How did you handle it?
  9. How would you coordinate a market launch in a new country, working across product, engineering, legal, and marketing teams in parallel?
  10. Describe your experience managing technical debt that was slowing down program delivery.
  11. How do you manage a program during a period of organizational uncertainty, such as a reorg or a significant cost-cutting exercise?
  12. Tell me about a time you had to sunset or kill a project. How did you manage communication and stakeholder expectations?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a complex, cross-functional program you owned end to end.

*Situation:* My company was launching a payments compliance program across five countries in Asia, with legal, product, engineering, and finance all involved and operating on different timelines.

*Task:* I was the single TPM responsible for aligning all workstreams and shipping within a regulatory deadline that could not move.

*Action:* I set up a weekly cross-functional sync with clear owners for each workstream, built a shared tracker visible to all teams, and created a risk log I reviewed every day. When the engineering team flagged a three-week delay mid-program, I immediately called a scoping session with the PM and legal team, identified which features were required for regulatory compliance versus nice-to-have, and cut the non-critical items to protect the deadline.

*Result:* We shipped on time in all five countries. The risk log surfaced two other potential delays early enough to resolve them before they became blockers. Legal confirmed we met all requirements in every market.

---

Q: Describe a time a program you owned was at serious risk of failure.

*Situation:* Six weeks before a major platform migration, a dependency team told me their API changes would not be ready because of an internal reprioritization.

*Task:* I needed to protect the program timeline without damaging the relationship with the dependency team, since we would need them for future work.

*Action:* I requested an urgent meeting with the dependency team's lead and their engineering manager. I came prepared with data showing the downstream impact of their delay, a proposed interim solution using a compatibility shim, and a specific ask for a partial delivery within two weeks. I also escalated to my VP with a written summary of options and tradeoffs so leadership was not surprised.

*Result:* The team agreed to the partial delivery plan. We launched the migration on schedule using the shim, and the full API update followed three weeks later. My VP later cited this as an example of proactive risk management in a company all-hands.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you had to push back on a senior stakeholder.

*Situation:* A VP of Product wanted to add a significant new feature to a program already in final testing, two weeks before launch.

*Task:* I needed to give an honest assessment without damaging the relationship, and protect a launch timeline that three other teams were depending on.

*Action:* I prepared a one-pager showing the specific engineering work the new feature would require, the testing cycles it would affect, and the cascade impact on dependent teams. I met with the VP and walked through the tradeoffs clearly: add the feature and delay by four weeks, or ship on time and schedule the feature for the next release cycle. I came with a concrete proposal for how the feature could be prioritized in the following quarter.

*Result:* The VP agreed to defer the feature. It shipped three months later in the next release. The launch went out on time, and the VP later said she appreciated having the tradeoffs laid out clearly rather than just a 'no'.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use STAR, but keep it tight. Airbnb interviewers are evaluating multiple candidates. Keep your Situation and Task to two or three sentences, and spend most of your answer on Action and Result. If you ramble in Situation, you signal poor communication skills, which is a red flag for a TPM role.

For program management questions, make your reasoning visible. When asked how you would approach something (a launch, a risk, a conflict), open with: 'Here is how I think about this, then here is what I would do.' Airbnb values structured thinkers who can articulate their decision-making process, not just their decisions.

For cross-functional conflict questions, show empathy before action. Before describing what you did, briefly acknowledge the other team's constraints. Saying something like 'I understood they were under pressure because...' before your action shows that you lead with listening, which aligns with the belonging-first culture Airbnb promotes.

For data questions, be specific about the metric. Do not say 'I used data to decide.' Say which metric you tracked, what the number told you, and what you changed as a result. Vague data references are one of the most common weak points in TPM interviews at top-tier tech companies.

For values questions, connect naturally to Airbnb's mission. The company's mission is about creating a world where anyone can belong anywhere. When answering questions about inclusion or culture, let this connection come through in your examples, not by quoting the mission statement word-for-word.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Airbnb TPM interviewers are looking for a specific combination of qualities. Understanding these helps you frame every answer correctly.

Cross-functional ownership. They want to see that you did not just coordinate meetings. They want to hear that you identified risks others missed, resolved conflicts others avoided, and made tradeoff decisions that moved the program forward. Passive coordination is not enough.

Technical credibility. You do not need to write code, but you need to hold a room with senior engineers. Interviewers will probe whether you understand technical constraints, can evaluate engineering estimates, and know when to escalate a technical risk. Candidates who cannot engage meaningfully on technical topics typically struggle at Airbnb.

Data-driven thinking. 'We tracked success with OKRs' is too vague. Interviewers want to hear what specific metrics you defined, how you monitored them, and how the data changed your decisions. Airbnb is a metrics-driven company and expects TPMs to operate the same way.

Clarity under ambiguity. Many TPM interview questions at Airbnb are deliberately open-ended. Interviewers want to see you ask a clarifying question or two, state your assumptions, then structure your answer. Jumping straight to solutions without scoping the problem is a common way to fail these rounds.

Cultural alignment. Airbnb's values around belonging are real and interviewers notice whether they come through naturally in your answers. Candidates who give technically strong answers but never mention team health, inclusion, or bringing people along can clear the technical bar but fail the culture bar.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A structured four-week approach that candidates report works well for Airbnb TPM prep:

Week 1: Know your own story. Write out your five to seven strongest program management stories in STAR format. Make sure at least two involve cross-functional conflict, at least one involves a program that nearly failed, and at least one involves a data-driven decision that changed your direction. Practice telling each story out loud in under three minutes.

Week 2: Go deep on Airbnb. Read the company's earnings calls, product announcements, and engineering blog (search 'Airbnb tech blog' to find it). Understand how Airbnb thinks about its platform, trust and safety, and global expansion. Prepare two or three specific observations about Airbnb's programs that you can reference naturally during the interview.

Week 3: Practice the hard questions. Use the 12 questions listed in this guide and practice answering each one out loud, alone or with a peer. Record yourself if possible. The goal is to eliminate filler words and tighten your answers. Mock interviews with another TPM candidate or a peer in a similar role are especially useful here.

Week 4: Sharpen your questions for them. Prepare four or five thoughtful questions to ask your interviewers. Strong questions for Airbnb include ones like: 'How does the TPM team interface with product at the program inception stage?' or 'What does success look like for this role in the first six months?' Questions that are easily answered on the company website signal low preparation.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Confusing project management with program management. Airbnb TPMs operate at the program level: multiple workstreams, multiple teams, strategic tradeoffs. If your answers describe managing a single project with a single team, interviewers will downlevel you or pass. Always frame your answers at the cross-team, multi-stakeholder level.

Hiding behind 'we.' In cross-functional work it is easy to say 'we delivered' or 'our team built.' But interviewers are evaluating you, not your team. Be explicit: 'I identified the risk,' 'I made the call to cut scope,' 'I escalated to the VP.' This is not taking credit from your team. It is demonstrating your individual judgment.

Skipping the concrete result. Saying 'the program was successful' without a specific metric, timeline, or business outcome signals that you do not measure your own work. Always close your STAR answers with something tangible: a deadline met, a risk averted, a measurable improvement.

Not asking clarifying questions. When an interviewer gives you a broad, ambiguous scenario, jumping straight to solutions makes you look reactive rather than strategic. Spend thirty seconds asking one or two scoping questions. This signals the structured thinking Airbnb looks for in a TPM.

Ignoring the cultural dimension. Candidates who give technically strong answers but never mention team health, inclusion, or stakeholder wellbeing sometimes pass the technical bar but fail the culture bar. Weave in how you brought people along, not just what you delivered.

Over-preparing for one round type. Candidates report that Airbnb's process typically includes both technical depth rounds and behavioral rounds. If you only prep behavioral stories, a technical deep-dive can catch you off guard. Make sure you can speak in detail about the technical decisions you influenced or made in your programs.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Airbnb TPM interview process typically have?

Candidates report that the Airbnb TPM process typically includes a recruiter screen, a hiring manager conversation, and then two to four panel rounds covering program management depth, technical questions, and behavioral assessments. The exact structure varies by team and level. Airbnb occasionally adds a written component or a live program design exercise, so ask your recruiter what to expect for your specific role and level before you start preparing.

Does Airbnb ask coding questions in TPM interviews?

Typically, no. Airbnb TPM interviews focus on program management skills, cross-functional leadership, and technical judgment, not on writing code. However, candidates report being asked to evaluate technical tradeoffs, assess engineering estimates, or discuss system design concepts at a high level. You should be comfortable discussing how engineering decisions affect program timelines and risk, even if you are not expected to write code yourself.

How important are Airbnb's values in the interview?

Very important, according to consistent candidate reports. Airbnb has a genuine culture around belonging and community, and interviewers look for this in your behavioral answers. It is not enough to mention the values by name. You need to show through your stories how you actively created inclusive environments, brought in quieter voices, or designed programs that considered diverse stakeholder needs. Surface-level answers on culture questions are a common reason candidates clear the technical bar but still get rejected.

How should I prepare if I am coming from a non-FAANG background?

If your programs were genuinely complex, structure your STAR answers to make that complexity visible.
