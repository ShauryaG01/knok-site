03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe your hands-on experience with AVFoundation and how you used it in a real project.

*Situation:* At my previous company we built a voice journaling feature where users recorded short audio clips and played them back with a live waveform visualisation.

*Task:* I was responsible for the entire audio layer, from capture to playback to file storage.

*Action:* I used AVAudioEngine to set up a real-time tap on the input node, capturing PCM buffers at low latency. I wrote a background serial queue to process those buffers and compute RMS values for the live waveform display. For playback I used AVAudioPlayerNode with scheduled buffers so users could scrub through clips without loading the full file into memory.

*Result:* The feature shipped with no audio-related crashes in the first release cycle and received strong marks in user testing for responsiveness. The audio engine wrapper was later reused across other features in the app.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you significantly improved the performance of an iOS feature.

*Situation:* A home-screen feed in our app was loading slowly on older devices, and our internal analytics showed user drop-off within the first few seconds of opening the screen.

*Task:* I was asked to investigate and reduce the perceived load time.

*Action:* I profiled the screen with Instruments and found we were decoding images synchronously on the main thread and making redundant layout passes. I moved image decoding to a background queue, added a simple in-memory cache keyed by URL, and flattened the UIView hierarchy to cut layout recalculations.

*Result:* Time-to-interactive dropped noticeably on our target devices and the drop-off rate on that screen improved in our analytics within the next sprint.

---

Q: Describe a moment when you influenced a product decision as an iOS engineer.

*Situation:* Our team planned a new onboarding flow that required users to fill out a lengthy multi-step form before they could try the product.

*Task:* I had concerns that front-loading so much friction would hurt activation, but I was an engineer stepping into product territory.

*Action:* I pulled publicly available research on mobile onboarding best practices, built a quick prototype of a shorter 'try-first' flow, and presented both approaches side by side in a sprint review with a clear trade-off summary.

*Result:* The team agreed to test the shorter flow first. That prototype informed a product decision to move full sign-up to after the user had experienced the core feature, which the PM confirmed aligned with industry best practices for consumer apps.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions. Structure every 'tell me about a time' answer as Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep the Situation brief (one or two sentences) and spend the bulk of your time on Action and Result.

Think-aloud for system design. Speak's product is audio-heavy and real-time. When asked to design a feature, state your constraints first (latency, battery life, offline support, target iOS version), then propose components. Candidates report that interviewers respond well to explicit trade-off reasoning rather than a single 'right' answer.

Rubber-duck for live coding. Before writing any code, restate the problem in your own words and confirm edge cases. For Speak, edge cases often involve audio session interruptions, background modes, or devices with limited memory.

Product lens for any technical decision. Speak is a consumer app with a strong design culture. Frame architectural choices in terms of user impact. Instead of saying 'I chose this pattern to reduce coupling', say 'I chose this pattern because it let us ship without a forced update, so existing users were not disrupted'.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Deep iOS platform knowledge. Candidates report that Speak interviewers probe well below surface-level Swift syntax. Expect questions on memory management, run loops, audio session categories, and how the OS schedules background work.

Real product thinking. Speak is a consumer app with a strong design culture. Interviewers want to see that you think about the user experience, not just code correctness. Mentioning latency, accessibility, or battery impact without being prompted tends to land well.

Ownership and self-direction. The team is lean and moves fast. They look for engineers who can take a loosely defined requirement, ask the right clarifying questions, and drive it to a shipped feature without constant hand-holding.

Clear communication. Speak is a remote-friendly company, so written and verbal clarity matters. Candidates report being evaluated on how well they explain their reasoning, not just whether their solution is correct.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Audio and platform fundamentals. Review AVFoundation, AVAudioEngine, and AVAudioSession. Build a small demo app that records audio, processes it in real time, and plays it back. Read Apple's documentation on audio session interruption handling and background audio modes.

Week 2: System design and architecture. Practice designing an offline-first data sync layer, state management for a multi-screen app, and a speech recognition pipeline. Focus on trade-offs: on-device vs. server-side processing, battery vs. accuracy, local cache vs. always-fresh data.

Week 3: Product and behavioural prep. Use the Speak app daily. Note what feels fast or slow, where the UX delights you, and where you would make different engineering choices. Prepare several STAR stories covering performance improvements, cross-functional collaboration, and a time you pushed back on a product decision.

Week 4: Mock interviews and polish. Do at least two live mock sessions covering system design and behavioural rounds. Review Swift concurrency (async/await, actors) and SwiftUI state management if the job description references either.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating audio as an afterthought. Speak's core product is speech. Candidates who give generic iOS answers without connecting them to audio, latency, or real-time processing miss the mark.

Over-engineering the take-home. Candidates sometimes spend too long building abstractions for a take-home task. Interviewers typically want clean, working code with clear trade-offs explained, not a personal framework.

Skipping the 'why'. Saying 'I used MVVM' without explaining why that architecture suited the specific problem is a common gap. Always explain the trade-off you were navigating.

Not asking clarifying questions in system design. Jumping into a solution before confirming constraints (offline support, target iOS version, expected data volume) signals poor real-world habits.

Ignoring the product context. Candidates who focus purely on code correctness and never mention user experience, accessibility, or performance as user-facing qualities tend to score lower in Speak's process, based on candidate reports.
