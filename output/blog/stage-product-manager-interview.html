01 Overview

Overview

Stage is one of India's fastest-growing regional OTT platforms, built around Haryanvi and other vernacular content for audiences across Tier 2 and Tier 3 India. Product Managers at Stage work at the intersection of content, community, and consumer growth, typically owning metrics like activation, retention, and subscription conversion for a deeply regional user base.

As of July 2026, knok's job radar tracked 2,009 Product Manager openings across India, with Stage alone listing 159 open roles, signalling an active hiring push. The interview process at Stage typically spans three to four rounds: a recruiter or HR screening call, a product case study or take-home exercise, a structured interview covering product thinking and analytics, and a final round with a senior leader.

Candidates report that Stage interviewers focus heavily on empathy for Bharat users, comfort building products for low-bandwidth and first-time smartphone users, and a genuine curiosity about regional content consumption habits. Coming in with a personal point of view on the Stage product, backed by actual usage, makes a strong first impression.

Most Asked Questions

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How would you improve content discovery for a first-time Stage user who has never used an OTT app before?

*Situation:* At my previous company, we ran a regional entertainment app where a large share of new installs came from Tier 2 cities, and many users were first-time OTT consumers who found a standard browse experience overwhelming.
*Task:* I was asked to redesign the first-session experience to improve the share of new users who completed at least one video watch in their first visit.
*Action:* I led user interviews across two Tier 2 cities, mapped the full activation funnel, and found that users stalled on the home screen because the content catalogue felt unfamiliar and the language on screen did not always match their dialect. I proposed a dialect and genre selector at onboarding, a curated 'Start Here' rail limited to short-form popular clips, and a progressive profile built from watch signals rather than upfront questions.
*Result:* The redesigned flow improved first-session completion (exact figures are confidential). The dialect-first onboarding approach was later adopted as the default pattern across the product.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you made a product decision with incomplete data.

*Situation:* We were evaluating whether to launch a live events feature on a content platform I worked on. We had solid usage data for recorded content but no first-party data on live viewing behaviour for our specific audience segment.
*Task:* I needed to give leadership a go or no-go recommendation within two weeks, with no time for a full-scale experiment.
*Action:* I triangulated from three sources: qualitative interviews with a small cohort of power users, publicly reported industry data on live sports and music engagement in regional markets, and a lightweight waitlist test to gauge declared interest. I framed the recommendation as a time-boxed pilot with explicit kill criteria rather than a full launch commitment, which lowered the cost of being wrong.
*Result:* Leadership approved the pilot. It validated strong appetite for live music events specifically, and we ran a proper experiment from there. The kill criteria also prevented over-investment when early numbers on one content type came in below threshold.

---

Q: Describe a product failure you owned. What would you do differently?

*Situation:* I led a push notification re-engagement campaign for lapsed users on a subscription app, intending to bring back subscribers who had not opened the app in several weeks.
*Task:* My goal was to improve reactivation among that cohort without damaging the experience for active users.
*Action:* I shipped a notification sequence based on content recommendations, optimising for open rate as the primary signal. I underweighted notification fatigue and did not set up a holdout group to measure downstream subscription impact.
*Result:* Open rates looked healthy in the first week, but uninstall rate rose sharply and notification opt-outs climbed among the targeted cohort. I had optimised a proxy metric rather than the true outcome. If I ran this again, I would define the north star as reactivated subscriptions from day one, set a holdout group before launch, and cap notification frequency based on individual engagement signals rather than a fixed schedule.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For product design questions, start by naming a specific user and their core job-to-be-done. For Stage, be concrete: rather than 'users,' describe someone like 'a first-time smartphone owner in a small Haryanvi-speaking town who discovered Stage through a WhatsApp share.' Then identify the biggest friction point, propose two or three solutions with explicit trade-offs, and recommend one with a clear rationale tied to a metric Stage would care about.

For metrics questions, open by mapping the user journey: acquisition, activation, engagement, retention, revenue. Identify where value is created or lost, then propose a primary metric tied to a business outcome plus two or three supporting signals. Avoid vanity metrics like raw installs. For an OTT subscription platform, watch-time per session, free-to-paid conversion rate, and monthly renewal rate are stronger anchors than download counts.

For prioritisation questions, state your criteria before scoring options. A simple framework: reach (how many users affected), impact on the north star metric, confidence in that estimate, and engineering effort. Name what you are deprioritising and why. Stage interviewers typically probe the trade-off reasoning as much as the final answer.

For behavioural questions, use STAR: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task to two or three sentences each. Spend the most time on Action, where your individual judgment shows up clearly. Result should tie back to a metric or a concrete business outcome, even if the specific figure is confidential.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Deep empathy for Bharat users. Stage's core audience is not the metro tech-savvy user. Candidates who can speak specifically about low-bandwidth constraints, vernacular UI expectations, or the social sharing behaviours of Tier 2 users stand out. Generic 'user-centric' language without grounding in Stage's actual audience tends to land poorly.

Comfort with ambiguity and early-stage product thinking. Stage operates in a space where data is often thin and market definitions are still evolving. Interviewers want to see how you frame a problem under uncertainty, what assumptions you make explicit, and how you design small experiments to reduce risk before committing resources.

A bias toward outcome metrics over output metrics. Talking about features you shipped without connecting them to retention, revenue, or engagement signals is a common gap. Stage PMs are expected to own the number, not just the roadmap item. Honesty about a failed experiment is valued more than a polished narrative where everything worked perfectly.

A genuine, specific product opinion. Interviewers across rounds report appreciating candidates who have actually used Stage, formed a concrete view on what works and what does not, and can defend that view under pushback without being rigid. This is harder to fake than it sounds, which is why spending time with the app before your interview matters.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know the product inside out.
Download Stage and use it actively for at least a week before your interview. Map the full user journey from install to paid subscription. Note where the experience is strong and where it creates friction. Form two or three concrete improvement hypotheses you can defend in an interview, grounded in your actual experience as a user.

Week 2: Practice core PM frameworks.
Work through at least one product design question, one metrics question, and one prioritisation question each day. Use the frameworks in the Answer Frameworks section. Record yourself answering out loud or practice with a peer, since articulating trade-offs verbally is a different skill from thinking them through silently.

Week 3: Prepare behavioural stories.
Map four or five experiences from your career to the STAR format. Cover: a product you built from zero to one, a time you made a decision with limited data, a stakeholder conflict you navigated, and a failure you owned. These stories should feel natural rather than rehearsed, because Stage interviewers probe and follow up.

Before your interview:
Research Stage's recent product updates and any publicly available news on their content strategy. Glassdoor and LinkedIn can surface interview experiences from other candidates. Prepare two or three sharp questions for the interviewer about current team priorities and how PM success is measured. 07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Talking about generic OTT without Stage specificity. Saying 'I would improve the recommendation algorithm' without connecting it to Stage's regional-language context reads as unprepared. Name the actual audience, the actual constraint, and the specific trade-off Stage faces.

Jumping to solutions before defining the problem. Stage interviewers typically give credit for a well-structured problem definition and flag candidates who propose features before identifying the user, the pain point, and the success metric. Slow down and frame the problem first.

Using metro-centric assumptions. Designing for users with high-speed internet, English-language familiarity, or large-screen devices signals a mismatch with Stage's core market. State your assumptions about connectivity and device type explicitly at the start of any product design answer.

Optimising output over outcome. 'I shipped X feature' without 'and it moved Y metric' is an incomplete answer. If the result is confidential or the experiment failed, say so honestly and describe what you learned. Interviewers at Stage value intellectual honesty about failure over polished narratives.

Ignoring the subscription business model. Stage's revenue depends on subscription conversion and renewal. Any product recommendation that does not consider impact on those metrics will feel disconnected from what Stage actually needs from its PMs.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Stage PM interview typically have?

Candidates report a process that typically runs three to four rounds: a recruiter screening, a product case study or take-home exercise, a structured panel covering product thinking and analytics, and a final round with a senior leader or cross-functional stakeholder. The exact sequence can vary by level and team. Ask your recruiter for the format upfront so you can prepare the right material for each round.

What salary can I expect as a PM at Stage?

Knok's job radar data shows PM compensation in India broadly spanning 12-20 LPA at the Associate PM level, 24-40 LPA for PMs with three to six years of experience, 40-60 LPA for Senior PMs, and 55-90+ LPA for Group or Principal PMs. Stage-specific compensation data is limited in public sources. Glassdoor and levels.fyi are the best places to find self-reported Stage figures before you negotiate.

Does Stage give a take-home assignment?

Candidates report that Stage typically includes a case study or product exercise, either as a take-home before the interview or as a live case during it. These exercises commonly involve designing a feature, diagnosing a metric drop, or making a prioritisation call. Using the actual Stage app before your interview gives you a concrete, specific anchor for whatever case you are handed.

How important is regional content knowledge for a Stage PM role?

It is quite important. Stage's differentiation is built around vernacular content for Bharat users, and interviewers tend to probe whether candidates have genuine empathy for that audience. You do not need to be a native Haryanvi speaker, but you should be able to discuss the constraints and behaviours of Tier 2 and Tier 3 users with specificity. Generic 'user empathy' language without grounding in Stage's actual audience tends to land poorly.

Is prior OTT or media experience required?

Not necessarily. Candidates report that Stage values strong product fundamentals, analytical thinking, and consumer empathy above domain-specific experience. Familiarity with subscription business models, content platforms, or mobile-first consumer products is a practical advantage. If your background is in a different domain, draw explicit parallels to the problems Stage faces in your answers.

How should I prepare for the metrics round?

Focus on the full user funnel for a subscription OTT product: acquisition, activation, engagement, retention, and revenue. Practice defining a north star metric, identifying leading indicators, and diagnosing a metric drop using a structured top-down approach. Stage interviewers typically value clarity on what you are measuring and why over familiarity with specific analytics tools or dashboards.
