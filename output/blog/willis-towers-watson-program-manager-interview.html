01 Overview

Overview

Willis Towers Watson (WTW) is a global advisory and solutions firm with deep expertise in risk management, insurance broking, and human capital consulting. Their India technology centres, primarily in Bangalore, run large-scale enterprise programs spanning benefits administration platforms, actuarial data pipelines, regulatory compliance systems, and SaaS migrations for large enterprise clients worldwide.

The TPM role at WTW sits at the intersection of technology delivery and business outcomes. You coordinate multiple engineering squads, manage relationships with senior business and client-facing stakeholders, and deliver programs within the compliance and risk frameworks that WTW's clients depend on. Candidates report that domain knowledge in insurance, pensions, or HR technology is a plus, but WTW values structured thinking and strong stakeholder management more than deep vertical expertise at the start.

What the process typically looks like: WTW's interview process typically spans multiple rounds. Candidates report an initial recruiter or HR screen, followed by a hiring manager conversation focused on your past program delivery. One or two cross-functional panel interviews then probe stakeholder management, technical depth, and delivery methodology. A final conversation with a senior leader is common for mid-to-senior tracks. Timelines vary by team, so confirm the format with your recruiter early.

As of mid-2026, WTW had 273 open roles listed across job platforms in India, with Bangalore accounting for the largest share of TPM-adjacent openings. This volume signals active headcount expansion across WTW's technology organisation and a genuine window to apply.

Most Asked Questions

  1. Tell us about a program that involved regulatory or compliance requirements. How did you ensure nothing slipped through the cracks?
  1. How do you manage cross-team dependencies when engineering groups are spread across different locations and time zones?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell us about a complex program you managed end-to-end.

*Situation:* Our company was migrating a legacy benefits administration platform used by over a dozen enterprise clients to a modern cloud-based system. The program involved six engineering teams, a compliance review committee, and three business units with different priorities.

*Task:* As the TPM, I was responsible for overall program delivery, stakeholder alignment, and ensuring that client commitments were not missed during the transition.

*Action:* I set up a weekly program sync with all workstream leads, defined a RACI for decision-making so that escalations had a clear path, and introduced a traffic-light status report that went to senior leadership every two weeks. I also ran monthly alignment sessions with the client-facing account managers to surface any risk to client commitments early.

*Result:* The program delivered on the committed milestone with zero client-facing incidents during go-live. The platform then handled onboarding for new clients without the manual workarounds the team had relied on with the old system.

---

Q: Describe a time a program went off-track and how you handled it.

*Situation:* We were building an internal risk analytics dashboard for a global insurer. About two months in, the engineering team flagged that a third-party data vendor was not delivering clean data feeds, which blocked three downstream modules.

*Task:* I needed to protect the overall program timeline while unblocking the teams that depended on the vendor feeds.

*Action:* I immediately escalated the vendor issue to our procurement and legal teams with a hard response deadline. In parallel, I worked with engineering to redesign two of the three blocked modules to run on synthetic test data so development could continue. I also revised the program schedule and used part of an existing testing buffer to absorb the vendor delay without changing the external delivery date.

*Result:* The vendor resolved the data issue within ten days of escalation. Because development had continued in parallel, we lost only one week of overall schedule and delivered the dashboard on the revised date. The client received weekly status notes throughout and did not escalate.

---

Q: How have you handled conflict between a business team and an engineering team?

*Situation:* During a payroll system integration program, the business stakeholders wanted a feature that required a non-standard API connection. The engineering lead felt this would introduce significant security risk and refused to build it as specified.

*Task:* My job was to get both sides to a decision without the program stalling and without creating lasting friction between two teams that would need to work together for several more months.

*Action:* I facilitated a structured working session where engineering walked the business team through the specific security concern with concrete examples, not just a general 'it is risky' statement. I then asked the business team to articulate the underlying need, which turned out to be about audit traceability rather than the specific API approach they had requested. Engineering proposed an alternative: a secure internal logging layer that met the audit requirement without the API risk. I documented the decision, the reasoning, and the trade-offs so there was no ambiguity later.

*Result:* Both teams agreed on the alternative within the session. The feature shipped on schedule and passed the security review in the first audit cycle. The business team later said the logging layer was an improvement over their original request.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for all behavioral questions. Every interview answer at WTW should have a clear Situation, Task, Action, and Result. Keep the Situation and Task brief, one or two sentences each. Spend most of your time on the Actions you personally took. End with a concrete Result that shows business or client impact, not just technical completion.

RACI for ownership and accountability questions. When asked how you clarify roles or resolve stakeholder confusion, show that you know how to define who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed. WTW's matrixed environment means this framework comes up often in panel conversations.

The 'so what' test for metrics. When you share a success metric, follow it immediately with the business implication. 'We shipped on time' is weaker than 'we shipped on time, which meant the client avoided a contractual penalty and renewed.' WTW panels respond well to answers that connect delivery outcomes to business or client consequences.

Layered communication framing for status reporting questions. When asked how you communicate program status, show that your answer changes with the audience. For engineering leads: detailed blockers and dependency maps. For business leaders: RAG status, key risks, and decisions needed. For C-level: a one-page narrative with business impact and the one or two decisions they need to make. Name these layers explicitly when you answer.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Structured thinking under ambiguity. WTW programs often begin with incomplete requirements from multiple business units. Interviewers want evidence that you can create clarity and structure without waiting for a perfectly defined scope to be handed to you.

Stakeholder management with specific evidence. Saying you are good with stakeholders is not enough. Bring at least three distinct stories: one where you managed upward to senior leadership, one where you managed laterally across teams that did not report to you, and one where you managed a difficult external party such as a vendor or client contact.

Client-centricity. WTW serves enterprise clients in regulated industries. Interviewers look for candidates who understand that program delivery has downstream consequences for clients, not just internal teams. Tie your delivery stories to client outcomes wherever you can.

Domain curiosity. You do not need to be an insurance or pensions expert on day one. WTW interviewers respond well to candidates who have done basic research into WTW's business lines, ask sharp domain questions, and demonstrate genuine comfort learning complex regulated environments quickly.

Communication adaptability. Show that you write and speak differently for different audiences. References to specific artefacts you have created, such as status reports, executive briefing notes, or engineering coordination documents, signal the kind of maturity WTW looks for in TPM candidates.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Company and domain research. Read WTW's publicly available annual reports and investor materials to understand their three main business segments: risk and broking, health and benefits, and wealth. Identify which segment the team you are interviewing with primarily serves. This context will make your answers feel tailored rather than generic.

Week 1 to 2: Build your story bank. Map your past programs to common TPM question themes: delivery under pressure, stakeholder conflict, scope change, team continuity, and cross-functional alignment. Write out STAR notes for at least six stories. Aim for stories that show impact on clients or business outcomes, not just internal technical milestones.

Week 2: Practice out loud. Record yourself answering at least five of the questions listed above. Listen back for filler words, vague language (phrases like 'I kind of managed'), and answers without a clear Result. Most strong interview answers run 2-3 minutes, not five.

Before each round: Prepare sharp questions. WTW interviewers typically expect candidates to ask meaningful questions. Strong options include: 'What does success look like for this TPM in the first three months?', 'What is the biggest program risk the team is managing right now?', and 'How does this team interface with the client-facing side of WTW's business?'

Logistics check. Confirm the interview format with your recruiter: whether it is a video panel, a case discussion, or a one-on-one sequence. Candidates report that WTW panels tend to be conversational rather than rigid, but structured preparation still gives you a clear edge.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Staying too technical without connecting to business outcomes. TPM interviews at WTW are not engineering interviews. If your answers focus on tech stack, sprint velocity, or engineering process without tying back to business or client impact, you will lose the panel quickly.

Not having specific details ready. Vague answers hurt. 'I managed a large program with many stakeholders' is far weaker than describing the actual scope, team structure, and outcome in concrete terms. You do not need to memorise every data point, but you should be able to describe scale and result clearly.

Treating every stakeholder the same in your answers. When a question asks how you manage stakeholders, avoid giving a single generic answer. Show that you communicate and engage differently with engineers, business leads, and senior executives. Collapsing all three into one answer suggests limited experience with complex programs.

Underestimating the compliance and risk angle. WTW operates in heavily regulated industries. Answers that show no awareness of compliance checkpoints, audit trails, or regulatory risk signal a mismatch with the environment. Even if your past programs were not in insurance or pensions, draw parallels to any compliance, security, or data governance work you have done.

Skipping the 'what I learned' part of failure stories. When asked about a program that went wrong, candidates often rush to the recovery and skip the reflection. WTW interviewers want to see that you extracted a genuine lesson and changed your approach as a result. End every failure story with what you do differently now.
