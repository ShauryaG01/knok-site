01 Overview

Overview

GSK (GlaxoSmithKline) is one of the world's largest pharmaceutical companies, with a significant and growing data engineering function in India. As of mid-2026, knok jobradar is tracking 64 open Data Engineer roles at GSK, out of 542 Data Engineer openings across the broader market.

Data Engineers at GSK typically work on clinical trial data pipelines, regulatory submissions, supply chain analytics, and commercial insights platforms. The role often intersects with compliance requirements like GxP and GDPR, meaning interviewers look for engineers who understand both the technical side and data governance.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up frequently, based on what candidates report from GSK Data Engineer interviews:

  1. Regulated data pipelines. Describe a time you built or maintained a data pipeline in a regulated or compliance-heavy environment. How did you handle audit trails and data lineage?
  1. GxP or GDPR familiarity. How do you ensure data handling meets regulatory requirements like GxP or GDPR? What processes or controls do you put in place?
  1. Data quality at scale. You receive a dataset from a clinical or research system with missing values and inconsistencies. Walk us through how you would detect, report, and resolve the issues.
  1. Cloud data platform experience. Which cloud platforms have you worked on? Describe the architecture of a data platform you built or contributed to.
  1. ETL vs. ELT. When would you choose ETL over ELT and vice versa? Give an example from your own work.
  1. Real-time vs. batch processing. Describe a scenario where you had to decide between streaming and batch ingestion. What factors guided your decision?
  1. SQL optimisation. A query on a large table is running slowly. Walk us through your debugging and optimisation approach.
  1. Schema evolution. How do you handle breaking schema changes in an upstream source without disrupting downstream consumers?
  1. Stakeholder collaboration. Describe a project where you worked closely with data scientists or business analysts. How did you translate their data needs into a reliable pipeline?
  1. Data governance and cataloguing. What tools or practices have you used to maintain data lineage, cataloguing, and access controls?
  1. Incident handling. A pipeline you own failed overnight and reports are unavailable in the morning. Walk us through how you would diagnose and communicate the issue.
  1. Why GSK. GSK works at the intersection of healthcare and data. How does that align with what you want to build in your career?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for behavioural questions. Here are three examples tailored to what GSK typically asks:

---

Q: Describe a time you built a data pipeline in a compliance-heavy environment.

*Situation:* My previous company processed patient-linked data for a healthcare analytics product. A new regulation required all data movement to be fully auditable with timestamps and user-level access logs.

*Task:* I was asked to redesign the existing ETL pipeline so every transformation step logged who accessed what data and when, without significantly slowing down the pipeline.

*Action:* I introduced an event-driven audit layer using Apache Kafka. Every pipeline step published a structured event (source, destination, row count, timestamp, run ID) to a separate audit topic. I also moved sensitive fields into a dedicated encryption module and updated the data catalogue to reflect lineage.

*Result:* The pipeline passed an internal compliance audit on the first review. The audit log overhead added minimal latency to batch runs, and the team adopted the same pattern for two other pipelines.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you caught and fixed a serious data quality issue.

*Situation:* We were ingesting sales data from a third-party CRM. A mid-sprint data check revealed that records from one region had been duplicated due to a retry bug in the source system's API.

*Task:* I needed to identify the scope of the duplication, fix the current data, and prevent recurrence, all while keeping dashboards available to users.

*Action:* I wrote a deduplication query using window functions (ROW_NUMBER partitioned by business key and timestamp) to flag duplicates. I applied this as a correction layer in the staging zone rather than touching the raw layer, preserving the original data. I then added a row-count reconciliation check between source API counts and our ingested counts to every future pipeline run.

*Result:* Dashboards were unaffected during the fix. The reconciliation check has since caught two further upstream issues before they reached any consumer.

---

Q: Describe a project where you collaborated closely with a data scientist.

*Situation:* A data science team wanted to build a churn prediction model but kept hitting issues with feature inconsistency between training and production scoring.

*Task:* My role was to build a feature store pipeline that would serve both the training job and the real-time scoring API with identical transformations.

*Action:* I worked with the data scientist to document every feature transformation in code rather than notebooks. We used dbt to version and test transformations, and I built a scheduled pipeline that materialised features daily. I also added data freshness checks so the scoring API would alert rather than silently use stale data.

*Result:* The training-to-production skew issue was eliminated. The data scientist reported that model performance in production matched offline evaluation for the first time in that team's history.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For technical questions: Use a structured 'problem, approach, tradeoff' flow. State the problem clearly, explain the approach you would take and why, then acknowledge the tradeoff you are making (speed vs. cost, simplicity vs. flexibility). Interviewers at pharmaceutical companies appreciate that you recognise constraints, not just optimal solutions.

For behavioural questions: Stick to STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result). Keep Situation brief (one or two sentences). Spend the most time on Action, which is where your actual skill shows. End with a concrete Result: a metric from the data, a process that was adopted, or a problem that did not recur.

For compliance or governance questions: Lead with the 'why' before the 'how.' GSK operates under strict regulatory frameworks. Showing that you understand why data lineage, audit trails, and access controls matter in a pharma context, before diving into the tooling, signals the right mindset.

For system design questions: Think out loud. Interviewers want to see how you break down ambiguity. Start by clarifying requirements (scale, latency, consistency needs), then sketch an architecture, then explain where you would monitor and what could go wrong.

For 'why GSK' questions: Connect your answer to something specific about healthcare data. Generic answers about 'impact' are common. Candidates who mention clinical data complexity, the role of real-world evidence, or regulatory data challenges tend to stand out.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Domain awareness. GSK is not a typical tech company. Interviewers want to see that you understand the data environment in pharma: clinical trial datasets, supply chain data, post-market surveillance. You do not need a life sciences degree, but you should be able to discuss why data quality and traceability matter more in this industry than in most others.

Compliance instinct. Questions about GxP, GDPR, and audit trails come up frequently. Candidates report that interviewers probe whether you treat compliance as an afterthought or as a first-class engineering concern. GSK consistently wants the latter.

Cloud and modern stack fluency. GSK has invested heavily in cloud platforms. Expect questions on Azure or AWS data services, Spark, Databricks, or dbt. You do not need to know every tool, but you should be able to explain the architecture of a cloud-native pipeline end to end.

Communication with non-technical stakeholders. Data Engineers at GSK regularly work with scientists, regulatory affairs teams, and commercial leads. Interviewers look for candidates who can explain a technical decision in plain terms without losing the person on the other side.

Ownership and reliability. In a regulated environment, pipelines cannot silently fail. Interviewers are particularly interested in how you monitor pipelines, handle failures, and communicate incidents. Treating data reliability as your responsibility (not the platform's) is a strong signal.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know the domain.

Read about GSK's data and tech strategy from their publicly available annual reports and investor materials. Understand the role of real-world evidence and clinical data in the pharmaceutical industry. This context will make your answers feel specific rather than generic.

Week 2: Sharpen your technical core.

Revise SQL window functions, query optimisation, and indexing. Practice explaining ETL vs. ELT, batch vs. streaming, and schema evolution out loud. If you have not worked with cloud data services (Azure Data Factory, AWS Glue, Databricks), run through one or two hands-on tutorials.

Week 3: Prepare your STAR stories.

Write down five to six experiences that cover: a compliance or governance challenge, a data quality incident, a collaboration with a non-engineer, a system design decision, and a pipeline failure you resolved. Each story should end with a concrete result. Vague answers are probed harder than specific ones.

Week 4: Practice and mock rounds.

Do at least two timed mock interviews where you answer questions out loud, not just in your head. Focus on keeping answers to two to three minutes. Prepare the 'why GSK' answer last, because it should reflect everything else you have prepared.

On the day: Candidates report that GSK interviewers appreciate thoughtful questions from the candidate's side. Asking about the team's current data platform, upcoming initiatives, or how the role contributes to a specific product area shows genuine interest.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

1. Treating compliance as a checkbox. Many candidates mention GDPR or GxP briefly and move on. At GSK, interviewers look for depth. If you say you ensured compliance, be ready to explain exactly how: what controls you put in place, how you tested them, and how you documented them.

2. Generic cloud answers. Saying 'I have worked on AWS' without being able to describe the specific services (S3, Redshift, Glue, Lambda) or the architecture you built is a common weak point. Be specific about the stack and your role in building it.

3. Skipping the monitoring story. Many candidates explain how they built a pipeline but forget to mention how they monitored it. In regulated industries, observability is as important as the build. Always include how you would know if something went wrong.

4. Not asking questions. Candidates who have no questions for the interviewer often come across as less engaged. Prepare at least two questions about the team's data platform, data challenges, or roadmap.

5. Overstating tool experience. Interviewers at companies like GSK often follow up on tools listed in your resume. If you list Databricks, expect a question on Delta Lake, cluster configuration, or a specific feature. Only claim tools you can actually discuss in depth.

6. Missing the healthcare angle. GSK looks for people who are motivated by the healthcare mission, not just the engineering. If your answer to 'why GSK' focuses purely on tech stack or growth opportunities, it tends to land flat.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the GSK Data Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report that the process typically involves three to four rounds. This commonly includes an initial recruiter screening, one or two technical rounds covering SQL, pipeline design, and cloud architecture, and a final round focused on behavioural questions and team fit. Round structure can vary by team and hiring manager, so confirm the format with your recruiter after the first call.

Does GSK ask live coding questions or is it more system design?

Candidates report a mix of both. SQL questions covering window functions, joins, and query optimisation come up in most technical rounds. System design questions on pipeline architecture, batch vs. streaming decisions, and data modelling are common at mid and senior levels. Live coding in Python or PySpark is less consistently reported but does appear in some rounds, typically for roles with a heavier engineering component.

Do I need a pharma or life sciences background to get a Data Engineer role at GSK?

No, a life sciences degree is not typically required. Candidates report that GSK values strong data engineering fundamentals and the ability to learn domain context quickly. Showing some awareness of why data quality and compliance matter in pharma (clinical trials, regulatory submissions, GxP standards) gives you a clear advantage over candidates who treat the role as a generic tech position.

What cloud platforms does GSK use for data engineering in India?

Publicly reported information and candidate feedback suggest GSK primarily uses Microsoft Azure alongside some AWS services, though the specific stack can vary by business unit and region. Familiarity with Azure Data Factory, Azure Databricks, or equivalent AWS data services is useful preparation. Strong fundamentals in cloud-native data architecture matter more than memorising one vendor's toolset.

What salary can I expect for a Data Engineer role at GSK in India?

Based on knok jobradar data, the broader Data Engineer market in India ranges from 6-12 LPA at entry level (0-2 years), 14-26 LPA at mid level (3-5 years), 28-45 LPA at senior level (6-9 years), and 42-65+ LPA at Lead or Staff level. GSK-specific compensation can vary and is best verified via Glassdoor or confirmed directly with the recruiter during the offer stage.

How should I prepare for the 'why GSK' question?

Avoid generic answers about 'impact' or 'global scale.' Instead, connect your answer to something specific: the complexity of clinical or regulatory data, the role data engineering plays in accelerating drug discovery, or GSK's publicly stated digital strategy. Candidates who show awareness of the healthcare context, even without a pharma background, consistently report stronger outcomes in this part of the interview.
