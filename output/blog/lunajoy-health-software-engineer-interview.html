01 Overview

Overview

LunaJoy Health builds mental health technology that connects patients with licensed therapists and care coordinators. Engineers here work on scheduling systems, telehealth infrastructure, patient-provider matching, and secure health records. The product sits at the intersection of consumer-grade UX and healthcare-grade reliability, which shapes how interviews are run.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions reflect patterns candidates report for health-tech and mission-driven software companies similar to LunaJoy.

  1. Sensitive data handling: 'Tell me about a time you worked with health or personally identifiable data. What did you do to protect it?'
  2. System design for matching: 'How would you design a patient-therapist scheduling or matching system to handle high concurrency and edge cases?'
  3. Production incident ownership: 'Describe a production bug or outage you owned from detection to resolution. What was the root cause?'
  4. High-stakes code quality: 'How do you approach writing code when a mistake could directly affect a vulnerable user's care experience?'
  5. Cross-functional collaboration: 'Tell me about a time you gathered requirements from a non-technical stakeholder, such as a product manager or clinical staff, and translated them into a working solution.'
  6. API security: 'How have you secured an API that handles sensitive or health-related user information?'
  7. Pushing back on scope: 'Describe a time you pushed back on a deadline or feature request because the system was not ready. What happened?'
  8. Technical debt tradeoffs: 'How do you balance paying down technical debt against shipping new features on a lean team?'
  9. Notification system design: 'How would you design a notification system that respects a user's mental health preferences and avoids causing anxiety or alert fatigue?'
  10. Mission alignment: 'Why do you want to work on mental health technology specifically? What draws you to this space?'
  11. Testing strategy: 'How do you decide what to test and at what level for a healthcare feature where correctness has real consequences?'
  12. Incident communication: 'How have you communicated a production issue to non-technical stakeholders while still actively working on the fix?'

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a time you worked with sensitive user data and ensured it was protected.

*Situation:* I was a backend engineer at a fintech startup where we stored user KYC documents including identity verification files.
*Task:* We needed to add a document retrieval feature for internal compliance review, but the existing access controls were loose and did not log who accessed what.
*Action:* I audited existing data access paths, introduced role-based access control so only authorised compliance staff could retrieve documents, added an audit log to every read operation, and encrypted documents at rest. I also wrote a runbook for the team on data access protocols.
*Result:* The feature shipped with zero compliance flags in the next internal audit, and the audit log later helped us identify and block an accidental bulk-export triggered by a misconfigured script.

---

Q: Describe a production incident you owned end-to-end.

*Situation:* Our appointment booking service started returning server errors for a subset of users on a weekend afternoon, with a significant share of booking attempts failing.
*Task:* I was the on-call engineer and needed to identify the cause and restore service without a full rollback.
*Action:* I checked error logs and found a null pointer exception in a timezone-conversion function deployed that morning. I isolated the failing code path, deployed a hotfix that added a null check and defaulted to UTC when timezone data was missing, then verified the fix on staging before pushing to production.
*Result:* The error rate dropped to zero shortly after the hotfix was deployed. I then wrote a post-mortem and added a unit test covering the null case to prevent recurrence.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you collaborated with a non-technical stakeholder to build a useful feature.

*Situation:* A clinical operations manager at my company wanted a dashboard showing therapist capacity and patient waitlist sizes, but she was not sure what data she actually needed to make staffing decisions.
*Task:* I needed to gather requirements from someone unfamiliar with technical constraints and deliver something genuinely useful, not just technically correct.
*Action:* I ran two sessions with her using a simple prototype built in a spreadsheet first. I asked her to walk me through her current manual process, noted which questions she repeated, and used those to define four key metrics. I then built a read-only dashboard querying our existing database, with no new data pipelines required.
*Result:* She used the dashboard daily within the first week and reported it significantly reduced her Monday morning reporting prep time.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR is the core structure for every behavioural question at LunaJoy: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task brief (two or three sentences). Spend most of your time on Action, because that is where interviewers learn how you actually think. End with a concrete, observable Result.

For system design questions, start by clarifying requirements out loud. At a health-tech company like LunaJoy, interviewers typically want to hear you ask about data sensitivity, failure modes, and user experience before jumping to architecture. A reliable sequence: clarify scope, estimate scale, define the data model, sketch components, then discuss tradeoffs.

For 'why this company' questions, connect your answer to something specific about mental health technology. Candidates who mention a genuine interest in healthcare access or who cite publicly reported details about LunaJoy's mission tend to stand out over those who give a generic 'I want to make an impact' answer.

For 'quality vs. speed' questions, name a concrete mechanism you actually use, such as a feature flag, a gradual rollout, or a specific testing layer. Vague answers do not differentiate you.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

LunaJoy interviewers typically look for three qualities together: technical depth, user empathy, and mission alignment.

Technical depth means you can go beyond 'what' to explain 'why'. For coding rounds, candidates report that interviewers care about code readability and edge case handling, not just whether the solution runs. For design rounds, they want to see you reason about failure modes proactively.

User empathy carries unusual weight here because the product serves people in emotionally difficult moments. Candidates who treat users as abstractions or data points tend not to advance past the values round, based on publicly reported interview feedback. Frame your past work in terms of how it affected real people.

Mission alignment does not require a personal mental health story. It means you can clearly explain why reliable, privacy-respecting software matters in healthcare and why that motivates you to do your best engineering work.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Weeks 1-2: Company and domain research
Read LunaJoy's publicly available material about their product and mission. Understand how telehealth scheduling, patient matching, and secure messaging typically work at the architecture level. Note any publicly reported engineering blog posts or talks from their team.

Weeks 2-3: Technical preparation
Practise coding problems focused on scheduling algorithms, graph problems (useful for matching), and API design. Review how sensitive health data is typically protected at the architecture level, since candidates report this topic comes up in design discussions even for candidates without a healthcare background.

Week 3: Behavioural preparation
Write out five to six STAR stories covering: a time you handled sensitive data, a production incident, a cross-functional collaboration, a quality vs. speed tradeoff, and a time you disagreed with a teammate or manager. Practise saying each one out loud in under three minutes.

Before each call: Prepare two or three specific questions for the interviewer about their engineering culture, on-call practices, or how the team handles technical debt. Thoughtful questions signal genuine interest in the role.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating healthcare context as generic: Candidates who answer data-handling questions without acknowledging patient sensitivity or compliance constraints miss a key signal LunaJoy is looking for. Even without a health-tech background, show you understand why it matters here specifically.

Jumping to a solution before clarifying requirements: In system design rounds, candidates report that LunaJoy interviewers watch for whether you ask clarifying questions first. Starting to architect before understanding scale or constraints is a red flag.

Vague mission alignment: Saying 'I want to make an impact' without connecting it to mental health or healthcare reads as generic. Interviewers can tell when the same answer would be given to any company in any sector.

Skipping the result in STAR answers: Many candidates give a long account of what they did but forget to state what happened as a result. The outcome is what gives the story weight and makes it stick.

Not preparing questions to ask: Candidates who ask nothing during the interview sometimes signal low interest. Prepare at least two genuine questions in advance.

Over-engineering the design: For a company at LunaJoy's stage, proposing a massively distributed architecture for a straightforward problem can raise doubts about practical judgement. Match the design to the stated scale and constraints.
