01 Overview

Overview

GlossGenius is a vertical SaaS company building an all-in-one platform for beauty and wellness professionals, including solo stylists, barbers, estheticians, and multi-chair salon owners. Their product covers online booking, payment processing, client management, marketing automation, and team scheduling. It is a US-headquartered company with a product-forward culture where designers work directly on problems faced by small business owners who rely on the app to run their livelihood.

GlossGenius currently has 4 open Product Designer roles. No specific round names are standardized, so timelines vary. The role sits at the intersection of consumer-grade polish and business-critical workflows. You will be expected to own design end to end, from discovery and user research through high-fidelity prototypes to post-launch iteration.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Interviewers at GlossGenius typically focus on product thinking, empathy for small business owners, and your design process. Here are the questions candidates most commonly report.

  1. Walk us through a project where you designed for a non-technical user. What did you learn about their mental model?
  2. GlossGenius serves small business owners. How do you balance simplicity for first-time users against depth for experienced ones?
  3. Tell us about a time you used data or user research to change the direction of a design.
  4. How do you approach designing for mobile-first experiences, especially for users who may be mid-service (mid-haircut, mid-treatment)?
  5. Describe a design decision you pushed back on. How did you make your case to stakeholders?
  6. Walk us through the portfolio piece that best shows your product thinking, not just your visual craft.
  7. How do you design for trust in a product that handles real money for small business owners?
  8. Tell us about a time your first design solution turned out to be wrong. How did you find out, and what did you do?
  9. How do you collaborate with engineers during handoff to make sure your design intent survives implementation?
  10. GlossGenius competes in a crowded market for booking and payments. How do you approach competitive research without simply copying what others have done?
  11. How would you measure whether a new feature you shipped actually improved the experience for a salon owner?
  12. Describe your process for running or synthesizing user research when you have very limited time.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through a project where you designed for a non-technical user.

*Situation:* I was the lead designer on a job-scheduling feature for a home services app. Our users were tradespeople who managed their entire business from a phone.

*Task:* I needed to redesign the booking flow, which had a significant drop-off that product and analytics had flagged as a priority.

*Action:* I ran several contextual interviews, watching users try to complete bookings on-site. I found they were confused by date-picker patterns borrowed from travel apps. I rebuilt the flow around 'today / tomorrow / this week' quick-select options and reduced required fields by letting users add details after confirming the slot.

*Result:* Usability testing showed a clear improvement in task completion, and the direction was approved for production. The lesson I carry forward: patterns that work for tech-savvy users often create friction for trade professionals.

---

Q: Describe a design decision you pushed back on.

*Situation:* A PM wanted to add a promotional banner on the home screen of a business dashboard, based on a request from the marketing team.

*Task:* My job was to design it, but I believed it would add noise to a screen that users relied on to quickly scan their day.

*Action:* Rather than just saying no, I pulled session recordings showing users spent only a moment on the home screen before going straight to their calendar. I put together a short comparison: a banner approach versus a lower-friction placement in the notifications panel. I proposed a short A/B test before committing to either option.

*Result:* The PM agreed to run the test. The notification panel variant performed better and the home screen stayed clean. That experience shaped how I handle pushback now: pair the critique with a concrete next step, not just a concern.

---

Q: How would you measure whether a new feature you shipped actually improved the experience?

*Situation:* After shipping a redesigned client-messaging feature at a previous company, we had no agreed success metric before launch.

*Task:* I needed to build a post-launch measurement plan and make the case for doing this earlier in the process on the next project.

*Action:* I partnered with the data analyst to define a north-star metric: the share of service providers who sent at least one client message within their first week using the feature. I also set up a short survey focused on that flow and scheduled a follow-up usability session with a small group of users several weeks post-launch.

*Result:* We had a clear baseline within a month and could tie design iterations to real movement. In the next planning cycle, I successfully pushed for agreeing on success metrics before design starts, and that became a team norm.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For product thinking questions (such as 'how would you design X for GlossGenius'):

  1. Clarify the user and context first. Who is this salon owner? What are they trying to do, on which device, and at what point in their day?
  2. State the problem you are solving, not just the feature you are building.
  3. Walk through the trade-offs you considered and why you chose your direction.
  4. Explain how you would validate your direction before or after shipping.

For behavioral questions, use STAR: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep the Situation brief. Spend most of your time on the Action, specifically what YOU did personally. Make Results concrete even if qualitative rather than numerical.

For 'critique a product' questions (GlossGenius may ask you to critique their own app or a competitor's):

  1. Start with what the product does well. This shows you are a fair observer, not just a critic.
  2. Identify one specific user need that is underserved, grounded in user behavior rather than personal preference.
  3. Propose a direction with a rationale tied to that behavior.
05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

GlossGenius interviewers typically look for four things.

Empathy for small business owners. Their customers run tight operations with no IT support and no room for confusing software. Interviewers want to see that you have done real work to understand users who are unlike the average tech professional.

Clear product thinking. Designers here are expected to contribute to product strategy, not just execute visual work. Expect questions about why a feature should exist, not just how it should look. If your portfolio only shows screens, add the story behind each decision.

Craft at the right fidelity. Your portfolio should show polished final work but also rough explorations. Candidates report that showing messy thinking (journey maps, quick sketches, annotated wireframes) is valued as much as pixel-perfect UIs.

Collaboration under real constraints. GlossGenius is a mid-size company and designers work closely with engineers and PMs. Show examples of negotiating trade-offs, shipping something imperfect, and iterating based on what you learned.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Know the product before your first call. Try a GlossGenius free trial or watch walkthrough demos. Document two or three flows you think work well and one you believe could be stronger. Note their mobile patterns since their users are often on their feet between appointments.

Curate a focused portfolio. Pick two or three projects that show different strengths: one driven by research, one solving a complex UX problem, one showing visual quality. Prepare a brief verbal walkthrough for each, covering the problem, your decisions, and what you learned.

Practice behavioral answers out loud. Run STAR answers for the most common scenarios: pushing back on a stakeholder, a design that failed, and a cross-functional conflict. Recording yourself once helps catch vague language and pacing issues.

Prepare thoughtful questions. Read GlossGenius's blog and LinkedIn for signals about current priorities or recent product launches. Tailor at least one question you ask each interviewer to what you have read. Generic questions signal low preparation.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Showing only polished deliverables. Interviewers at companies like GlossGenius typically want to see your thinking, not just the final screen. Candidates who present only high-fidelity mockups without explaining the reasoning behind decisions often struggle to progress.

Ignoring the end user in your answers. Discussing design decisions without grounding them in the salon owner or stylist's perspective is a red flag at a company whose entire product exists for that user.

Being vague about your contribution. In team projects, say 'I did X, my colleague handled Y' clearly. Interviewers probe for individual ownership, and vague answers create doubt about what you actually built.

Presenting only ideal solutions. Strong design answers acknowledge what you could NOT do and why. Skipping constraints (technical, time, resource) can make you sound inexperienced or out of touch with how real products ship.

Not asking good questions. GlossGenius operates in a fast-moving, competitive market. A thoughtful question about product direction or team structure signals genuine interest. Saying 'I have no questions' is a common way to leave a weak final impression.
