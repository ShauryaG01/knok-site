01 Overview

Overview

Five9 is a cloud contact centre platform used by enterprise customer service teams worldwide. Product Designers at Five9 typically own the design of agent desktops, supervisor dashboards, AI-assist features, and reporting interfaces. These are mission-critical B2B tools where clarity and speed matter more than visual polish, because every extra click costs an agent time on a live call.

Five9 currently has 165 open roles across the company, pointing to active hiring. The Product Designer interview process typically includes a recruiter screen, a portfolio review with the design team, a design challenge (take-home or live), and one or two cross-functional rounds with product managers, engineers, and sometimes customer success leads. Candidates report that interviewers focus heavily on design thinking and communication, not just the quality of your visuals.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These are questions candidates report hearing in Five9 Product Designer interviews. Prepare a specific example for each one.

  1. Walk us through a project where you redesigned a complex workflow for a B2B or enterprise user. What changed and why?
  2. How would you design a real-time dashboard for a contact centre supervisor monitoring a large team of agents?
  3. Five9's users are often non-technical agents handling live calls under time pressure. How does that context shape your design decisions?
  4. Describe a time you had to balance conflicting priorities from business stakeholders and users. How did you resolve it?
  5. How have you used quantitative data or analytics to validate or change a design decision?
  6. Tell us about a design that did not perform as expected after launch. What did you learn?
  7. How do you approach designing for accessibility in a fast-paced, high-frequency work environment like a contact centre?
  8. How do you collaborate with engineers during the build phase, and how do you handle gaps between your design and what gets shipped?
  9. If you joined Five9 next week, which part of the agent or supervisor experience would you look at first, and why?
  10. Tell us about a time you pushed back on a feature request from a stakeholder. How did you handle the conversation?
  11. How do you design for multiple user personas (agents, supervisors, admins) within the same product without fracturing the experience?
  12. Walk us through your end-to-end design process, from a vague problem statement to a shipped feature.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use these as a starting point. Replace the specifics with your own experience.

Q: Walk us through a project where you redesigned a complex workflow for an enterprise user.

*Situation:* Our support team was using a legacy case management tool with a multi-step resolution flow. Agents were spending more time navigating the tool than actually helping customers.

*Task:* I was asked to reduce navigation friction without changing the underlying data model or requiring a full rebuild.

*Action:* I ran several contextual inquiry sessions with agents on the floor, mapped every click in the existing flow, and identified three steps that could be collapsed using smarter defaults. I prototyped the revised flow in Figma, ran usability tests with a small group of agents, and iterated twice before handing off to engineering with detailed specs.

*Result:* Teams reported a measurable drop in average handle time after launch. Agent satisfaction with the tool improved in the next internal survey, and the project became an internal reference for the 'design for the agent first' principle.

---

Q: Describe a time you had to balance competing stakeholder priorities.

*Situation:* A product manager wanted to add a prominent promotional banner to the agent desktop homepage to drive adoption of a new feature. Agents had already flagged the desktop as too cluttered in recent feedback sessions.

*Task:* My job was to find a solution that served the adoption goal without adding to the noise agents were already complaining about.

*Action:* I proposed a dismissible contextual tooltip that appeared only the first few times an agent was eligible to use the feature. I mocked up both options, pulled click-through benchmarks from a similar earlier experiment, and presented the trade-offs to the PM and the agent experience lead together in the same room.

*Result:* The team chose the tooltip approach. The feature's adoption rate was publicly reported by the product team as comparable to past banner campaigns, while desktop satisfaction scores held steady in the next survey cycle.

---

Q: Tell us about a design that did not land as expected.

*Situation:* I redesigned a knowledge base search interface that agents used during live calls. I was confident the new layout would speed up search.

*Task:* Ship the redesign and measure its effect on agent search success rate.

*Action:* I ran an A/B test. Partway through, data showed that more experienced agents who relied on text labels were struggling with the new icon-only navigation. I had assumed icon recognition would be universal, but it was not.

*Result:* I rolled back the icon-only version, added persistent text labels alongside the icons, and relaunched. The revised version performed better across all agent groups. I now include 'label removal' as a specific checkpoint in every accessibility review I run.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions. Every 'tell me about a time' question calls for Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task brief. Spend most of your time on Action, where your thinking is visible. Close with a concrete Result, even if it is qualitative.

'How would you design X' questions. Start by clarifying constraints: who is the primary user, what does success look like, what are the technical or time limits? Then walk through your process: discovery, problem framing, idea generation, narrowing down, testing, and iteration. Interviewers want to see structured thinking, not a finished answer delivered in one breath.

Portfolio walkthroughs. Use a four-part structure: the problem and why it mattered, your specific role on the project, the two or three key design decisions and why you made them, and the outcome. Avoid walking through every screen. Pick the moments where you made a non-obvious call and explain your reasoning there.

Pushback and stakeholder questions. These test your communication as much as your design instincts. Lead with curiosity ('I wanted to understand the goal behind the request') before describing your alternative proposal. Show that you listen first and advocate second.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Systems thinking. Five9's products are deeply interconnected. Interviewers want to see that you consider upstream and downstream effects of a design change, not just the screen in front of you. If you redesign one part of the agent desktop, what breaks or improves elsewhere?

Empathy for B2B users. Contact centre agents are not casual app users. They are under time pressure, working across multiple screens, doing repetitive tasks for hours at a stretch. Showing that you have designed for this kind of high-frequency, high-stakes context is a strong signal.

Data-driven decisions. Five9 teams typically expect designers to partner with analysts and use quantitative signals alongside qualitative research. Be ready to talk about metrics you have tracked and decisions you have revised based on data.

Cross-functional collaboration. At a B2B company, product, engineering, customer success, and sales all have opinions on the product. Candidates who can work across these functions without becoming passive tend to stand out.

Clear communication. Your ability to explain a trade-off plainly in a design review or stakeholder meeting matters as much as the trade-off itself. Practise describing your decisions in plain language before the interview.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Use the product. Watch Five9 demo videos or request a trial. Spend time in the agent desktop and supervisor views. Note specific moments of friction and clarity. Come ready to discuss what you observed.

Step 2: Curate your portfolio. Select two or three projects that show complex workflow design, data-dense interfaces, or enterprise and B2B tools. Contact centre, CRM, or productivity software experience is especially relevant. For each project, prepare the four-part walkthrough described in the frameworks section.

Step 3: Prepare your STAR stories. Write out answers to the questions listed above. Aim for three to five strong stories you can adapt across question types. Practise saying them out loud until they feel natural, not rehearsed.

Step 4: Research Five9's product direction. Read their recent press releases and product update posts. Pay attention to the AI features they are building, such as intelligent virtual agents and real-time transcription. This context will sharpen your answers to 'how would you improve X' questions.

Step 5: Prepare your own questions. Candidates report that thoughtful questions at the end leave a strong impression. Ask about the design team's structure, how design decisions get made in practice, and what a strong first few months looks like for this specific role.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating it like a consumer app interview. Five9 is enterprise software. Answers that focus on delightful animations or brand aesthetics miss the point. Talk about efficiency, error prevention, and designing for repeated daily use.

Skipping constraints in design challenges. Jumping to a solution without establishing user, business, and technical constraints signals shallow thinking. Always frame the problem before you sketch.

Vague outcomes in STAR answers. Saying 'the design was well received' is weak. Connect your result to something observable: a metric, a user quote, or a decision the team made because of your work.

Walking through every screen in your portfolio. Interviewers have limited time. A walkthrough of every state and edge case is less effective than a focused story about two or three key decisions.

Staying silent during a live design challenge. Silence is not confidence. Asking smart clarifying questions and narrating your reasoning shows how you would actually work with the team day to day.

Underselling your individual contribution. On team projects, be specific about what you personally owned. Use 'we' for context, but make sure the key decisions have an 'I' in them somewhere.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many interview rounds does Five9 typically have for a Product Designer role?

Candidates report the process typically involves four to five touchpoints: a recruiter screen, a portfolio review with the design team, a design challenge, and one or two cross-functional rounds. The exact structure can vary by team and seniority level. Ask your recruiter for the full process upfront so you can prepare for each stage specifically.

Is there a take-home design challenge, and what should I expect?

Most candidates report receiving a design challenge, either as a take-home assignment or a live exercise during an interview round. Take-home prompts typically ask you to redesign a specific workflow or propose a new feature for a contact centre context. The debrief is usually where interviewers ask the most probing questions, so be ready to defend every decision you made, including the ones you ruled out.

What kind of portfolio work should I highlight for a Five9 role?

Prioritise projects that show complex interaction design, data-heavy interfaces, or enterprise and B2B tools. Contact centre, CRM, or productivity software experience is especially relevant. If your portfolio is consumer-focused, explicitly connect the challenges you solved (multiple user types, high task frequency, error prevention under pressure) to what a Five9 designer faces every day.

What salary can I expect as a Product Designer at Five9?

Five9 does not publicly share salary bands for India-based roles. Based on knok jobradar data, mid-level Product Designers (3-5 years) in India typically see offers in the 14-24 LPA range across the broader market. Check Glassdoor and levels.fyi for reported Five9-specific compensation data before you go into a negotiation conversation.

How should I prepare for the portfolio review round specifically?

Pick two or three projects that show your design process end to end, from early research to a shipped product. For each one, practise the four-part structure: the problem, your specific role, the key decisions you made and why, and the outcome. Interviewers often ask what you would do differently with more time or resources, so prepare a candid answer to that question as well.
