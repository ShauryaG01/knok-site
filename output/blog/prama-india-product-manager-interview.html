01 Overview

Overview

Prama India is a homegrown surveillance and security technology brand with products spanning IP cameras, video management software (VMS), access control systems, and integrated smart security solutions. Their clients include enterprises, retail chains, and government bodies running smart city and critical infrastructure projects. This hardware-software combination makes PM roles here quite different from a pure software product company.

Prama India currently has 4 open Product Manager positions. The broader Indian PM job market is active, with 2009 openings tracked by knok jobradar as of July 2026, led by Bangalore (271 roles) and Delhi (177 roles).

Typically, the interview process runs 3-4 rounds covering product thinking, domain knowledge in security tech, and stakeholder management. Candidates report that knowing Prama's product portfolio before the first call makes a visible difference.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates who have interviewed at Prama India typically face questions across four themes: product strategy, hardware-software complexity, B2B and government stakeholder management, and security domain knowledge. Here are the questions most commonly reported:

  1. Walk us through a product you built from scratch. What was the biggest learning?
  2. How would you prioritize a roadmap for a video management system serving both enterprise and government clients?
  3. Prama sells hardware and software together. How do you manage PM responsibilities when the product has physical components?
  4. Describe a time you worked with a hardware engineering team. How did you handle delays caused by supply chain or manufacturing issues?
  5. How would you define and measure success for a new AI-powered surveillance feature?
  6. A large government client is asking for a custom feature that is not on your roadmap. How do you handle this?
  7. Walk us through how you would run user discovery for a new smart city product.
  8. How do you approach pricing for a B2B security product sold through channel partners and system integrators?
  9. Tell us about a time you had to kill a feature or project. How did you handle it with internal teams and with clients?
  10. Prama is looking to expand into new markets. How would you localize a security product for a new geography?
  11. How do you balance short-term client customization requests against the long-term platform vision?
  12. Describe your experience handling compliance, data privacy, or regulatory requirements in a product context.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR structure (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for all behavioral questions. Here are three examples tailored to Prama India's context.

---

Q: How would you prioritize a roadmap serving both enterprise and government clients?

*Situation:* At my previous company, our B2B product served mid-size corporate clients and two state government accounts. Each quarter, both segments submitted conflicting feature requests, and the engineering team had limited capacity.

*Task:* I needed to build a single defensible roadmap that reduced churn risk for both segments without forking the codebase.

*Action:* I set up a weighted scoring model with three factors: business impact (renewal value and upsell potential), user frequency (how many users would benefit), and engineering effort. I introduced a 'shared platform vs. module' split so government-specific workflows lived in a separate add-on layer. I also ran joint quarterly roadmap reviews with both segments so each group understood the other's constraints.

*Result:* The core platform shipped on schedule for two consecutive quarters. The government account renewed without needing a custom build, and enterprise clients reported fewer support issues because shared features were more stable.

---

Q: Describe a time you worked with a hardware engineering team and had to manage delays.

*Situation:* I was PM for a new outdoor IP camera model. Three weeks before the planned launch, the hardware team flagged that a key component was stuck in customs and would delay the physical unit by six weeks.

*Task:* I had to decide whether to delay the full launch or find a workaround, and communicate the decision to sales, distributors, and two enterprise clients who had already committed to the timeline.

*Action:* I mapped which parts of the product value were software-only (remote management dashboard, alert configuration) versus hardware-dependent (the physical unit). I proposed launching the software platform on the original date so existing camera users could benefit immediately, with the new hardware shipping six weeks later. I drafted talking points for the sales team and personally called both enterprise clients to explain the plan and offer early software access as a goodwill gesture.

*Result:* Both clients agreed to the phased approach and stayed on contract. The early software launch also surfaced a handful of UI issues before the hardware shipped, so the final product felt more polished at launch.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you had to kill a feature or project.

*Situation:* I was six months into building an analytics dashboard for an enterprise security product. The feature had missed two internal milestones and was consuming a large share of the team's bandwidth.

*Task:* I needed to assess whether to continue, pivot, or cut the project entirely, and bring a clear recommendation to leadership.

*Action:* I gathered three inputs: user interviews with several existing clients (none ranked analytics in their top priorities), a cost review showing the feature had consumed a substantial portion of the annual feature budget, and a competitor analysis confirming analytics was a 'nice to have' rather than a deal-breaker. I wrote a one-page brief recommending we pause the feature, redirect the team to a higher-priority integration that clients had been requesting for months, and revisit analytics in the next planning cycle. I also shared the reasoning with the engineering team directly so they understood the decision, not just the outcome.

*Result:* Leadership approved the pivot quickly. The integration feature launched on time, and several enterprise accounts mentioned it as a factor in their renewal decision.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the foundation for all behavioral questions at Prama India. Keep each part roughly equal in length and speak in the first person. Interviewers want to hear what you specifically did, not what 'the team' did.

RICE for prioritization questions. RICE stands for Reach, Impact, Confidence, and Effort. When asked how you would prioritize a roadmap, walk through each dimension out loud. At Prama India, 'Reach' typically means number of camera units deployed or enterprise accounts affected, not app downloads or monthly active users.

Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD) for user discovery questions. Frame user needs as jobs: 'a security manager at a retail chain needs to review incident footage in under two minutes.' This works well in Prama's B2B context because the 'user' (the security officer on the floor) and the 'buyer' (the IT or procurement head) are often different people with different priorities.

A simple three-step structure for product launch questions: first define success metrics upfront, then identify the riskiest assumption and how you would test it, then describe the go-to-market motion through channel partners and system integrators. Mentioning Prama's distribution model specifically signals that you understand the business.

For market sizing or analytical questions, break the problem into clear segments (enterprise, SME, government), state your assumptions out loud, and arrive at a range rather than a single number. You can anchor on publicly reported data about India's physical security market when relevant.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates report, Prama India PM interviewers typically look for four things above all.

Hardware-software product thinking. Most PM candidates come from pure software backgrounds. Showing that you understand supply chain dependencies, firmware update cycles, SKU management, and the difference between a software bug and a hardware defect will set you apart from the first round.

B2B and government client maturity. Prama's clients are enterprises and government bodies, not individual consumers. Interviewers want to see that you are comfortable with long sales cycles, procurement processes, and the reality that a government client's feature request can sometimes be non-negotiable regardless of your roadmap.

Domain curiosity in security and surveillance. You do not need to be a security expert before day one, but you should have done your homework on Prama's product line, the competitive landscape in Indian surveillance, and how AI is changing video analytics. Interviewers consistently notice candidates who ask well-informed questions.

Structured communication under pressure. PM roles at Prama involve coordinating between sales, hardware engineering, software engineering, and field service teams. Interviewers will test whether you can simplify complex trade-offs into clear decisions that multiple stakeholders can act on.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Know the company and product.

Spend dedicated time on Prama India's website and any publicly available product documentation. Understand their camera lines, VMS platform, and any AI or cloud-connected features they have announced. Note two or three product decisions that seem interesting and be ready to discuss the trade-offs behind them.

Step 2: Prepare your STAR stories.

Write out at least five STAR stories before any round. Cover: roadmap prioritization under resource constraints, cross-functional conflict resolution, a failed or cancelled project, a time you used data to change direction, and a stakeholder management challenge with a difficult client. Practise saying each story out loud in under three minutes.

Step 3: Sharpen your frameworks.

Practise answering prioritization questions using RICE and product launch questions using a structured three-step approach. Focus on B2B and hardware-software scenarios rather than consumer app examples, since consumer-first frameworks will feel off-context at Prama.

Step 4: Prepare smart questions.

Arrive with two or three thoughtful questions for the interviewer about Prama's roadmap direction, how the PM team coordinates with hardware engineering, or how they manage long-term government client relationships. Candidates report that asking sharp, specific questions often leaves a stronger impression than any single answer.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating Prama like a pure software company. Candidates who rely on consumer app frameworks (funnels, DAU, retention curves) without translating them to a hardware-plus-software B2B context tend to fall short. Always anchor your examples to enterprise or government client scenarios.

Vague STAR answers. Saying 'I collaborated with the team' or 'we prioritized based on impact' is not enough. Interviewers want to hear exactly what you did, what trade-off you made, and what the measurable outcome was.

Not knowing Prama's product line. Walking in without exploring Prama's camera and VMS portfolio signals low interest. Spend at least a few hours on their product pages before any round.

Ignoring the channel partner layer. Prama typically sells through system integrators and channel partners rather than directly to end users. Candidates who talk about selling directly to the security manager miss an important layer of how the business actually works.

Jumping to solutions before clarifying the problem. If you start building a feature without first asking about the user, the problem, and the success metric, you signal weak product instinct. Always pause to clarify before proposing anything.

Underestimating compliance and regulatory questions. Surveillance products are increasingly subject to data protection rules and government procurement requirements. If you have experience navigating these, lead with it.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Prama India PM interview typically have?

Candidates report a process that typically runs 3-4 rounds, though this can vary by seniority and team. Rounds commonly cover an initial screening call, a product thinking or case discussion, a behavioral round, and a final conversation with a senior leader. Prama India does not publicly publish a fixed interview structure, so ask your recruiter for the expected format at the start of the process.

Does Prama India give take-home assignments or live case studies?

Candidates have reported both live case discussions and occasional take-home product exercises, depending on the role level. Senior PM rounds are more likely to include a case where you work through a product or business problem in real time. If given a take-home, focus on clarity of thinking and structured reasoning rather than impressing with sheer volume of content.

Do I need a background in security or surveillance technology to get through the interview?

A domain background helps but is not always required, based on what candidates report. What matters more is that you have researched Prama's products and can speak to PM fundamentals in a hardware-software B2B context. Showing genuine curiosity about the security tech space and asking informed questions often compensates for a lack of direct industry experience.

What salary can I expect as a PM at Prama India?

Prama India does not publicly confirm its salary bands. Based on knok jobradar data for PM roles across India, a mid-level PM with 3-6 years of experience typically falls in the 24-40 LPA range, while senior roles trend higher. For company-specific data points, check Glassdoor or levels.fyi alongside your own negotiation research.

How should I prepare for AI-related product questions in a surveillance context?

Prama and the broader security industry are actively integrating AI for video analytics, object detection, and anomaly alerts. You do not need deep ML knowledge, but be ready to discuss how you would define success metrics for an AI feature, how you would treat false positive rates as a product problem, and how you would communicate AI limitations to enterprise clients. Reading publicly reported coverage of AI adoption in India's physical security market before your interview will give you useful grounding.
