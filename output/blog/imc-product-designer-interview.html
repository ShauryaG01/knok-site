01 Overview

Overview

imc is a global proprietary trading and technology firm, known for algorithmic market-making across major financial exchanges worldwide. Unlike consumer-facing tech companies, imc builds and runs its own sophisticated trading systems, risk tools, and developer platforms. Product Designers here work closely with traders, quants, and engineers to create interfaces where accuracy and speed are non-negotiable.

As of July 2026, knok jobradar shows 162 open roles at imc across functions, with Product Designer positions among them. The design work skews heavily toward information density, real-time data display, and workflow efficiency rather than visual polish or brand identity.

Candidates report that the interview process typically includes a portfolio review, a design exercise (sometimes a take-home challenge), and rounds with the design team and cross-functional stakeholders. imc values structured thinking, comfort with complexity, and the ability to work credibly alongside technical colleagues. The exact format varies by team and quarter, so confirm the current structure with your recruiter.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

  1. Walk us through the most complex dashboard or data-heavy interface in your portfolio. What made it complex and how did you handle that?
  1. How do you design for expert users who know their domain far better than you do? How do you earn their trust?
  1. Describe a project where speed and accuracy were both critical for the end user. How did your design support those needs?
  1. Tell us about a time you had to simplify an extremely complicated workflow. What was your process and what tradeoffs did you make?
  1. How do you handle a situation where engineers disagree with your design decisions? Give a specific example.
  1. Describe your approach to conducting user research when your users are busy domain experts with very limited availability.
  1. How do you prioritise which UX problems to solve when you have more requests than bandwidth?
  1. Tell us about a design that did not perform as you expected after launch. What did you learn and what would you do differently?
  1. How do you approach designing for real-time data: latency, refresh rates, status indicators, and error states?
  1. Walk us through how you would design a real-time monitoring tool from a blank canvas. (Live or take-home exercise)
  1. Describe a time you pushed back on a stakeholder request. How did you frame the conversation and what was the outcome?
  1. How do you measure the success of a design change in a product used by internal expert users?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through the most complex dashboard or data-heavy interface in your portfolio.

*Situation:* At my previous company, the operations team tracked live order statuses across multiple vendor integrations using a shared spreadsheet with dozens of columns. Critical updates were regularly missed because the data had no visual hierarchy and everything looked equally urgent.

*Task:* I was asked to redesign this into a proper dashboard that would reduce missed alerts and help the team respond faster to exceptions.

*Action:* I started by shadowing the ops team for a week to identify which data points they actually acted on versus which they simply monitored. I ran a card-sorting session to understand their mental model of priority. I then designed a tiered layout: a summary strip for quick status at a glance, a filterable detail table below it, and a side panel surfacing only items that needed action. I used colour coding sparingly and only for statuses that required a human response.

*Result:* After launch, the team reported a clear drop in missed exceptions and faster response to critical alerts. The ops lead said it was 'the first tool that actually fits how we think.'

---

Q: Describe a time you pushed back on a stakeholder request and how you handled that conversation.

*Situation:* A product manager wanted to add several new data columns to an already dense trading summary screen, based on a single request from one senior trader.

*Task:* My job was to evaluate the request and either implement it or propose an alternative that served the underlying need without degrading the experience for all users.

*Action:* I first interviewed the trader to understand what problem he was actually trying to solve. I then ran a quick survey with a few other traders to check if they shared the same need. Only one other trader wanted those same columns. I brought this data to the PM and proposed a personalisation feature: a column-picker letting each user pin the columns most relevant to their work. I mocked up a lightweight version in a day and shared it with both the PM and the trader in the same meeting.

*Result:* The PM agreed to the personalisation approach. The trader was satisfied because he got exactly the columns he needed. We avoided cluttering the default view for everyone else, and the column-picker later became the foundation for a broader personalisation system.

---

Q: How do you conduct user research when your users are busy domain experts with limited time?

*Situation:* I was designing a new onboarding flow for a developer tool. The primary users were senior engineers who had almost no time available for traditional research sessions.

*Task:* I needed enough insight to make confident design decisions without burning the goodwill of users who were already stretched thin.

*Action:* I replaced long interviews with short ride-along observations where I watched users complete one specific task while they narrated their thinking. I also embedded a lightweight feedback widget in the product that surfaced a single question at natural pause points in the workflow. I complemented this with usage analytics from existing logs to validate what I was hearing qualitatively.

*Result:* I quickly identified three major friction points with clear signal from multiple sources. The engineers appreciated that I respected their time. One of them later volunteered to be a regular design reviewer, giving me a trusted internal advocate for future projects.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For portfolio walkthroughs: Use a Problem, Process, Outcome structure. Open with the business or user problem (not just 'I was asked to design a dashboard'). Describe your specific contribution to the process, not what the team did collectively. Close with a concrete, observable result. imc interviewers typically pay close attention to how you discuss tradeoffs and constraints, so prepare to explain why you made the choices you did.

For 'how would you design X' questions: Start by asking clarifying questions to scope the problem before touching a whiteboard or screen. Then describe how you would understand the users and their core tasks. Only then move to solution ideas, and finish by explaining how you would validate your assumptions. A useful structure: understand users and context, define the core problem, explore solution directions, test with real users.

For behavioural questions: Use STAR: Situation (keep it brief), Task (your specific role), Action (what you personally did, in detail), Result (a measurable or observable outcome). Candidates report that imc interviewers often probe further with 'why did you choose that approach?' or 'what would you do differently?' so prepare your reasoning, not just the story.

For live design exercises: Think aloud throughout. State your assumptions explicitly. Invite feedback as you go. imc interviewers typically want to see your reasoning process, not a polished final answer.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

imc's design team works in a fast-moving, high-stakes environment where their tools directly affect trading performance. Based on what candidates report, interviewers are typically looking for:

Comfort with complexity. Can you design for expert users who work with dense information all day? Do you understand when to simplify and when preserving complexity is the right call for the user?

Structured thinking. Can you break a vague problem into a clear, logical process? Do you ask the right clarifying questions before proposing solutions? Interviewers want to see a designer who thinks before they sketch.

Credibility with engineers. Can you work closely with technical teams, understand their constraints, and earn their trust without losing your design perspective? At imc, design must coexist with a very strong engineering culture.

Evidence-based decisions. Do you use data, research, and user feedback to make design choices, or do you rely primarily on intuition and taste? imc values designers who can show their work and back it up.

Clear communication. Can you explain a design decision to a non-designer in plain, direct language? Can you give and receive feedback without ego?

A strong portfolio for imc typically includes at least one complex, information-dense product (a dashboard, analytics interface, or operational tool) where the design work demonstrably improved efficiency or reduced errors for expert users.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Two to three weeks before:
Review your portfolio and identify your strongest example of a data-heavy or expert-user product. Prepare to walk through it in depth, covering your process and the tradeoffs you made. If your portfolio lacks a relevant example, do a brief self-initiated case study: pick a publicly available financial or analytics tool and redesign one key workflow, documenting your process as you go.

One week before:
Practise STAR answers for common behavioural questions: pushing back on stakeholders, a design that failed, collaborating with engineers, working under ambiguity. Record yourself and listen back to check that your answers are concise and close with a clear result. Read publicly available material about imc's products and culture. Showing genuine interest in how financial systems work (even at a basic level) is noted positively by candidates who have interviewed there.

Two to three days before:
Prepare a handful of questions to ask your interviewers. Strong questions for imc focus on: how the design team earns credibility with engineering, how design impact is measured on internal tools, and what the biggest unsolved design challenges are right now.

Day of the interview:
For any design exercise, resist the urge to jump to solutions. Spend the first few minutes asking clarifying questions and stating your assumptions aloud. imc interviewers typically care more about seeing your reasoning than seeing a polished final design.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

  1. Leading with visuals instead of thinking. imc cares more about your problem-solving process than the visual polish of your screens. Lead with the problem you were solving and the decisions you made. Show screens as evidence of those decisions, not as the centrepiece.
  1. Over-simplifying your examples. Do not frame every project as 'I made it clean and simple.' imc works in genuinely complex domains. Showing that you can engage with real complexity and resolve it meaningfully for users is more impressive than claiming you eliminated it.
  1. Saying 'we' throughout. Interviewers want to know what you personally did. Practise saying 'I designed,' 'I decided,' 'I pushed back' rather than 'we built' or 'the team created.'
  1. Vague outcomes. If your design improved something, say what it improved and by how much. 'It helped the team work faster' is weaker than 'the ops team's exception-resolution time dropped noticeably after we launched.'
  1. Skipping clarifying questions in design exercises. Jumping straight into solutions is the most common red flag in live design rounds. Always pause to ask who the users are, what their core task is, and what success looks like before you start designing.
  1. Treating technical constraints as someone else's problem. imc is an engineering-first company. Candidates who can speak intelligently about performance constraints, data refresh rates, and rendering tradeoffs earn more credibility than those who defer all technical questions to engineers.

What kind of design work does imc focus on for Product Designers?

imc Product Designers typically work on internal tools: trading interfaces, risk dashboards, real-time monitoring systems, and developer platforms. This is not consumer-facing product design. The focus is on helping expert users (traders, quants, engineers) work faster and with fewer errors in high-stakes environments. Candidates report that experience with data-heavy, information-dense products is valued over consumer-app aesthetics or brand-focused work.

Is there a portfolio review or take-home assignment in the process?

Candidates typically report a portfolio walkthrough early in the process where you present one or two projects in depth, covering your process and decision-making rather than just the final screens. Some candidates also report a design exercise, either as a take-home challenge or a live whiteboard session.
