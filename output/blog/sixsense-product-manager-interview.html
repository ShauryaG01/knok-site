02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up frequently in SixSense PM interviews, based on what candidates report across review platforms. Prepare detailed answers for each.

  1. What is the north star metric for SixSense's account engagement platform, and how would you measure product success?
  2. A customer reports that your buyer-intent predictions are frequently wrong. How do you investigate and address this?
  3. How would you prioritise a backlog that includes AI model accuracy improvements, new CRM integrations, and a redesigned dashboard?
  4. Walk us through how you would design a new feature to help sales reps act on SixSense's intent signals faster.
  5. How do you handle competing priorities when the sales team wants more CRM integrations and the marketing team needs better attribution reporting?
  6. SixSense's platform relies on third-party data sources. How do you handle a situation where a key data provider restricts or changes access?
  7. How would you define and measure the ROI that enterprise customers get from SixSense?
  8. Describe a time you shipped a data product or analytics feature. What did you learn from that experience?
  9. How would you improve onboarding for new enterprise customers who are unfamiliar with intent-data concepts?
  10. How would you balance building for SixSense's current mid-market customers versus expanding upmarket into large enterprises?
  11. How do you work with data science teams to validate that a new prediction model is actually useful for end users?
  12. If you had to cut the roadmap significantly, which SixSense feature areas would you deprioritise first and why?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format for all behavioural and experience questions. Each answer should have a concrete situation, a clear task, specific actions you took, and a measurable or observable result.

Q: Tell us about a time you used data to change a product decision.

*Situation:* At my previous company, the sales team kept asking for improvements to our analytics dashboard. I initially assumed the issue was visual design.

*Task:* I needed to decide whether to invest in a UI redesign or dig deeper into the actual problem.

*Action:* I pulled usage analytics and found that most users dropped off before reaching the key insights section. I ran five customer interviews and discovered the real issue was slow load times on large accounts, not the design. I reprioritised the sprint from UI work to backend query optimisation.

*Result:* Load times improved significantly and weekly active usage on that dashboard increased over the next quarter. The sales team stopped escalating the issue, and it became a reference case for how we used data to drive product decisions.

---

Q: Describe a time you managed conflict between two internal stakeholders.

*Situation:* Our sales team wanted a new CRM integration urgently, while the marketing team was pushing for an attribution reporting overhaul. Both had senior sponsors and strong business cases.

*Task:* I had one engineering squad available for the quarter and needed to make a clear call without alienating either team.

*Action:* I mapped each request to revenue impact using existing customer feedback and churn signals. I presented both options to leadership with clear trade-offs. We agreed to build a lightweight CRM sync first (two sprints) and committed the attribution overhaul to the following quarter, with a detailed spec already prepared.

*Result:* The CRM integration helped close two enterprise deals. The marketing team felt heard because they had a firm date and a written spec. Both teams stayed aligned through the rest of the year.

---

Q: How have you worked with ML or data science teams on a product feature?

*Situation:* I was PM for a lead-scoring feature at a B2B SaaS company. The data science team had built a model but customers were not acting on the scores.

*Task:* I needed to understand why adoption was low and work with the team to fix it.

*Action:* I interviewed five customers and found that the scores felt like a black box. Users did not trust outputs they could not explain. I worked with the data science team to add a 'top reasons' explainability layer and ran a pilot with three customer accounts.

*Result:* Customers who saw the explanations used the scores at a much higher rate. The feature became a key selling point in new enterprise demos and was referenced in renewal conversations.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Three frameworks you can apply consistently across SixSense PM interviews:

CIRCLES (for product design questions). Comprehend the situation, Identify the customer, Report the customer needs, Cut through prioritisation, List solutions, Evaluate trade-offs, Summarise. SixSense's platform serves both sales reps and marketing managers, so always clarify which user you are solving for before diving into solutions. Jumping to a feature idea without asking 'who is this for?' is one of the most common ways candidates lose points in these rounds.

Jobs-to-be-Done (for feature prioritisation). Frame every request around what job the customer is trying to accomplish. A sales rep using SixSense is not just 'checking intent scores'; their real job is 'knowing which accounts to call this week.' This framing cuts through noisy feature requests and keeps your reasoning focused on outcomes rather than outputs.

RICE (for backlog prioritisation). Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort. This is widely used in PM interviews and gives you a structured way to talk through trade-offs. Candidates who report using RICE in SixSense interviews note that interviewers appreciate when you connect each score back to customer outcomes rather than treating it as a purely mechanical calculation.

For metrics questions, always name a north star metric first, then layer in guardrail metrics. For SixSense, a reasonable north star could be 'accounts where intent signals led to a qualified pipeline opportunity', with guardrails covering data coverage, prediction accuracy, and user engagement with the signal.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates report, SixSense PM interviewers typically look for four qualities:

Deep product thinking for enterprise and B2B. SixSense is not a consumer app. Interviewers want to see that you understand complex enterprise buying processes, long sales cycles, and products with multiple stakeholders. Show that you know the difference between the economic buyer and the person who uses the product every day.

Comfort with data and AI products. SixSense's core offering is built on predictive analytics and machine learning. You do not need to be a data scientist, but you should be able to discuss how ML models fail, what data quality means for end users, and how to measure success for an AI-driven feature. Candidates who speak to these topics fluently consistently stand out.

Cross-functional leadership without authority. SixSense PMs coordinate across engineering, data science, sales, and marketing. Interviewers look for evidence that you can align teams, manage competing priorities, and make clear calls when stakeholders disagree, all without having direct authority over most of the people involved.

Customer obsession backed by evidence. Saying 'I am customer-focused' is not enough. Bring specific examples of customer research or interviews that changed a product decision. SixSense serves enterprise customers with significant contracts, so PMs are held to a high standard of accountability to customer outcomes.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused plan for the two weeks before your SixSense PM interview:

Week 1: Understand the product and domain.
Spend time with SixSense's public resources, product videos, and available case studies. Understand what 'intent data' means and how B2B companies use it to prioritise sales outreach. Map out the key user personas: the sales rep, the marketing ops manager, and the revenue operations leader. Look for publicly available analyst commentary on the revenue intelligence category to understand the competitive context.

Week 2: Practise answers and frameworks.
Rehearse answers to the 12 questions in this guide using the STAR format. For each answer, check that you have a concrete outcome from your own experience to anchor the result. Practise the RICE framework on a SixSense-style backlog scenario out loud. Do at least one mock interview with a peer or through a PM community group.

Before each round:
Prepare three questions for the interviewer. Strong options include: 'What does success look like in the first six months for a PM here?', 'How does the PM team collaborate with data science?', and 'What is the biggest product challenge the team is focused on right now?' These show genuine curiosity and help you assess whether the role is the right fit.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating SixSense like a consumer product company. The platform is deeply B2B and enterprise-focused. Candidates who rely exclusively on consumer app examples without connecting them to enterprise dynamics come across as underprepared. Bring at least one B2B or SaaS example to every answer.

Ignoring the data and AI layer. Some candidates treat SixSense as a standard SaaS PM role and skip preparation on how data products work. Interviewers typically probe on this. Know how to talk about model accuracy, data freshness, and what happens when a prediction is wrong.

Vague answers on prioritisation. Saying 'I would talk to customers and then decide' is not sufficient. You need a framework, a way to score options, and a clear explanation of what you would say no to. Practise giving specific, structured answers where you name trade-offs out loud.

Skipping clarifying questions in design problems. Jumping straight to a solution without asking who you are building for and what job they are trying to do is a common red flag. Interviewers at SixSense typically expect you to slow down and ask before you propose anything.

Underselling cross-functional examples. SixSense PMs work across engineering, data science, sales, and marketing. If you have examples of aligning technical and non-technical stakeholders, highlight them clearly rather than burying them in vague answers about 'good communication skills'.

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the SixSense PM interview process typically have?

Candidates report that the process typically involves multiple rounds, often starting with a recruiter screen followed by rounds focused on product sense, analytical thinking, and a final panel or leadership discussion. The exact number of rounds can vary by level and team. It is worth asking your recruiter to walk you through the expected structure before your first round.

Does SixSense give a take-home assignment or case study?

Some candidates report receiving a take-home product case or an analytical exercise as part of the process, though this is not universal. Typically, the case involves analysing a product scenario or designing a feature relevant to the SixSense platform. If you receive one, allocate focused time to structure your answer clearly and anchor every recommendation with explicit reasoning.

What salary can I expect for a PM role at SixSense?

Specific SixSense compensation data is not publicly reported in enough detail to cite precisely. How important is prior B2B SaaS experience for this role?

SixSense's platform serves enterprise B2B customers, so prior B2B or SaaS experience is a strong advantage. Candidates without direct B2B experience can still be competitive if they demonstrate understanding of enterprise buying dynamics, multi-stakeholder products, and data-driven decision making. The key is showing that you understand how enterprise products differ from consumer ones, even if your background is primarily consumer.

Do I need to understand machine learning to succeed as a PM at SixSense?

You do not need to be a data scientist, but SixSense's core product is built on predictive AI and intent signals, so comfort with data concepts matters. Interviewers typically want to see that you can discuss how models fail, what data quality means for users, and how to define success metrics for an AI feature. Spending time on the basics of ML product management before your interview will pay off.
