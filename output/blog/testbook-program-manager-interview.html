01 Overview

Overview

Testbook is one of India's leading edtech platforms, built around government exam preparation for SSC, Railways, Banking, and state-level exams. With 96 open roles currently listed, Testbook is actively scaling its tech and product teams. The Technical Program Manager role here is central to shipping features on time, aligning engineering with business goals, and managing complex, multi-team projects.

As of July 2026, there are 313 TPM openings across India. Bangalore leads with 41 openings, followed by Delhi (14), Pune (13), Hyderabad (12), and Chennai (5). The Testbook interview process for a TPM typically involves multiple rounds, and candidates report seeing a mix of technical, program management, and behavioural questions spread across two to four conversations.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up frequently in Testbook TPM interviews, based on candidate reports and the edtech context:

  1. Walk us through a complex, multi-team project you managed end to end. How did you handle dependencies and blockers?
  2. Testbook ships exam content and features on tight schedules before major government exam dates. How do you manage hard deadlines with shifting requirements?
  3. Describe a time you had to push back on a product or business request as a TPM. How did you handle that conversation?
  4. How do you track and communicate project status to senior leadership without overwhelming them with detail?
  5. Tell us about a technical decision you influenced. What was your role and how did you build alignment with engineers?
  6. How do you prioritise when multiple high-priority projects compete for the same engineering bandwidth?
  7. Testbook serves millions of students who rely on the platform during exam season. How have you handled incidents or outages in a high-stakes product environment?
  8. Describe your experience working with data teams or ML-based features. How do you manage uncertainty in delivery timelines?
  9. How do you build trust with engineering teams when you do not own the code yourself?
  10. Give an example of a process improvement you introduced that measurably sped up delivery for your team.
  11. How would you handle a situation where two senior stakeholders disagree on the scope of a feature close to launch?
  12. What metrics do you use to measure the health of a program you are running?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through a complex, multi-team project you managed end to end.

*Situation:* Our platform needed to launch a new adaptive test engine before a major exam cycle. It involved three engineering squads, a content team, and an external vendor for proctoring.

*Task:* My job was to bring all threads together, keep the timeline on track, and surface risks early enough to course-correct without slipping the exam date.

*Action:* I set up a shared project tracker visible to all teams, ran a short daily sync for blockers only (not status updates), and built a dependency map so each team could see where they sat in the critical path. When the vendor integration slipped, I escalated immediately and negotiated a phased rollout so core features launched on time.

*Result:* The launch happened on the agreed date. The phased rollout reduced post-launch issues because we could monitor in stages. Leadership cited the project as a model for future vendor integrations.

---

Q: How do you prioritise when multiple high-priority projects compete for the same engineering bandwidth?

*Situation:* I was managing three concurrent programs, all flagged as P1 by different business owners, with one shared backend team.

*Task:* I needed to create a rational prioritisation without alienating any stakeholder and without asking engineers to context-switch constantly.

*Action:* I facilitated a joint prioritisation session with all business owners, framed around business impact and user harm if delayed, not internal politics. I introduced a simple scoring model covering revenue impact, user impact, and technical risk. We agreed on a sequenced roadmap together.

*Result:* Engineers worked in focused two-week blocks. The two highest-priority programs shipped on time. The third shifted by a few weeks, but the business owner had agreed to that trade-off upfront, so there was no surprise.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you handled a high-stakes incident or outage.

*Situation:* Our platform went down during a mock test session that thousands of students were actively using. It was a weekend afternoon close to a major exam date.

*Task:* I had to coordinate the incident response, keep communication flowing to leadership and support teams, and make sure students were not left without answers.

*Action:* I joined the incident bridge immediately, assigned a single engineer as incident commander, and kept everyone else off the call unless needed. I sent a holding message to the support team to share with students. I tracked time-to-resolution estimates regularly and relayed them to leadership in plain language.

*Result:* The platform was restored within the hour. We ran a structured post-mortem the following week, identified the root cause as a missing circuit breaker, and shipped a fix within the same sprint. Students received a complimentary extension on their test access.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the standard for behavioural questions. Keep the Situation brief, spend most of your time on Action, and always land on a concrete Result.

CIRCLES (Comprehend, Identify, Report, Cut, List, Evaluate, Summarise) works well if you get a product sense or prioritisation question, though it is more commonly used in PM interviews. A simpler version for TPM: define the problem, list options, pick one and defend it.

The 'Why, What, How' frame is useful for technical alignment questions. Why does this project matter (business goal), What are we building (scope and success criteria), How will we get there (execution plan and risk mitigation). This mirrors how Testbook engineering leaders typically think about new initiatives.

For conflict or pushback questions, use a structure of: acknowledge the other person's goal, share your concern with data or logic, propose a path forward. Avoid framing it as 'I was right, they were wrong.' Show that you preserved the relationship while solving the problem.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Testbook interviewers for TPM roles typically look for a few things beyond the basics:

Comfort with ambiguity at scale. Testbook moves fast and exam deadlines are non-negotiable. They want to see that you can make good decisions with incomplete information and escalate the right things at the right time.

Technical credibility without pretending to be an engineer. You do not need to write code, but you should be able to read a system design diagram, ask the right questions about trade-offs, and understand why a delay in the data pipeline affects the front-end feature.

Cross-functional influence. TPMs at edtech companies often have to align content teams, engineering, QA, and product with no direct authority. Interviewers want examples of you building alignment through clarity and trust, not hierarchy.

User empathy tied to the edtech context. Testbook serves students who are often preparing for life-changing exams. Showing that you understand the stakes for end users, not just the engineering teams, tends to resonate strongly.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know the company and role. Use the Testbook app as a student would. Take a free mock test. Review any public engineering or product content the company has shared. Understand what makes their platform different from competitors. Map your experience carefully to the job description requirements.

Week 2: Prepare your stories. Pick six to eight projects from your career that showcase different skills: delivery under pressure, stakeholder conflict, technical decision-making, process improvement, incident handling, and cross-functional alignment. Write them out in STAR format. Keep each story under two minutes when spoken.

Week 3: Sharpen the technical side. Brush up on program management fundamentals: critical path, risk registers, RACI, OKRs, sprint metrics. Review basic system design concepts so you can speak credibly about engineering trade-offs without going deep into code.

Before the interview: Prepare two or three thoughtful questions for your interviewer. Good ones include: 'What does success look like in the first few months for this role?', 'What is the biggest cross-team coordination challenge your TPM team faces right now?', and 'How does the TPM function work alongside product and engineering leadership here?'

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Staying too vague on impact. Saying 'the project went well' is not enough. Interviewers want to understand what changed because of your work, even if you cannot share exact figures. Say 'the team shipped faster than our previous comparable launch' or 'stakeholders stopped asking for status updates because the tracker answered their questions.'

Overplaying the manager angle. TPMs lead without authority. If your stories sound like you were giving orders, that is a red flag. Show influence, facilitation, and alignment, not command-and-control.

Ignoring the edtech context. Generic TPM answers that could apply to any industry miss an opportunity. Where relevant, tie your answer to what matters in edtech: exam schedules, student trust, content accuracy, and peak traffic during exam seasons.

Not asking good questions. Candidates who ask no questions or raise compensation in the first round often leave a weak impression. Save salary conversations for after an offer or when the recruiter raises it.

Rambling in STAR answers. Keep Situation and Task brief. Interviewers are most interested in what you personally did and what happened as a result. Practice cutting your stories to the essential detail.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Testbook TPM interview typically have?

Candidates typically report two to four rounds for TPM roles at Testbook. This usually includes an initial recruiter screen, one or two technical and program management rounds with hiring managers or senior TPMs, and a final conversation with leadership. The exact structure can vary by team and seniority level, so confirm the process with your recruiter early.

Does Testbook expect TPM candidates to have coding skills?

Testbook does not typically expect TPMs to write production code, but technical credibility matters. You should be comfortable discussing system design concepts, understanding engineering trade-offs, reading a basic architecture diagram, and asking sharp questions during technical discussions. Candidates with a prior engineering background often find it easier to build trust with engineering teams quickly.

What salary range should I expect for a TPM role at Testbook?

Testbook does not publicly disclose salary bands for TPM roles. For a sense of market ranges, Glassdoor and levels.fyi have user-reported figures for TPM roles at Indian edtech companies, though sample sizes can be small. Compensation typically depends on your years of experience, the seniority of the role, and your negotiation. Get a current range from the recruiter before the offer stage.

How should I prepare for the technical questions in a Testbook TPM interview?

Focus on system design at a conceptual level rather than deep implementation. Be ready to discuss how you have worked with engineering teams on technical decisions, how you have managed technical debt or risk in a program, and how you communicate technical complexity to non-technical stakeholders. Using the Testbook product yourself before the interview helps you frame answers in their actual context.

Is edtech domain knowledge required for a TPM role at Testbook?

Domain knowledge is helpful but not strictly required. What matters more is your ability to learn the domain quickly and connect it to user impact. If you are coming from a different industry, prepare a short narrative about why you want to move into edtech and how your program management skills transfer. Showing genuine curiosity about the student experience tends to go a long way.

How competitive is the Testbook TPM hiring process?

With 96 open roles currently listed at Testbook and 313 TPM openings across India as of July 2026, there is active hiring in this space. That said, TPM is a specialised role and competition from experienced candidates is real. Differentiate yourself by preparing specific, outcome-focused stories and showing you understand the unique challenges of shipping a high-stakes edtech product.
