02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up frequently in Cloud Engineer interviews at AI-focused companies like Heartbeat AI GmbH, based on candidate reports and the demands of the role.

  1. Design the infrastructure to serve a real-time AI model at low latency. How would you handle traffic spikes while keeping costs under control?
  2. Walk through how you have managed Kubernetes in production. What challenges did you face with scaling, upgrades, or multi-tenancy?
  3. How do you structure a Terraform or Pulumi codebase for a team working across multiple environments such as dev, staging, and prod?
  4. Describe your CI/CD setup for containerised AI workloads. How do you handle large Docker images and long build times?
  5. How do you monitor cloud spend and prevent cost overruns? Which tools have you used and what thresholds do you set?
  6. What is your approach to secrets management in a cloud-native stack? Walk through a real implementation you have set up.
  7. How would you design for high availability and disaster recovery for a stateful service that an AI pipeline depends on?
  8. How do you handle GPU or compute resource scheduling in Kubernetes for training jobs that run on unpredictable schedules?
  9. Describe a production incident you owned end to end. What was the root cause, how did you resolve it, and what changed afterwards?
  10. How do you enforce IAM least privilege and network security policies without slowing down development teams?
  11. When would you choose serverless over container-based compute? Give a concrete example from your own experience.
  12. How do you approach multi-cloud or cloud-agnostic architecture? What are the real trade-offs, not just the theory?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for every behavioural and scenario question. Here are three examples tailored to the Cloud Engineer role.

---

Q: Describe a time you improved the reliability of a critical cloud service.

*Situation:* Our main data ingestion pipeline ran on a single-AZ database instance and had caused two outages in one quarter, each lasting over an hour.

*Task:* I was asked to redesign the setup to eliminate single points of failure without requiring a full rewrite of the application layer.

*Action:* I migrated the database to a Multi-AZ configuration, introduced connection pooling to reduce failover impact on the application, and set up automated failover tests in staging on a fortnightly schedule. I also added alerting on replication lag so the team received early warning before any issue escalated into an outage.

*Result:* The service had zero database-related outages in the two quarters following the change. Failover tests completed consistently within two minutes, giving the team confidence in the setup.

---

Q: How have you managed cloud costs when a team was overspending?

*Situation:* Our cloud bill had grown sharply over several quarters and no one had clear ownership of which services were responsible.

*Task:* I was given two weeks to identify the largest waste areas and propose changes the team could implement without disrupting live services.

*Action:* I introduced a tagging policy enforced via a CI check so every resource had an owner and environment label. I used the cloud provider's cost dashboard to identify idle compute and oversized instances, moved non-production workloads to spot or preemptible instances, and set up scheduled shutdown policies for dev environments outside business hours.

*Result:* The changes produced a meaningful reduction in monthly spend and gave the team ongoing cost visibility they had not had before. The approach was later adopted as a standard practice across other services in the organisation.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you introduced infrastructure-as-code where none existed.

*Situation:* The team I joined had all infrastructure set up manually via the cloud console. There were no repeatable deployment scripts and setting up a new environment took days.

*Task:* I proposed migrating to Terraform and was given a month to prove the concept on one service before the wider team committed to the approach.

*Action:* I started by importing existing resources into Terraform state rather than recreating them, which avoided any downtime risk. I wrote reusable modules for networking, compute, and IAM, and introduced a PR-based workflow where 'terraform plan' output was reviewed before every merge. I ran a short internal session so the rest of the team could follow the same pattern independently.

*Result:* The pilot service was fully managed by Terraform within three weeks. The team adopted the approach for two more services the following month, and new environment setup time dropped from days to a few hours.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the most reliable structure for behavioural and scenario questions. Keep your Situation brief (one or two sentences), spend the most time on Action, and make your Result concrete even when you cannot give exact numbers.

CAR (Context, Action, Result) is a lighter version of STAR. Use it for short, direct questions such as 'how do you handle X.' Describe the context quickly, explain your action, and state what it produced.

Depth-first for technical questions. When asked to design a system, state the constraint you care most about first (latency, cost, or resilience), explain your reasoning, then layer in the components. Interviewers at AI companies typically value your reasoning process more than the specific tools you name.

The 'I not we' rule. Cloud Engineering is almost always team-based, but interviewers need to hear your individual contribution. Replace 'we migrated the cluster' with 'I designed the migration plan and the team executed it together' so your ownership is clear.

Signpost when you are estimating. If asked a capacity or sizing question and you lack exact figures, say 'based on a workload of this type' before your estimate. It signals engineering judgement rather than guessing.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

End-to-end ownership. Growth-stage AI companies like Heartbeat AI GmbH typically want engineers who are accountable for reliability and cost outcomes, not someone who hands problems off to a separate team. Show that you have owned results, not just implementation tasks.

AI infrastructure awareness. Because the company builds AI products, expect questions that connect cloud infrastructure to machine learning needs: model serving latency, GPU scheduling, data pipeline reliability, and experiment reproducibility. You do not need to be a data scientist, but you should understand what ML teams need from the platform.

Security as a default, not an afterthought. Candidates report that questions about IAM, network policies, and secrets management come up early in the process. Weave security thinking into every design answer rather than treating it as a separate topic.

Clear communication across teams. Cloud Engineers at AI companies regularly work with research and product teams who do not speak infrastructure. Interviewers may probe how you explain trade-offs to non-technical stakeholders or how you push back on requests that introduce risk.

Comfort with ambiguity. At a scaling company, requirements change. Interviewers want evidence that you can scope work when the spec is incomplete and make sensible trade-offs rather than waiting for perfect clarity before starting.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Gap analysis

Read the job description carefully and list every tool and concept mentioned. For each item, rate your depth honestly: production experience, side project only, or theory only. Focus your study time on anything listed as required that you have only touched in theory.

Week 2: System design practice

Practise designing two or three AI-relevant infrastructure scenarios out loud: a model serving system with autoscaling, a reliable data pipeline, and a multi-environment IaC setup. Talking through designs as if explaining to an interviewer surfaces gaps in reasoning that reading alone will not catch.

Week 3: Behavioural story bank

Write out five to seven stories from your past work covering reliability improvements, cost optimisation, security decisions, and cross-team collaboration. Map each story to the STAR format and note which question types each story can answer.

Week 4: Mock interviews and logistics

Do at least two mock technical interviews with a peer or a practice partner. Test your audio, video, and screen-sharing setup before the actual rounds, since technical issues in early rounds leave a poor impression. Check the company's public blog or any talks by their engineers to pick up the terminology they use internally.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Using 'we' throughout your answers. Interviewers need to understand your personal contribution. If every sentence uses 'we,' the interviewer cannot tell what you actually did versus what your team did. Be specific about your own role.

Skipping the 'why' on tool choices. Naming Terraform or Kubernetes without explaining why you chose them over alternatives makes answers sound rehearsed. Always add one sentence on the trade-off or problem you were solving.

Treating this as a generic cloud interview. Cloud Engineer interviews at AI companies typically go deeper on GPU resource management, model serving patterns, and data pipeline reliability than a general SRE interview would. Candidates who prepare only for generic cloud topics often get caught out.

Over-engineering system design answers. Proposing a multi-region active-active setup when the interviewer asked for a reliable internal service signals poor judgement. Ask one clarifying question about scale and constraints before you start designing.

Ignoring cost in design answers. At a growth-stage company, cloud cost is a real and immediate concern. A system design answer that never mentions cost trade-offs suggests you have only worked with unlimited budgets.

Not preparing questions for the interviewer. Asking nothing at the end signals low interest. Prepare two or three specific questions about the team's current infrastructure challenges or how they handle on-call to show genuine engagement.
