01 Overview

Overview

FranConnect is a B2B SaaS company that builds franchise management software used by franchise brands to manage operations, field audits, learning, and franchise development. Product Designers at FranConnect work on tools used by two distinct audiences: franchisors (the corporate brand team) and franchisees (individual outlet owners). Each group has different goals, permissions, and software comfort levels, which makes the design work genuinely complex.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

The questions below are grounded in FranConnect's product context. Franchise management involves complex workflows, field operations, and two very different user types, so expect questions that test your B2B thinking, not just your visual craft.

  1. Walk us through a complex B2B workflow you designed from scratch. How did you figure out what users actually needed?
  2. FranConnect serves both franchisors and franchisees in the same platform. How do you design when two very different user types share the same interface?
  3. Describe a time you simplified a data-heavy dashboard for users who are not particularly tech-savvy.
  4. How do you conduct user research when direct access to end users is limited or infrequent?
  5. Tell us about a situation where you disagreed with a product manager or stakeholder on a design direction. How did you handle it?
  6. How do you approach building or contributing to a design system for an enterprise product?
  7. Walk us through a feature you shipped. How did you measure whether the design was successful after launch?
  8. How do you design for accessibility in enterprise SaaS, where users may be on the product for hours each day?
  9. Describe a time you had to make a design decision with incomplete or no user data. What did you do?
  10. If you could improve one thing you noticed in FranConnect's current product, what would it be and why?
  11. Tell me about a time you worked closely with engineers during implementation and something changed from your original design.
  12. How do you prioritize design work when multiple teams or stakeholders have competing requests?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

These three answers follow the STAR format. Adapt the specifics to your own experience.

Q: Describe a time you simplified a complex workflow for non-technical users.

*Situation:* At my previous company, I was redesigning a compliance audit form that field managers filled out during store visits. The original form had dozens of fields on a single scrollable page, and managers frequently abandoned it halfway through.

*Task:* My job was to reduce abandonment and make the form completable during a live store walk, on a tablet, often with poor connectivity.

*Action:* I ran contextual interviews with field managers, watching them do actual audits. I mapped the workflow step by step and found that three categories of questions were always skipped because they required reference materials the manager did not have on hand. I restructured the form into short sections tied to physical zones of the store, added offline support, and built in a 'flag for later' option for those tricky categories.

*Result:* After the redesign shipped, completion rates improved (tracked internally by the product team). Managers said in follow-up sessions that the new flow felt 'natural' rather than like paperwork. The product lead cited improved data completeness in the next quarterly review.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you pushed back on a stakeholder request.

*Situation:* A product manager wanted to add a bulk-action panel to our franchisor dashboard by expanding a sidebar, which would have collapsed the main data table that users spent most of their time in.

*Task:* I needed to make the case for a different approach without dismissing the PM's goal, which was valid: helping franchisors act on multiple records faster.

*Action:* I prepared two prototype options (the PM's preferred layout and an inline multi-select approach that kept the table full-width) and set up a short session with the PM and two available power users. Both users navigated the inline version faster and called it 'less cluttered.'

*Result:* The PM agreed to go with the inline approach. Having users in the room, even briefly, made the conversation much easier than arguing with wireframes alone.

---

Q: Describe a time something changed from your original design during engineering implementation.

*Situation:* I had designed a multi-step onboarding flow for new franchisees with animated transitions to convey progress. Engineering flagged that the animation library caused performance issues on lower-end Android devices, which a meaningful portion of our franchisee users relied on.

*Task:* I needed an alternative that preserved the sense of progress quickly, since the sprint was nearly done.

*Action:* I worked with the lead engineer to identify the exact bottleneck. We found that a simple CSS progress bar with a step-highlight gave the same wayfinding benefit at a fraction of the rendering cost. I updated the Figma file and documented the decision in the design system notes so future contributors understood the constraint.

*Result:* The flow shipped on time, worked smoothly on lower-end devices, and the PM appreciated that we caught it before QA. It taught me to ask about device constraints much earlier in the process.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For B2B portfolio walkthroughs: State the business problem first, not just the UX problem. Name the two or three users affected. Show your process: research, wireframes, and key iterations. Quantify the result if you can; describe qualitative signals honestly if you cannot.

For 'how do you measure success' questions: Name the specific metric tied to your design goal (task completion rate, time on task, error rate, or feature adoption). Explain how you set a baseline before launch. Describe how you tracked post-launch results, whether through product analytics, follow-up user sessions, or support ticket trends.

For 'two different user types' questions: Acknowledge the tension directly rather than glossing over it. Talk about how you mapped each persona's goals and permissions separately. Mention how you validated with both groups, since a workflow that feels obvious to a franchisor's ops manager can be completely confusing to a franchisee with limited software experience.

For stakeholder pushback questions: Lead with the goal you shared with the stakeholder, not the conflict. Show that you used data or user evidence rather than personal opinion to shift the direction. Interviewers at enterprise product companies look for designers who can influence without formal authority.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

B2B and enterprise SaaS experience. FranConnect's users are not casual consumers. They are ops managers, franchise owners, and field auditors using the tool as part of their daily work. Interviewers want to see that you have designed for professional users with high task frequency and low tolerance for confusion.

Systems thinking. Franchise platforms involve overlapping workflows, role-based permissions, and shared data views. Expect questions that test whether you think in components and patterns, not just individual screens.

Comfort with data-heavy interfaces. Tables, filters, audit trails, and dashboards are core to this product. If your portfolio is heavy on clean marketing-site work and light on complex UI, be ready to explain how you approach data density.

Stakeholder communication skills. Product Designers at FranConnect work alongside product managers, engineers, and franchise operations experts. Interviewers probe for how you handle disagreement and how you bring non-designers along in the design process.

Genuine curiosity about the franchise domain. You do not need prior franchise industry experience. Candidates who have clearly explored the product and can articulate what franchisors versus franchisees need stand out from those who treat it as a generic enterprise SaaS role.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

  1. Explore the product. Spend time with FranConnect's publicly available product pages, screenshots, and help documentation. Get familiar with their core modules: operations management, field activity tracking, and franchise development. This lets you speak specifically about their context rather than generic SaaS patterns.
  1. Prepare four STAR stories. Cover: simplifying a complex workflow, navigating a stakeholder disagreement, contributing to a design system, and making a design decision with limited data. Four well-rehearsed stories will cover most questions you face.
  1. Have a product opinion ready. The question 'what would you improve in our product?' comes up often, candidates report. Pick one specific thing you noticed and be ready to explain the user problem it solves, not just the visual change.
  1. Review accessibility basics. Enterprise SaaS users are on the product all day. Interviewers at serious B2B product companies care whether designers think about colour contrast, keyboard navigation, and screen reader compatibility.
  1. Frame your portfolio for enterprise users. If your strongest case studies are consumer apps, add context that bridges your learning to a B2B setting. Explain what you would do differently for a professional user versus a casual one.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

  1. Showing only consumer or mobile-first work. FranConnect's product is desktop-first and data-dense. If your portfolio leads with consumer apps, bridge the context explicitly, otherwise interviewers will wonder if you can handle complex, role-based interfaces.
  1. Describing 'what' without explaining 'why.' Saying 'I redesigned the dashboard' tells the interviewer nothing useful. Lead with the business problem and the user pain before showing screens.
  1. Not researching the franchise domain. Franchise management has specific workflows (royalty tracking, field audits, franchise development pipelines) that differ from generic SaaS. Using the right vocabulary signals that you did your homework.
  1. Giving vague collaboration answers. 'I work well with cross-functional teams' is not a story. Have a specific example of navigating a disagreement with a PM or unblocking an engineer, with a real outcome.
  1. Treating a take-home exercise as a visual test. Interviewers evaluate your problem framing and decision-making first, and visual execution second. Show your thinking, including what you considered and ruled out.
  1. Not asking questions at the end. Candidates who ask nothing signal low interest. Prepare two or three questions about how the design team gathers feedback from franchisee users or how design collaborates with franchise operations experts during discovery.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many interview rounds does FranConnect typically have for a Product Designer role?

Candidates report the process typically runs 3-4 rounds. This usually includes a recruiter or HR screen, a portfolio review with the hiring manager or design team, a design exercise (take-home or live), and a final cross-functional discussion. The exact number can vary by seniority level and team, so it is worth asking your recruiter for a rough timeline upfront.

Is there a take-home design exercise?

Many Product Designer candidates at FranConnect report receiving a design exercise as part of the process, either as a take-home brief or a live whiteboard session. The prompt typically involves solving a workflow or product problem specific to franchise management or enterprise SaaS. Interviewers evaluate your problem framing and rationale as much as the final screens, so document your thinking clearly, including what you considered and ruled out.

What design tools does FranConnect use?

Candidates commonly mention Figma as the primary design tool. The specific stack can change, so it is worth asking your recruiter early in the process. Having solid Figma skills is a safe baseline for any enterprise SaaS design role in India right now, regardless of the company.

How important is prior B2B or franchise industry experience?

You do not need prior franchise industry experience. What matters more is comfort with enterprise UX: complex permissions, data-heavy interfaces, and professional users with high task frequency. That said, candidates who demonstrate an understanding of the franchisor vs. franchisee distinction during the interview typically stand out from those who treat it as a generic role.

What salary can a Product Designer expect at FranConnect?

FranConnect does not publicly list salary bands, and verified company-specific data is limited. Actual offers depend on your experience, interview performance, and internal bands, which vary by company.

Should I prepare questions to ask the interviewer?

Yes, and it matters more than most candidates expect. Good questions for a Product Designer role at FranConnect include asking how the design team gathers feedback from franchisee users, or how design collaborates with franchise operations experts during product discovery. These questions show domain curiosity and product thinking without claiming expertise you do not have.
