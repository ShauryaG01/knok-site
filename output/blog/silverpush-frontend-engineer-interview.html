01 Overview

Overview

Silverpush is a global adtech company building AI-powered contextual advertising and campaign management products. Their engineering teams work on data-heavy dashboards, real-time reporting UIs, and ad delivery tooling used by major brands and agencies worldwide. A Frontend Engineer here is expected to care deeply about rendering performance, because slow UIs directly affect client outcomes in an ad-serving context.

With 16 open Frontend Engineer roles currently listed on knok jobradar, Silverpush is one of the more active adtech hirers right now. The interview process typically runs 3-4 rounds: an initial recruiter or hiring manager screening, a coding assignment or live coding session, one or two technical rounds on JavaScript and React, and a final discussion with a senior engineer or team lead. Candidates report that performance optimisation, component architecture, and React internals come up consistently across rounds.

Salary bands for Frontend Engineers in India from knok jobradar data: entry level (0-2 years) sits at 5-11 LPA, mid level (3-5 years) at 12-22 LPA, senior (6-9 years) at 24-40 LPA, and lead or staff roles at 38-58+ LPA. Silverpush-specific numbers vary; use these as market reference points and check Glassdoor or levels.fyi for more data points before your offer call.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates at Silverpush report these questions appearing most often across technical and behavioural rounds:

  1. How would you build a high-performance ad banner component that must not block the main thread?
  2. Walk me through how React's reconciliation algorithm works. Where does the virtual DOM approach fall short?
  3. What is the difference between useEffect and useLayoutEffect, and when would you choose one over the other?
  4. How would you design a live campaign analytics dashboard that shows real-time metrics without hammering the browser?
  5. What strategies do you use to reduce JavaScript bundle size in a large React application?
  6. How do you handle cross-browser rendering differences, especially for video and rich media ad units?
  7. Describe a production frontend performance bug you debugged. What tools did you use, and how did you confirm the fix?
  8. How would you implement lazy loading for images and iframes on a page that also loads third-party ad scripts?
  9. What is the trade-off between server-side rendering and client-side rendering for a campaign reporting page?
  10. How do you design a component library that multiple product teams can use without tight coupling?
  11. How would you ensure accessibility in a dashboard that non-technical marketing users will navigate every day?
  12. How do you measure and report Core Web Vitals (LCP, CLS, INP) in an environment where third-party ad scripts are also running?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How have you optimised a React component for performance?

*Situation:* At my previous company, a campaign reporting table rendered a large set of rows and caused noticeable lag whenever a user changed date filters.

*Task:* I was asked to reduce the time-to-interactive without a full rewrite of the feature.

*Action:* I profiled the component with React DevTools Profiler and found that every filter change triggered a full re-render of all rows, even unchanged ones. I wrapped the row component in React.memo, moved the filter computation into a useMemo hook keyed to the filter value, and replaced the full list with a windowed renderer using react-window so only visible rows stayed in the DOM.

*Result:* Filter response became noticeably faster based on profiler readings, and the team shipped the change in a single sprint. The pattern became our standard approach for any large data table going forward.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you worked with a designer on a tight deadline.

*Situation:* A client demo for a major brand campaign was scheduled for a Friday, and the design spec for a new heatmap visualisation was handed to me on the Monday of that week.

*Task:* I had four days to build a pixel-accurate, interactive heatmap that could overlay on a video ad player.

*Action:* I set up a short daily sync with the designer to catch visual mismatches early instead of finding them the day before the demo. I built the canvas overlay as a standalone component first so it could be tested independently, and I used mock data from the product manager to validate the colour scale logic before the real data pipeline was connected.

*Result:* The demo ran on schedule, the client approved the feature, and the component shipped to production with only minor tweaks. The daily sync habit was something the wider team adopted for other deadline-driven work.

---

Q: Describe a time you had to learn a new technology quickly to deliver a feature.

*Situation:* My team needed to embed a custom video player inside a programmatic ad unit, and the spec required the Web Components API, which I had not used in production before.

*Task:* I had two weeks to deliver a working prototype that the marketing team could test with real advertisers.

*Action:* I spent the first two days reading the spec and building a throwaway prototype to understand shadow DOM boundaries and event bubbling. I then designed the component's external API to mirror our existing React props patterns so colleagues could use it without learning Web Components. I also wrote a short doc on the gotchas I found, specifically around events not bubbling across shadow DOM boundaries.

*Result:* The prototype was ready with time to spare, passed QA within the remaining days, and the doc I wrote was used by two other engineers who later extended the component.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

The STAR structure (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the baseline for every behavioural question. Keep Situation and Task brief, invest most of your time in Action, and always close with a concrete Result. Vague endings like 'it went well' leave interviewers unconvinced.

For technical questions, follow a three-step pattern: state your understanding of the problem, walk through your approach while naming the trade-offs you considered, then land on your preferred solution and explain why. Silverpush interviewers typically value engineers who acknowledge limits over those who bluff.

For system or architecture questions, think out loud in layers. Start with the user experience (what should the UI feel and respond like), move to component structure and state management, then address data flow, and finish with performance or scalability. In an adtech context, mentioning Core Web Vitals, render budgets, or bundle impact signals the right instincts to a Silverpush interviewer.

For debugging scenarios, name your tools before your conclusions. Saying 'I opened Chrome DevTools, went to the Performance tab, and recorded a trace' is more credible than jumping straight to the fix. Your process matters as much as the answer.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Silverpush builds products for global advertisers, so interviewers are looking for engineers who treat performance as a core deliverable, not an optional polish step. Candidates report being asked to quantify improvements rather than describe them in vague terms.

Strong product intuition. Silverpush's end users are marketers, not engineers. Candidates who can frame technical decisions in terms of campaign outcomes, page load impact on ad revenue, or client experience tend to make a stronger impression than those who talk only in code.

React and JavaScript depth. The team uses React extensively across their products. Expect questions that go beyond surface-level hooks knowledge into rendering behaviour, reconciliation, and state management trade-offs.

Comfort with data-heavy UIs. Dashboards and reporting interfaces are central to the product. Familiarity with charting libraries, canvas rendering, or efficient list rendering is a genuine plus.

Collaboration across functions. Silverpush teams are cross-functional and candidates report that behavioural questions specifically probe how you work with designers and product managers, not just other engineers.

Ownership and initiative. Interviewers look for engineers who have shipped features end-to-end, caught their own issues before code review, and proactively flagged risks to the team.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Core JavaScript and React fundamentals
Review closures, the event loop, prototypal inheritance, and async/await patterns. For React, focus on the rendering lifecycle, hooks internals, and controlled versus uncontrolled components. Build one small project that fetches data, renders a filterable list, and handles loading and error states cleanly.

Week 2: Performance and tooling
Practice with Chrome DevTools Performance and Network panels. Read about Core Web Vitals (LCP, CLS, INP) and how to measure them programmatically. Set up a small Webpack or Vite project, add code splitting, and compare bundle output before and after. Practice explaining what you see in a flame chart.

Week 3: System thinking and adtech context
Read about how programmatic advertising works at a high level: what a DSP does, how ad auctions run, and why page load speed affects ad revenue. You do not need to be an adtech expert, but asking an informed question in the final round signals genuine interest and separates you from candidates who did no research.

Before each round:
Prepare two or three STAR stories covering a performance optimisation, a cross-team collaboration situation, and a time you handled ambiguity or a missing spec. Map your experience to each bullet point in the job description word for word.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Saying what you used without saying why. Answering 'I used Redux for state management' without explaining why you chose it over Context API or Zustand for that specific use case is a common miss. Silverpush interviewers typically probe the trade-off, not just the choice.

Treating performance as optional. Candidates who describe solutions without mentioning rendering cost, bundle impact, or behaviour at scale tend not to progress at Silverpush. Build the habit of adding one performance observation to every technical answer.

Vague behavioural answers. Saying 'I am a good team player' or 'I always communicate clearly' without a specific story does not land. Prepare concrete examples with measurable or visible outcomes.

Staying silent at the end of the round. Candidates report that Silverpush interviewers welcome genuine questions about the product roadmap, how the team handles technical debt, or what the biggest frontend challenge is right now. Silence reads as disengagement.

Bluffing on unknowns. If you have not used the Web Performance API or are unfamiliar with shadow DOM, say so and describe how you would approach learning it. Intellectual honesty is reportedly valued more than false confidence in Silverpush interviews.

Underestimating the take-home. Candidates report that submissions are evaluated on code organisation, naming clarity, edge case handling, and whether a README explains key decisions, not just whether the code runs.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Silverpush Frontend Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates typically report 3-4 rounds: a recruiter or hiring manager screening call, a coding assignment or live coding session, one or two deep technical rounds on JavaScript and React, and a final conversation with a senior engineer or team lead. The exact structure varies by team and level, so ask your recruiter to walk you through the format at the start of the process.

Is the coding round a take-home or a live session?

Candidates report both formats, depending on the role and hiring team. Some describe a take-home assignment of a few hours building or extending a React component, while others report a live shared-editor session. Either way, expect to explain your choices out loud, not just submit working code. Clean structure and a brief README on your decisions tend to matter as much as correctness.

What salary can I expect as a Frontend Engineer at Silverpush?

Publicly reported and Glassdoor figures for Silverpush vary by team and negotiation, so treat the knok jobradar bands as a market reference: entry level (0-2 years) broadly falls at 5-11 LPA, mid level (3-5 years) at 12-22 LPA, and senior (6-9 years) at 24-40 LPA. Check levels.fyi and recent Glassdoor reviews before your offer call for more current data points, and come prepared to negotiate based on your specific experience.

Do I need prior adtech experience to be hired as a Frontend Engineer at Silverpush?

No. Candidates report that Silverpush does not expect deep adtech domain knowledge at the point of hiring. Strong React fundamentals, performance awareness, and a willingness to learn the business context are what matter most. That said, spending a few hours reading about programmatic advertising basics before your final round will help you ask sharper questions and show genuine interest in the company's work.

How much system design should a mid-level candidate expect at Silverpush?

Candidates at the mid level report that system design questions are focused on component architecture, state management choices, and how to structure a dashboard feature, rather than full distributed systems design. For senior and lead roles, expect more depth on shared component library design, frontend scalability, and how the UI layer integrates cleanly with backend APIs and data pipelines.

What makes a take-home assignment stand out at Silverpush?

Candidates who report positive outcomes describe writing clean, well-named code with a short README covering key decisions, handling edge cases such as empty states, loading, and errors, and adding a note on how they would improve the solution with more time. Since Silverpush works on data-heavy UIs, showing you can handle a list or table that scales gracefully with large datasets tends to make a strong impression.
