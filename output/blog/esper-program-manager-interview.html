01 Overview

Overview

Esper is a B2B SaaS company that builds Android device management and fleet management infrastructure for enterprises. A Technical Program Manager at Esper works across engineering, product, customer success, and solutions teams to drive complex programs from kick-off to delivery.

Knok jobradar (as of July 2026) shows Esper has 14 open roles in India, reflecting active hiring across technical functions. The TPM role is among them, and competition is real.

Candidates report that the interview process typically includes a recruiter screen, a hiring manager conversation, at least one technical or case-based discussion, and a behavioral panel. Round count and order may change, so confirm the structure with your recruiter before you begin. The overall bar tests whether you can think technically enough to earn engineer trust, manage stakeholders under ambiguity, and run programs at scale without being hand-held.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up frequently in Esper TPM interviews, based on candidate reports and the nature of the role:

  1. How do you manage a program that spans multiple engineering teams with competing priorities?
  2. Walk us through how you structure OKRs or milestones for a multi-quarter program.
  3. Esper's product spans Android firmware, platform services, and cloud infrastructure. How do you coordinate across those layers without deep expertise in each?
  4. Tell us about a time a key engineering dependency was delayed. How did you handle it?
  5. How do you distinguish a risk from an issue, and how do you track both?
  6. Describe your approach to communicating program status to engineers versus to senior leadership.
  7. How do you handle scope creep once a program is already in flight?
  8. How do you measure program success beyond on-time delivery?
  9. Tell us about a time you had to push back on a product or business request because the technical cost was too high.
  10. How do you onboard yourself to a new technical domain when you join a company?
  11. Describe a situation where you had to make a difficult trade-off between speed and quality.
  12. How do you keep a distributed, cross-functional team aligned week-to-week?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell us about a time a key engineering dependency was delayed and threatened your launch date.

*Situation:* At my previous company, we were shipping a new fleet enrollment feature. Midway through the program, the platform team flagged that a critical API they were building would slip by several weeks due to an unexpected architectural issue.

*Task:* My job was to protect the overall program while keeping leadership and the customer-facing team informed and not panicking.

*Action:* I mapped every downstream feature against the delayed API to find which ones could proceed independently. I worked with the product manager to propose a phased launch: ship the independent features first and follow with the API-dependent ones in the next release cycle. I set up a short daily check-in with the platform team lead to catch any further slippage early, and rewrote the executive status update to explain the revised scope and the reasoning behind it.

*Result:* The first phase shipped on the original date. The platform-dependent features followed in the subsequent release cycle. Stakeholders told me the proactive communication helped them plan customer messaging in advance.

---

Q: How do you communicate program status to engineers versus to senior leadership?

*Situation:* I was running a cross-functional program at a SaaS company involving multiple engineering squads and with direct visibility at the VP level.

*Task:* I needed both audiences to stay informed without creating noise for either group.

*Action:* I built separate artifacts for each audience. For leadership, a concise weekly update with traffic-light status, top risks, and decisions needed from them. For engineers, a living tracker with task-level status, blockers, and clear owners. I ran a short weekly engineering sync to surface blockers early and a separate monthly review with leadership for strategic decisions.

*Result:* Engineers reported fewer ad-hoc interruptions from leadership because questions were being answered before they were asked. The VP used the format to make a resourcing call quickly without needing a separate deep-dive meeting.

---

Q: Describe a time you identified a risk before it became a blocker.

*Situation:* We were rolling out a device enrollment feature to a large enterprise customer. During a pre-launch review, I noticed their IT policy required a specific certificate configuration that our standard onboarding documentation did not address.

*Task:* I needed to close the gap without pushing back the go-live date.

*Action:* I escalated to the solutions engineering and security teams the same day, pulled together a working session, and drafted a supplementary configuration guide for the customer. I also updated the standard onboarding checklist so future enterprise rollouts would catch this step automatically.

*Result:* The customer went live on schedule. The updated checklist was adopted for all subsequent enterprise onboardings, preventing the same gap from recurring.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use STAR every time, but keep it tight. Interviewers lose the thread when Situation and Task run long. Get to the Action quickly, using a sentence or two for context. Spend most of your time on what you specifically did, not what the team did collectively.

Show your thinking, not just the outcome. Esper interviewers want to understand how you make decisions under ambiguity. Say 'I chose option A over option B because...' rather than jumping straight to the result.

Be specific about your role. Use 'I' when describing your actions and 'we' only when describing shared outcomes. Ambiguous ownership is a red flag in TPM interviews.

Quantify where you honestly can. If you have real impact numbers from your own experience, use them. If not, describe the qualitative outcome clearly. Do not invent numbers to sound impressive.

Tailor to Esper's domain. When possible, frame your examples around hardware-software coordination, enterprise customers, or multi-layer technical systems. This shows you understand what makes the Esper TPM role different from a generic PM or project management position.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Esper TPM interviewers typically look for a combination of technical credibility, program rigor, and stakeholder influence.

Technical credibility: You do not need to write code, but you must understand how software systems are built well enough to spot risks, question estimates, and earn engineer trust. Familiarity with APIs, release cycles, or device management concepts is a plus.

Program discipline: Candidates report that interviewers probe for real structure, such as how you track dependencies, how you run status meetings, and how you escalate. Vague answers about 'keeping everyone aligned' without concrete mechanisms tend to score low.

Stakeholder influence without authority: TPMs at Esper work with teams they do not manage. Interviewers want evidence that you can drive alignment, surface trade-offs, and get decisions made without relying on org chart authority.

Proactive risk management: Esper operates in a space where a firmware bug or a compliance gap can block a customer deployment. Interviewers value candidates who build risk visibility into their programs, not those who surface problems only after they become crises.

Customer orientation: Because Esper's product is used in enterprise environments, TPMs often interact with or are accountable to customer timelines. Showing awareness of how your program decisions affect the customer experience is a strong signal.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Understand Esper's product. Spend time with Esper's public documentation, engineering blog, and any available customer case studies. Know what Android device management means in practice, who their customers are, and what problems they solve. You should be able to explain their product clearly in an interview.

Step 2: Map your experience to the role. List your past programs and annotate each with the technical domain, the teams involved, the risks you managed, and the outcomes you drove. Select your strongest stories and practice them using the STAR structure.

Step 3: Prepare for technical questions. You will likely be asked how you coordinate across software and hardware boundaries. Think through how you have worked with platform engineers, infrastructure teams, or vendor dependencies in the past.

Step 4: Research Esper's engineering culture. Look at their engineering blog and LinkedIn posts from current employees. This gives you material to ask thoughtful questions in each round, such as how they handle dependencies between firmware and cloud releases.

Step 5: Prepare questions for each round. Asking thoughtful questions signals genuine interest. Good topics for a TPM interview at Esper include how they measure program success, what the biggest coordination challenges are across their engineering org, and how TPMs are involved in customer-facing decisions.

Step 6: Practice out loud. Run your answers with a peer or record yourself. TPM interviews reward clarity and concision. If an answer rambles past a comfortable listening length, cut it down.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Being too vague about your role. Saying 'we delivered the project on time' tells the interviewer nothing about what you did. Always specify your individual contribution.

Skipping the risk management angle. Many candidates describe programs that went smoothly. Interviewers learn more from how you handled something that went wrong or nearly went wrong. Prepare at least one story where you caught or managed a real problem.

Treating the technical questions as optional. Some candidates assume a TPM role means they can avoid technical depth. At Esper, where the product involves firmware, Android OS, and cloud services, you need to show enough technical understanding to work credibly with those teams.

Over-explaining the Situation. A long wind-up before you get to what you actually did is a common interview mistake. Keep context brief and get to your actions and decisions quickly.

Not asking questions. Candidates who ask no questions at the end of a round often leave interviewers with doubts about genuine interest. Prepare specific, thoughtful questions for each round.

Confusing PM and TPM responsibilities. Technical Program Managers drive program execution and cross-team coordination. Product Managers own the roadmap and feature decisions. If you blur this line in your answers, interviewers may question whether you understand the scope of the role.

How many rounds does Esper's TPM interview typically have?

Candidates report a process that typically includes a recruiter screen, a hiring manager conversation, a technical or case-based round, and a behavioral panel. The exact number of rounds can vary by team and role level. Confirm the structure directly with the recruiter after your first call so you can prepare for each stage.

Does Esper expect TPM candidates to have device management or MDM experience?

Domain experience is valued but not always required. Candidates report that Esper interviewers care more about your ability to learn a new technical domain quickly and coordinate across hardware-software boundaries. If you have experience in adjacent areas like enterprise SaaS, embedded systems, or platform engineering programs, highlight how that experience translates. Show that you can ramp up on unfamiliar technology by explaining how you have done it before.

Is the Esper TPM interview more technical or more behavioral?

Candidates report it is a mix of both. You will likely face behavioral questions using the STAR format and at least one round that probes how you think about technical trade-offs, dependencies, and risks. You do not need to write code, but you should be comfortable discussing APIs, release cycles, and system architecture at a conceptual level.

How should I prepare for case-based or scenario questions in the Esper TPM interview?

Treat case questions the same way you treat behavioral ones: think out loud, structure your reasoning, and be explicit about trade-offs. Interviewers want to see how you identify risks, prioritize work, and communicate decisions, not whether you arrive at a perfect answer. Practice with scenarios like a delayed dependency, a scope change request, or a misalignment between engineering teams.
