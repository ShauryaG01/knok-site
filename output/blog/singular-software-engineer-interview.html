01 Overview

Overview

Singular is a marketing intelligence and mobile attribution platform. Their engineering team builds the systems that help app marketers track where their installs and revenue come from, processing events across hundreds of ad networks at scale. If you are interviewing here, expect strong emphasis on data pipelines, distributed systems, and backend engineering.

The interview process typically runs 3-4 rounds: an HR screen, a coding round focused on data structures and algorithms, a system design round, and a hiring manager or team conversation. Candidates report the end-to-end process taking 2-4 weeks. Singular currently has 18 open Software Engineer roles on knok jobradar (as of July 2026), so there is consistent hiring activity.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly in Singular Software Engineer interviews, based on what candidates report:

  1. Design a high-throughput event ingestion pipeline that receives large volumes of mobile attribution events per day. How do you handle backpressure, deduplication, and ordering?
  1. Explain idempotency in distributed systems. How would you make a conversion event handler idempotent so that duplicate events from an ad network do not corrupt your data?
  1. You have a SQL query on a very large table that is running slowly. Walk us through your optimization approach step by step.
  1. How does last-click mobile attribution work? If a user sees an ad on one network, then installs an app two days later after seeing a second ad, how would your system decide which source gets credit?
  1. How do you handle schema evolution in a data pipeline so downstream consumers do not break when you add, remove, or rename fields?
  1. What is the difference between Apache Kafka and a traditional message queue? When would you choose one over the other?
  1. Walk me through a production incident you debugged. How did you detect it, narrow it down, fix it, and prevent a repeat?
  1. Design a rate-limiting system for an API serving high traffic. What algorithm would you use, and how would you make it work across multiple servers?
  1. How do you ensure data quality in a pipeline that ingests records from third-party ad networks that sometimes send duplicates or malformed data?
  1. Tell me about a time you disagreed with a technical decision your team made. What did you do?
  1. How would you approach migrating a high-traffic monolithic service to microservices without causing downtime for users?
  1. What observability setup and metrics would you put in place to monitor the health of a real-time data pipeline?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR method (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for behavioral questions. Here are three examples tailored to what Singular interviewers focus on:

---

Q: Tell me about a time you debugged a critical production issue.

*Situation:* Our event processing service started dropping incoming attribution events during peak traffic. Downstream dashboards were showing data gaps, and our on-call alert fired in the early hours of the morning.

*Task:* I was the on-call engineer. I needed to identify the root cause, stop the data loss, and restore normal processing without losing in-flight events.

*Action:* I pulled the service logs and noticed a spike in 'connection timeout' errors to our database. I checked the connection pool settings and found we had a hard cap that was fine at normal load but was exhausting under the traffic spike. I temporarily increased the pool size, which stopped the drops immediately. I then traced why traffic had spiked: a partner had sent a bulk retry of two days of events all at once. I added a per-partner rate limiter and opened a ticket to make the connection pool size configurable via environment variable.

*Result:* Data loss stopped within minutes. We recovered the dropped events from the partner's retry batch. The rate limiter prevented a repeat. I documented a runbook so the next on-call engineer would not start from scratch.

---

Q: Describe a situation where you improved the performance of a slow system.

*Situation:* Our reporting queries on a large events table were running well over our acceptable time limits, causing timeouts for users on the dashboard.

*Task:* I was asked to bring query times under our SLA threshold without changing the underlying data model, since other teams depended on it.

*Action:* I ran EXPLAIN ANALYZE on the slowest queries and found most were doing full-table scans because the WHERE clauses filtered on columns not covered by any index. I added a composite index on (advertiser_id, event_date) and rewrote the queries that were fetching all columns to only retrieve what was actually needed. I also created a materialized view for the most common aggregate query, refreshed on a nightly schedule.

*Result:* Average query times dropped well below our SLA threshold. Dashboard timeout complaints from customers fell to near zero the following week.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you had to convince your team to change a technical direction.

*Situation:* My team was planning to use a relational database to store raw event logs that we expected to grow to very large volumes over the coming year.

*Task:* I believed this would create performance and cost problems quickly, and I wanted the team to consider a columnar store instead.

*Action:* I did not just argue against the plan in a meeting. I built a small proof-of-concept using a columnar store on a sample of our real data and ran benchmark queries comparing the two approaches. I shared the results in a written document with clear trade-offs, including the downsides: a columnar store had a steeper operational learning curve and required schema planning upfront.

*Result:* The team agreed to pilot the columnar approach for the new event log table. It handled the data volume well and query performance was significantly better. The team adopted it for two more tables over the following quarter.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For system design questions, clarify scope before drawing anything: what scale, what latency SLA, and what consistency guarantees does the system need? Then sketch the high-level components, and go deep on the parts the interviewer cares about. At Singular, design questions almost always involve large event volumes, so bring up partitioning, idempotency, and monitoring early.

For behavioral questions, use STAR: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep the Situation and Task brief (2-3 sentences each). Spend most of your time on Action, because that is where interviewers assess your thinking. Quantify the Result if you can, but hedge if you do not recall exact figures: 'query times dropped significantly' lands better than a number you are guessing at.

For coding questions, talk while you code. Singular engineers report that interviewers want to see your thought process, not just your final solution. Walk through edge cases before writing a single line. If you get stuck, say what you are trying to do next rather than going silent.

For debugging and incident questions, follow this structure: how you detected the problem, how you narrowed it down, what you changed, and what you did to prevent a repeat. This shows you think in systems, not just in code.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates report from Singular interviews, interviewers look for a few specific things:

Comfort with large-scale data. Singular's product processes attribution events at significant scale. If you can speak naturally about partitioning, deduplication, and exactly-once semantics, you will stand out. You do not need experience at hyper-scale, but you should be able to reason about what breaks as data volume grows.

Ownership mindset. Singular is a mid-size company where engineers typically own features end to end: design, build, monitor, and fix in production. Interview answers that stop at 'I wrote the code and handed it off' do not land as well as answers where you tracked the outcome and made it better.

Clear communication. Because Singular's teams work across time zones, interviewers pay attention to engineers who write and speak clearly. In your system design round, explain your reasoning as you go rather than drawing a diagram in silence and explaining at the end.

Pragmatism over perfection. Candidates report that interviewers push back with constraints mid-question (for example: 'now what if your budget for this is half?'). The right response is to trade off thoughtfully, not to insist on the ideal solution.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Technical foundations

Review data structures and algorithms with a focus on problems involving large data sets: sorting, hashing, sliding windows, and graph traversal. Singular's coding rounds are reported to be mid-to-hard in difficulty. Practice on a platform like LeetCode, aiming for consistency rather than memorizing solutions.

Week 2: System design and domain knowledge

Study event-driven architecture, message queues (especially Kafka), and data pipeline patterns like fan-out, idempotent consumers, and dead-letter queues. Read about how mobile attribution works at a high level: last-click attribution, view-through attribution, and the role of SKAdNetwork on iOS. You do not need to be a domain expert, but showing you understand Singular's business will set you apart.

Week 3: Behavioral prep and mock interviews

Write out 6-8 STAR stories from your own experience. Cover: a technical challenge you solved, a production incident you handled, a disagreement you navigated, and a time you improved a process. Do at least two mock interviews with a friend or an online practice partner to get comfortable talking while solving problems.

Before the interview

Check Singular's engineering blog and recent product announcements for any public detail about their technical stack. Prepare 2-3 questions for the interviewer about the team's current challenges or how they handle reliability at scale. 07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping into code without clarifying scope. In system design rounds especially, candidates lose points by drawing architecture diagrams before asking about scale, latency requirements, or budget. Take 2-3 minutes to ask clarifying questions first.

Generic behavioral answers. Saying 'I am a team player who loves learning' without a concrete story does not move the needle. Every behavioral answer needs a specific situation from your real work.

Ignoring the operational side. Many candidates design a system and then stop. Interviewers at data-heavy companies like Singular want to know: how do you monitor it, how do you know when it is broken, and how do you recover from failure? Always add observability to your design.

Over-engineering solutions. Proposing a complex multi-service architecture for a problem that a well-indexed database could solve signals that you reach for complexity before simplicity. Show that you know when simple is the right answer.

Not asking questions at the end. Candidates who ask no questions come across as uninterested. Prepare 2-3 genuine questions about the team's work, their biggest technical challenges, or how they handle on-call rotations.

Freezing on unfamiliar territory. If an interviewer asks about a tool you have not used, say so directly, then explain how you would approach learning it or reason about it from first principles. Pretending familiarity and then being exposed is far worse than honest admission.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many interview rounds does Singular typically have for Software Engineers?

Candidates report that Singular's process typically runs 3-4 rounds: an HR or recruiter screen, one or two technical rounds covering coding and system design, and a final conversation with the hiring manager or a team member. The exact structure can vary by team and role level. The full process typically takes 2-4 weeks from first contact to offer.

What programming languages does Singular use, and should I prepare in a specific one?

Singular's backend is reported to use Python and Go for data pipelines and services. For coding rounds, most interviewers will let you use the language you are most comfortable with. That said, Python fluency aligns well with the data-heavy nature of their work. Always check the specific job description for any language requirements before your interview.

Is system design a major part of the Singular Software Engineer interview?

Yes, candidates consistently report a dedicated system design round for mid-level and senior roles. Questions tend to focus on event processing, data pipelines, and API design, which directly reflect what Singular's engineering team builds. For entry-level roles, the system design component is lighter, but you should still be ready to explain basic concepts like caching, load balancing, and database indexing.

What salary can I expect as a Software Engineer at Singular in India?

Based on publicly reported and industry survey data, Software Engineer compensation at Singular in India broadly follows market bands: 6-12 LPA for entry-level (0-2 years), 15-25 LPA for mid-level (3-5 years), 28-45 LPA for senior (6-9 years), and 40-65+ LPA for lead or staff roles. Actual offers depend on your experience, the specific team, and how you negotiate. Cross-check with Glassdoor and levels.fyi for the most current figures.

Does Singular hire remotely, or do I need to be in a specific city?

Singular's India engineering hiring is concentrated in Bangalore, where the majority of their team is based. Some roles may allow hybrid arrangements, but this varies by team and is worth confirming early in the HR screen. How do I stand out as a candidate for a data-heavy role at Singular?

Candidates who do well at Singular typically show hands-on experience with large-scale data processing, whether through Kafka, Spark, Flink, or well-optimized SQL on large tables. Demonstrating even a high-level understanding of mobile attribution signals genuine interest in Singular's product. In behavioral rounds, focus on stories that show end-to-end ownership: you built something, it broke in production, you fixed it, and you made it more resilient.
