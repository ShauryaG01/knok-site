02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up frequently in Allianz frontend interviews, based on candidate reports and the company's focus on enterprise-grade financial software.

  1. Walk me through how you would structure a large React application for a financial services portal.
  2. How do you handle state management in complex multi-step forms, like a policy purchase flow?
  3. Explain your approach to making a web application accessible. Which WCAG standards are you familiar with?
  4. How do you optimize a React app that is loading slowly for users on slower mobile connections?
  5. Describe a time you caught a security vulnerability in a frontend codebase. What was it and how did you fix it?
  6. How do you manage shared component libraries between different product teams?
  7. Walk us through your testing strategy: what do you unit test, what do you integration test, and what do you leave to end-to-end tests?
  8. How do you handle breaking API changes from the backend without disrupting the user experience?
  9. Describe your experience with TypeScript. How has it helped or hindered you on a large codebase?
  10. How do you approach cross-browser and cross-device compatibility for products that need to support older users?
  11. Tell me about a time you had to push back on a design requirement because it was technically risky or not feasible.
  12. What does good frontend code review look like to you?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use these as templates and replace the specifics with your own projects.

Q: How do you handle state management in complex multi-step forms?

*Situation:* At my previous company, we built an insurance quote-and-buy flow with seven steps, including dynamic field logic and mid-flow validation.

*Task:* I was responsible for the entire frontend form architecture, and we needed it to be maintainable, testable, and able to resume from any step.

*Action:* I chose React Hook Form for field-level validation because it avoids unnecessary re-renders. For cross-step state, I used Zustand with a dedicated 'formSlice' so any component could read or update values without prop drilling. I also wrote unit tests for the validation logic in isolation.

*Result:* The form had zero reported data-loss issues after launch, and two other teams adopted the same pattern for their own flows.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you caught a security vulnerability in the frontend.

*Situation:* During a code review at my previous job, I noticed a component was rendering HTML from an API response using 'dangerouslySetInnerHTML' without any sanitisation.

*Task:* The feature was going to production in two days, and I needed to convince the team to delay the release.

*Action:* I wrote a quick proof-of-concept showing how a stored XSS payload in the API response could execute arbitrary script. I shared it with the team lead and suggested replacing 'dangerouslySetInnerHTML' with a sanitised renderer using DOMPurify.

*Result:* The team agreed, we shipped with the fix two days later, and the issue was added to our security checklist for all future code reviews.

---

Q: Walk me through how you optimised a slow-loading React app.

*Situation:* Our main dashboard was taking over six seconds to become interactive on a mid-range Android device on a 4G connection.

*Task:* I led a performance sprint to bring the time-to-interactive below three seconds.

*Action:* I used Chrome DevTools and Lighthouse to identify the bottlenecks. The two biggest issues were a massive initial bundle (all chart libraries loaded upfront) and unoptimised images. I introduced React.lazy and dynamic imports to code-split by route, converted images to WebP, and added lazy loading for below-the-fold content.

*Result:* Time-to-interactive dropped to under 2.5 seconds, and user session length increased noticeably according to our analytics.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For technical architecture questions: Start with the 'why' before the 'how'. Describe the problem you were solving, then explain your technology choices. Allianz interviewers typically want to see that you can reason about trade-offs, not just name tools.

For behavioral questions: Use the STAR format. Keep the Situation and Task brief (two to three sentences each). Spend most of your time on Action and Result. Be specific about what *you* did versus what the team did.

For accessibility and security questions: These carry extra weight in financial services interviews. Lead with standards you know (WCAG 2.1, OWASP Top 10 for frontend), then give a concrete example from your own work. Admitting you are still learning a specific area is fine, but show that you have a plan to fill the gap.

For 'tell me about a mistake' questions: Pick a real mistake, own it clearly, explain what you learned, and describe what you now do differently. Candidates who try to reframe mistakes as non-mistakes tend to score lower on self-awareness.

For system design questions at Lead/Staff level: Think out loud. Structure your answer around user needs, scalability, team boundaries (for example, micro-frontends), and how you would validate the design with stakeholders before building. For a sample prompt like 'design a claims tracker dashboard', start simple and layer in complexity only when the problem demands it.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on candidate reports and the nature of Allianz's products, frontend engineers here are expected to bring more than UI skills.

Code quality and maintainability matter a lot. Allianz codebases are large, long-lived, and touched by many teams. Reviewers want to see that you write code others can read, extend, and test without guessing at your intent.

Accessibility awareness comes up more here than at many product startups. Financial portals often serve older users and users with disabilities. Knowing WCAG 2.1 AA compliance basics and being able to audit a component with screen-reader tools is a genuine differentiator.

Security mindset is valued. Frontend engineers at a financial services company are expected to know about XSS, CSRF, and safe handling of sensitive data like policy numbers and payment details.

Communication and collaboration. Allianz has large, distributed teams. Interviewers typically probe for how you handle disagreements, how you document decisions, and how you work across design, backend, and product.

Ownership. Candidates who describe end-to-end ownership (from requirements through deployment and monitoring) stand out. Framing your work as 'I just built what the ticket said' is a red flag.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Technical foundations

Revise React internals (reconciliation, hooks lifecycle, rendering behaviour). Practice one medium-to-hard JavaScript problem daily. Set up a small project using TypeScript strictly and write tests for every component.

Week 2: Domain-specific topics

Read the WCAG 2.1 quick reference and audit a page you have built using axe or Lighthouse. Review OWASP's frontend security cheat sheet. Practice explaining state management choices out loud, as if walking a colleague through your decision.

Week 3: Behavioral and system design

Write out five STAR stories covering: a performance win, a security fix, a disagreement with a stakeholder, a time you improved a process, and a failure you learned from. Practice one frontend system design problem (for example, 'design a claims tracker dashboard') from scratch with no notes.

Before each round: Research what Allianz's public-facing products look like. Open their portals, inspect the DOM, note accessibility gaps or strong patterns. This gives you concrete reference points during the interview.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Naming libraries without explaining trade-offs. Saying 'I use Redux' is not enough. Interviewers at Allianz want to know why Redux over Zustand over Context, for this problem, at this scale.

Ignoring accessibility. Many candidates skip this entirely. For a financial services company, that is a visible gap. Even basic knowledge (semantic HTML, ARIA roles, keyboard navigation) sets you apart.

Vague STAR answers. 'We improved performance' is not a result. 'Load time dropped from six seconds to 2.4 seconds, measured with Lighthouse' is a result. Be specific, even if the numbers are approximate from memory.

Not asking questions at the end. Allianz interviewers typically expect you to have questions. Asking nothing signals low interest. Ask about team structure, deployment cadence, or how frontend decisions get made.

Over-engineering your system design answer. Jumping straight to micro-frontends and a distributed CDN for a simple dashboard shows poor judgment. Start simple, explain when you would add complexity, and only add it if the problem demands it.

Forgetting to mention testing. Any feature you describe should include how you tested it. If you cannot describe the test strategy for something you built, it raises doubts about your engineering rigour.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Allianz frontend interview typically have?

Candidates typically report three to four rounds. These usually include an initial screening call, one or two technical rounds covering coding and frontend concepts, and a final round focused on behavioral questions and team fit. Round structures can vary by team, so it is worth confirming with your recruiter after the screening call.

Does Allianz ask DSA (data structures and algorithms) questions for frontend roles?

Candidates report that Allianz's frontend interviews lean more toward practical JavaScript and React problems than pure DSA. That said, you may encounter problems involving arrays, objects, and async patterns. Brushing up on common JavaScript patterns is safer than skipping algorithms entirely, but heavy competitive programming practice is typically not the focus.

What tech stack does Allianz use for frontend development?

Based on publicly available job postings, Allianz commonly lists React, TypeScript, and modern build tools like Webpack or Vite. Some teams also use Angular for legacy products. It is worth checking the specific job description you applied to, as the stack can vary across business units and geographies.

Is domain knowledge about insurance required for the interview?

You do not need to be an insurance expert, but showing curiosity about the domain helps. Knowing the difference between a premium, a claim, and a policy, and being able to talk about how that affects UI decisions (like multi-step forms or document uploads), signals that you think about the user context and not just the code.

What salary can I expect as a mid-level frontend engineer at Allianz?

Based on knok data for mid-level frontend roles (3-5 years experience) across India, the typical market range is 12-22 LPA. Allianz-specific compensation data is not available in our dataset, so treat this as a market reference point. For company-specific figures, Glassdoor and levels.fyi have community-reported numbers that may be more precise.

How long does the Allianz hiring process take from application to offer?

Candidates report the process typically takes three to six weeks from first contact to offer, though this varies depending on the team's urgency and notice periods. If you have a competing offer or a deadline, candidates report that sharing this with the recruiter can sometimes speed up scheduling and decision timelines.
