01 Overview

Overview

Skeps is a US-based fintech company that builds embedded lending infrastructure, helping merchants offer point-of-sale financing and buy-now-pay-later options at checkout. Their platform connects merchants, lenders, and borrowers through APIs, which means engineering teams work on systems where reliability, data accuracy, and regulatory compliance are not optional constraints but core product requirements.

As of mid-2026, Skeps has 11 open roles on knok jobradar, reflecting active hiring across their engineering organisation. The Engineering Manager role sits at the intersection of technical leadership and fintech product delivery. You will typically own one or more squads, partner closely with product managers and compliance teams, and be accountable for delivery across lending workflows such as loan origination, underwriting integrations, and merchant onboarding.

Candidates who have been through the Skeps interview process report a structured but conversational approach. Interviewers tend to dig into real situations from your past rather than asking purely hypothetical questions. Coming prepared with specific stories about incidents, cross-team conflicts, and technical trade-offs gives you a clear advantage over candidates with polished but vague answers.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

The following questions are ones candidates report most frequently from Skeps Engineering Manager interviews:

  1. How do you balance feature delivery speed with the compliance and regulatory constraints that come with lending products?
  2. Skeps works with merchant partners and lending institutions at the same time. How have you managed engineering priorities when both sides have urgent requests?
  3. Tell us about a production incident that affected a financial transaction flow. What did you do, and what changed afterward?
  4. How do you approach hiring engineers in competitive markets like Bangalore or Hyderabad?
  5. How do you ensure system reliability and uptime for real-time loan processing infrastructure?
  6. How do you handle technical debt in a fast-growing fintech environment without letting it become a delivery or reliability risk?
  7. Describe a time you disagreed with a product or business decision that had technical implications. How did you handle it?
  8. How do you measure engineering team health and performance without relying purely on sprint velocity?
  9. Skeps integrates with multiple lenders and merchant partners. How have you led teams that own complex third-party integrations?
  10. How do you build a culture of code quality and security when your team is under constant delivery pressure?
  11. Tell us about a time you had to restructure your team or re-skill engineers to meet a new technical requirement.
  12. How would you handle a sustained underperformance situation with a senior engineer on your team?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell us about a production incident that affected a financial transaction flow.

*Situation:* At a previous role, our payment processing service failed during a high-traffic period, causing loan disbursement delays for a batch of approved applications.

*Task:* I was the on-call engineering manager and needed to coordinate the response across engineering, product, and our partner operations team simultaneously.

*Action:* I opened a bridge call immediately, assigned one engineer to root cause investigation and a second to prepare a rollback path. I sent structured updates to stakeholders at regular intervals so no one was left guessing about status. We isolated the failing downstream integration, validated a fix in a staging environment, and pushed to production.

*Result:* Service was restored within the same business hour. We ran a blameless postmortem the following day, identified the missing circuit breaker, and added it in the next sprint. That class of failure did not recur.

---

Q: How have you handled technical debt while keeping up with product commitments?

*Situation:* My team was building new merchant onboarding features on top of an integration layer that had grown fragile from rapid early-stage development.

*Task:* I needed to protect delivery commitments while preventing the debt from triggering incidents that would cost us more time than the refactoring itself.

*Action:* I introduced a standing allocation of sprint capacity for debt reduction, tracked it on the same board as product work, and made the risk visible to product and engineering leadership in weekly updates. I reframed the work as risk reduction rather than an engineering-only concern. Calling it 'stability investment' rather than 'cleanup' helped product leadership engage with it constructively.

*Result:* Over several quarters, incident frequency in that integration layer dropped noticeably. Product leadership became more willing to allocate capacity for it once they saw the direct connection to partner reliability.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you disagreed with a product decision that had technical or compliance implications.

*Situation:* Product wanted to launch a new credit-limit feature on a compressed timeline. I believed the plan left a gap in our data validation logic that created a real risk of incorrect loan calculations.

*Task:* I needed to push back constructively without damaging the relationship with product or killing the momentum of an important launch.

*Action:* I wrote a short risk brief covering the specific failure modes, proposed a phased rollout with tighter validation guardrails in the first phase, and requested a joint review with product, engineering, and our compliance contact. I framed the entire conversation around shared goals rather than technical preferences.

*Result:* We agreed on the phased approach. During the first-phase rollout, the stricter validation caught an edge case in how our rounding logic handled certain loan amounts. The product team later acknowledged that the phased approach prevented a visible calculation error for a subset of customers.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For technical leadership questions, cover the problem context, your reasoning process, how you involved the team, and the concrete outcome. Skeps interviewers are typically looking for specifics, so vague or high-level answers tend not to score well.

For people management questions, be clear about the behaviour you observed, how you approached the conversation with the individual, and what changed as a result. Avoid summaries like 'I gave feedback and things improved.' Interviewers want the specific steps you took.

For cross-functional questions, emphasise how you built alignment rather than how you drove your own team's agenda. Skeps operates across merchant, lender, and technology boundaries, so they value EMs who know how to bring different stakeholders to a shared position.

For incident and reliability questions, walk through your actual response process. Cover who you involved, how you communicated status, how you diagnosed the issue, and what systemic changes you made afterward. Fintech interviewers pay close attention to how you handle the communication side of incidents, not just the technical fix.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Skeps interviewers typically look for EMs who understand that fintech is not just software with a payments label on it. They want to see that you treat compliance, data integrity, and reliability as first-class engineering concerns rather than things the legal team worries about.

Domain fluency matters even if you are not a fintech specialist. Candidates who can speak to concepts like loan origination flows, underwriting API integrations, or merchant onboarding pipelines tend to get further in the process. You do not need deep expertise, but showing you have done basic research signals genuine interest in the role.

People clarity is valued over people warmth. Interviewers want to see that you can have hard conversations, set clear expectations, and follow through. Being supportive is a plus, but conflict-avoiding or vague people answers tend to score lower on the panel.

Ownership over escalation is a consistent pattern they look for. Candidates who demonstrate that they take direct responsibility during incidents and cross-team disagreements rather than defaulting to escalation tend to stand out in the later rounds.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1. Read about embedded lending, BNPL, and point-of-sale financing so you can connect your experience to Skeps' product domain. You do not need deep fintech credentials, just enough vocabulary to speak the language during interviews.

Step 2. Review Skeps' public presence: their website, LinkedIn posts from their engineering team, and any available engineering content. Understanding their current priorities helps you tailor your stories and ask sharper questions.

Step 3. Prepare five to six concrete stories from your experience covering production incidents, technical disagreements, hiring decisions, team restructuring, and cross-functional challenges. Practise each story out loud using the STAR structure before your first round.

Step 4. Prepare specific questions to ask your interviewers about team structure, current engineering challenges, how success is defined for the EM role in the first few months, and how engineering interacts with compliance at Skeps.

Step 5. Confirm the interview format with your recruiter in advance. Candidates report that the process can vary, so knowing the focus of each stage helps you bring the right stories to the right conversation.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Giving generic EM answers without connecting them to fintech or financial product context is the most common mistake. Skeps is not looking for a general-purpose people manager. They want someone who understands the stakes of building financial infrastructure.

Overstating technical depth after years of management is a trap. If you have been hands-off the code for several years, say so clearly and focus on your ability to evaluate technical decisions, not write them.

Being vague about failures. Skeps, like most fintech companies, values learning from incidents. If you cannot point to a real failure you owned and what changed as a result, you will appear either very lucky or evasive about past problems.

Not asking questions. Candidates who go through the full process without asking substantive questions about the product roadmap, current engineering challenges, or compliance constraints signal low curiosity about the business. Prepare at least three thoughtful questions for each stage.

Focusing only on the technical side. The EM role at Skeps is deeply cross-functional. Candidates who talk only about code quality and architecture without demonstrating strong communication and stakeholder management tend to score lower on the people dimensions.
