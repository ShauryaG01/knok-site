01 Hiring Overview

Hiring Overview

03 Interview Process

Interview Process

The hiring process at Urban Harvest typically runs across three to four stages, though the exact flow varies by role. Here is what candidates generally experience.

Application screening. Your resume and application are reviewed by HR or a recruiter. A clear, tailored resume that matches the job description improves your odds of making it to the next round. For some roles, a short online assessment may follow.

Initial HR call. If shortlisted, you will get a call from HR or a talent partner. This covers your background, notice period, and salary expectations. It is also your first chance to show genuine interest in what Urban Harvest is building. Keep the conversation concise and enthusiastic.

Functional or technical interview. This is the core assessment for your specific role. For operations or supply chain roles, expect scenario-based questions around logistics, coordination, or process improvement. For sales roles, expect questions about your pipeline approach and how you handle customer relationships. For tech roles, coding challenges or system-design discussions are common, and a take-home assignment is possible.

Culture and leadership round. A senior team member or someone from the founding team often conducts this final conversation. They want to understand how you think, how you handle ambiguity, and whether you genuinely align with the company's mission. Candidates who ask thoughtful questions and show real curiosity tend to leave a strong impression.

Offer and reference check. Candidates who clear the final round receive a written offer. Reference checks, if required, typically run in parallel. Salary negotiation is possible at this stage, so come prepared with a clear expectation.

04 What They Look For

What They Look For

Urban Harvest looks for people who combine functional competence with a genuine sense of purpose. Here is what tends to matter across roles.

Mission alignment. Sustainable food, urban farming, and reducing waste in the food supply chain are not just marketing for this company. Hiring managers notice whether a candidate has actually thought about why this problem matters. You do not need to be an agriculture expert, but showing that you have done some reading goes a long way.

Ownership mindset. At a company of this size, roles are broad and results are visible. They want people who take initiative, flag problems early, and follow through without needing constant supervision.

Cross-functional communication. Urban Harvest's teams work closely across departments. Whether you are in operations or technology, the ability to communicate clearly with people outside your own domain is consistently valued.

Comfort with ambiguity. Processes are still being built and refined. Candidates who are comfortable figuring things out as they go, rather than waiting for a perfect playbook, tend to thrive here.

Relevant functional skills. Each role also has its own technical bar. Supply chain roles require hands-on logistics experience. Tech roles require demonstrable coding or product skills. Sales roles require a track record of relationship-building or closing deals.

05 How To Prepare

How To Prepare

Preparing well for Urban Harvest interviews comes down to three things: knowing the company, knowing your own work, and being ready to show your thinking.

Learn what they actually do. Go beyond the homepage. Read about their products, their distribution model, and how they source produce. Look for recent news or updates about the company. If you can reference something specific in the interview, it signals that your interest is real and not part of a generic job search.

Tailor your resume to the role. Generic resumes get filtered quickly. Match your skills and achievements to the specific job description. Use concrete, result-oriented language. Instead of 'managed operations,' describe what you managed and what the outcome was.

Prepare for mission-alignment questions. Expect something like 'Why Urban Harvest?' or 'What excites you about sustainable food?' Have a genuine, thought-through answer ready. Vague enthusiasm is easy to detect and easy to forget. Specific reasoning is memorable.

Practice your functional stories. Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) to prepare a handful of examples from your past work. Pick stories that show initiative, problem-solving, and measurable impact. Be ready to adapt these to whatever scenario the interviewer raises.

Prepare questions to ask. Good questions show you have thought seriously about the role. Ask about how success is measured in the first few months, how the team works day to day, or what challenges the company is actively working through.

For the final culture round, be direct and genuine. Rehearsed, overly polished answers tend to fall flat. They want to know how you actually think.

06 Culture And Values

Culture And Values

Urban Harvest's culture has the feel of a company that is still shaping its identity while staying anchored to a clear mission.

Purpose drives the work. The company sits at the intersection of food, environment, and urban living. That sense of purpose filters into day-to-day conversations. People here tend to care about what they are building, not just the title or the compensation.

Collaborative and relatively flat. Teams are small, which means collaboration is not optional. Hierarchy exists, but direct communication across levels is more common than formal approval chains. You are expected to engage with people outside your immediate team.

Learning while building. Because the company is growing and evolving, a lot of learning happens on the job. This is a genuine advantage if you enjoy figuring things out and shaping how work gets done. It can feel uncertain if you prefer highly structured environments with clear processes already in place.

Visible impact. In a lean team, what you contribute is seen. There is less room to coast, but there is also more opportunity to build a strong track record quickly.

For candidates coming from large corporations, the shift in pace and structure can be refreshing or disorienting depending on your working style. If you want your work to have direct, visible impact and enjoy building things from the ground up, this environment will likely suit you well.
