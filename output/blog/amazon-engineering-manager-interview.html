01 Overview

Overview

Amazon is one of the most active hirers for Engineering Manager roles in India right now. The knok jobradar tracked 64 open EM positions at Amazon as of July 2026, against a total of 975 active EM roles across the country. Bangalore leads with 182 openings, followed by Delhi (53), Pune (20), Chennai (20), Hyderabad (16), and Mumbai (12).

The Amazon EM interview is built around the company's 16 Leadership Principles. Every question, whether behavioural or technical, connects to one or more of these principles. Candidates typically go through a recruiter screening, a hiring manager call, and a 'loop' of several panel interviews. One interview in the loop is typically conducted by a 'bar raiser', a senior Amazonian from outside the hiring team whose assessment carries extra weight. Candidates report that the process usually concludes with a written debrief and a decision within a couple of weeks after the loop.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Amazon EM interviews are almost entirely behavioural, anchored to Leadership Principles. System design comes up for technical EMs, but stories about how you led, decided, and delivered carry the most weight. Here are the questions candidates report most frequently:

  1. Tell me about a time you had to make a critical technical decision with incomplete information.
  2. Describe a situation where you disagreed with a senior leader and how you handled it. What was the outcome?
  3. How have you raised the hiring bar? Give a concrete example of a candidate you rejected or championed and why.
  4. Tell me about a time you managed a consistently underperforming engineer. What did you do, and what happened?
  5. Describe a project where you had to deliver results under tight deadlines and limited resources.
  6. How do you prioritise when multiple high-priority projects compete for the same team bandwidth?
  7. Tell me about a time you had to earn the trust of a sceptical team or stakeholder from scratch.
  8. How have you scaled an engineering team? What did you learn from the experience?
  9. Give an example of when you dove deep into a technical problem rather than delegating it entirely.
  10. Describe a time your team was consistently missing delivery targets. What did you do differently?
  11. Tell me about a time you had to think big and set a direction that others were not initially aligned to.
  12. How do you keep engineers motivated and engaged during a long, ambiguous, or poorly scoped project?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format for every behavioural answer. Lead with your personal action, not the team's. Here are three examples built around common Amazon EM questions.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you managed an underperforming engineer.

*Situation:* A senior engineer on my team had been missing sprint commitments for three consecutive quarters. Code quality was slipping and two other team members had silently started covering for him.

*Task:* My responsibility was to address the performance gap without losing the institutional knowledge this engineer held, and to protect the broader team's morale.

*Action:* I scheduled a private one-on-one and discovered he was struggling with an unclear role scope after an org restructure. I worked with him to define a focused set of deliverables for the next quarter, set fortnightly check-ins, and connected him with a senior engineer mentor. I also acknowledged the load-balancing problem openly with the broader team without naming him.

*Result:* Within two quarters, his on-time delivery improved noticeably and team morale scores rose. Candidates report that Amazon interviewers push hard with follow-ups like 'what exactly did you do,' so prepare specifics for every step of your story.

---

Q: Describe a time you disagreed with a senior leader.

*Situation:* My director wanted to cut a performance monitoring feature from a product launch to hit a deadline.

*Task:* I believed removing it would create serious on-call risk post-launch, but I needed to make the case clearly and respectfully without simply asking to delay the release.

*Action:* I prepared a short written document showing the on-call incident history from a past launch where we had shipped without adequate alerting. I presented two options: delay by one sprint to include basic monitoring, or scope down to a lightweight version that could still ship on time. I requested a formal decision with the product team in the room.

*Result:* The director chose the scoped-down version. Post-launch, we caught two critical issues through the lightweight alerting before they escalated. The director later cited this as a textbook example of 'disagree and commit' done right.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you had to deliver under pressure.

*Situation:* A key enterprise client threatened to churn if we did not ship a specific integration feature within six weeks. Our team had no room on the current roadmap.

*Task:* I had to assess feasibility honestly, build a plan the team could actually execute, and manage the client relationship throughout.

*Action:* I ran a short technical spike with two engineers to validate the effort. I negotiated with a neighbouring team to temporarily lend us a backend engineer. I removed two lower-priority items from the current sprint and communicated the trade-offs clearly to stakeholders. I set up a weekly status call with the client so they felt informed throughout.

*Result:* We shipped the integration in five weeks. The client renewed their contract. The team appreciated that I had shielded them from scope creep on other workstreams while we focused on the priority.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Lead with the Leadership Principle, not the story. Amazon interviewers explicitly map your answers to LPs during their debrief. Make the connection obvious in your answer, even if you do not name the LP directly. A story about a hiring decision should clearly reflect 'Hire and Develop the Best.'

Use the STAR structure every time. Situation (set the scene briefly), Task (your specific responsibility), Action (what you personally did, not what the team did), Result (measurable outcome wherever possible). Amazon interviewers will probe if your Action sounds collective. Use 'I' deliberately and specifically throughout.

Build a story bank before you interview. Map a solid set of real situations from your career to the 16 LPs. Some stories can serve double duty across multiple principles. The LPs that come up most often in EM loops are: 'Ownership,' 'Hire and Develop the Best,' 'Dive Deep,' 'Have Backbone, Disagree and Commit,' and 'Deliver Results.'

State impact concretely. If you improved deployment frequency, say by how much. If your team grew, say from what size to what. Amazon is a data-driven culture and interviewers notice vague results. If you cannot share an exact figure for confidentiality reasons, say so clearly and describe the business impact qualitatively instead.

For system design discussions (which typically come up for technical EMs): show that you can engage at an architecture level, ask the right clarifying questions, and make pragmatic trade-offs. You do not need to write code, but you should be able to discuss scalability, failure modes, and design decisions with confidence.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Amazon EM interviewers are not looking for general management competence alone. They are screening for a specific operating style that fits the company's culture.

Ownership without waiting for permission. They want to see that you treat your team's problems as your own and act on them directly. Stories where you waited for a VP to step in and fix something will not land well.

A track record of raising the talent bar. 'Hire and Develop the Best' is probed heavily at the EM level. Interviewers want examples of you turning down good candidates in favour of great ones, and actively coaching engineers to grow beyond their current level.

Data-driven decision-making. Decisions made purely on instinct raise flags. Bring metrics into your stories, even approximate ones framed with context ('our error rate was consistently above our SLA threshold').

Comfort with Amazon's written culture. Amazon's internal process runs on written documents. If you have led or contributed to formal written proposals or review documents in your current role, mention it. It signals cultural alignment.

Backbone with follow-through. 'Have Backbone, Disagree and Commit' is one of the most frequently tested LPs for EMs. Interviewers want to see that you pushed back with evidence, held your position under pressure, and then committed fully once a decision was made by the team.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Two to three weeks before the loop:
Read all 16 Amazon Leadership Principles carefully. For each one, write down one or two real situations from your career that illustrate it clearly. Prioritise stories from recent years, as they carry more weight than older examples. Identify your strongest stories and practise telling each one aloud, keeping it to a few minutes and focused.

One week before:
Do mock interviews with a peer or a coach who can play the role of an Amazon interviewer. Ask them to probe with follow-up questions like 'What exactly did you do?' and 'What would you do differently now?' If you are applying for a technical EM role, also practise system design discussions at an architecture level, not a code level.

Three to five days before:
Research the specific Amazon team you are interviewing with. What is their product? What engineering challenges do they publicly discuss? Map the key requirements from the job description to your prepared stories.

The day before:
Review your story bank. Do not memorise scripts. Know the key facts for each story and let the conversation flow naturally in your own words. Amazon loops are typically long, so plan for a full day of interviews and build in some buffer.

During the loop:
If you are stuck on a question, it is fine to pause and think before answering. Silence is better than a rambling, unfocused response. If you are unsure which LP an interviewer is probing, it is acceptable to ask them to clarify what aspect they want to explore.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Describing what 'we' did instead of what 'I' did. This is the single most common mistake from candidates. Amazon interviewers are assessing you individually. Reframe every story in the first person, especially in the Action section.

Skipping the result. A STAR story with no clear outcome feels incomplete and will often prompt the interviewer to push you for more. Even if you cannot share exact numbers, describe the business or team impact in plain, concrete terms.

Under-preparing hiring stories. Many candidates focus heavily on delivery and technical examples and neglect 'Hire and Develop the Best.' Prepare at least two strong stories specifically about hiring decisions or engineer development.

Treating the bar raiser round casually. The bar raiser interview is often the most rigorous in the loop. Do not assume it is a friendly chat. It is not, and relaxing into it is a common way to fail an otherwise strong loop.

Not researching the specific team. Generic answers about Amazon as a whole are noticeably weaker than answers that show you understand the team's product, technical challenges, and business context.

Neglecting 'Think Big' stories. Engineering Managers sometimes default to operational examples from their day-to-day. Amazon also wants to see that you can set a bold direction and bring a team along with you.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds are there in the Amazon EM interview process?

Candidates typically go through a recruiter screening, a hiring manager conversation, and a loop of several panel interviews held in one day or across two sessions. One of those interviews is typically conducted by a bar raiser. The exact number of conversations varies by team and level, so ask your recruiter upfront how many interviews to expect so you can plan your preparation accordingly.

What is a bar raiser and should I treat that round differently?

A bar raiser is a senior Amazon employee from a different team, trained to evaluate you against Amazon's overall hiring standard rather than just the immediate role's needs. Their vote in the hiring debrief carries significant weight and can block an otherwise positive loop. You will not always know in advance which interviewer is the bar raiser, so treat every conversation in the loop with equal preparation and seriousness.

Will I be tested on system design as an Engineering Manager?

It depends on the team and how technical the role is. Candidates report that most EM loops include at least one conversation covering architecture, trade-offs, or scalability at a high level. You are not typically expected to write code, but you should be able to lead a technical discussion with confidence. If the team owns complex infrastructure, prepare more thoroughly for design discussions before your loop.

What salary can I expect as an Engineering Manager at Amazon India?

The knok jobradar shows EM-level roles ranging from 35-60 LPA at Manager band, 55-90 LPA at Senior Manager, and 90-150+ LPA at Director level. Actual compensation depends on your specific band, stock grants, joining bonus, and how well you negotiate. Check Glassdoor and levels.fyi for recent figures shared by Amazon India employees to benchmark any offer you receive before responding.

How do I prepare for Leadership Principles questions if I have never worked at Amazon?

You do not need Amazon experience to answer LP questions well. The principles describe ways of working that apply in any company and at any scale. Read each of the 16 principles carefully and find real situations from your own career that match the intent of each one. The key is specificity: vague answers that could belong to anyone will not score well, so make every story concrete, personal, and anchored to a clear result.

How long does the full Amazon EM interview process take from application to offer?

Candidates report the process typically takes several weeks from initial recruiter contact to offer, though this varies significantly by team and how urgently they are hiring. The loop itself is usually scheduled within a few weeks of the hiring manager call. After the loop, candidates typically hear back within a couple of weeks. If you have not heard back within the timeline your recruiter mentioned, a polite follow-up email is completely appropriate.
