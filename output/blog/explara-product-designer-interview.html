01 Overview

Overview

Explara is a Bangalore-based event management and ticketing platform that builds tools for event organizers and attendees across India. Their product spans organizer dashboards, attendee registration flows, check-in experiences, and analytics views, making it a genuinely complex design environment with two distinct user groups pulling in different directions.

As of July 2026, Explara had 4 open Product Designer roles, sitting inside a broader market of 393 Product Designer openings tracked across India by knok jobradar. Bangalore leads demand with 62 open roles, followed by Delhi (33) and Mumbai (13).

Candidates report a process that typically covers a portfolio review, a design challenge, and one or more cross-functional discussions. The interview is design-depth heavy: expect to walk through your thinking and defend decisions, not just present finished screens.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly in Product Designer interviews at event-tech companies like Explara. Candidates report seeing variations of most of these across portfolio, challenge, and stakeholder rounds.

  1. Walk us through a product in your portfolio where you redesigned a complex multi-step workflow. What was your process from research to delivery?
  2. Explara serves two very different users: event organizers and attendees. Describe a time you balanced competing needs between two user groups in a single design decision.
  3. How would you approach improving an event registration flow to reduce drop-off before the payment step?
  4. You are designing the dashboard for a first-time event organizer. What information do you show above the fold and why?
  5. Tell us about a design system component you built or contributed to. How did you ensure other teams actually used it?
  6. Describe a time you used data or user research to challenge a stakeholder's assumption about what the product should do.
  7. How do you handle a situation where engineering says a design is not feasible two days before a launch?
  8. Explara's product touches high-stakes moments like ticketing deadlines and live event check-in. How do you design for trust and reliability under time pressure?
  9. How do you design for mobile-first experiences when a product's power users rely on desktop?
  10. Describe your process for writing a design brief or problem statement. Walk through a real example.
  11. How do you communicate design decisions to non-designers like product managers and developers?
  12. What metrics would you track to measure the success of a redesigned attendee check-in experience?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use these as a structure guide, not a script. Adapt each one to your actual experience.

---

Q: Walk us through a portfolio piece where you redesigned a complex multi-step workflow.

*Situation:* At my previous company, users were abandoning a multi-step onboarding flow at the third step out of six.

*Task:* I was asked to reduce drop-off and improve completion rates before the next product review cycle.

*Action:* I ran usability sessions, mapped the cognitive load at each step, and found that step three asked for information users did not have on hand. I restructured the flow so optional fields appeared later, added a 'save and continue' option, and introduced a progress indicator to set expectations upfront.

*Result:* Completion rates improved visibly across the following two weeks. I presented before-and-after data to stakeholders and the redesign shipped within a single sprint. The progressive disclosure pattern was later adopted in two other flows.

---

Q: Describe a time you balanced competing needs between two user groups.

*Situation:* While working on an event platform feature, organizers wanted maximum data collection at registration, but attendees found long forms a reason to abandon the flow entirely.

*Task:* Propose a design that satisfied both groups without compromising either experience or creating a separate workflow to maintain.

*Action:* I facilitated a short workshop with the product and growth teams to map must-have versus nice-to-have fields. I then designed a progressive disclosure pattern where core fields appeared upfront and optional fields could be completed post-registration via a follow-up nudge.

*Result:* Organizers got the data they needed and attendee registration time dropped. Both teams signed off without a redesign cycle, and the pattern became a template for future forms.

---

Q: Describe a time you used data to challenge a stakeholder assumption.

*Situation:* A senior stakeholder believed the biggest attendee pain point was the payment step. Session recordings and support tickets pointed elsewhere: the seat selection screen was causing most of the confusion.

*Task:* Present evidence that redirected design effort to the right area without creating conflict or making anyone defensive.

*Action:* I compiled a short deck with heatmaps, representative session recordings, and support ticket categories sorted by frequency. I framed the presentation around where users were struggling, not around who was right or wrong.

*Result:* The team agreed to prioritize the seat selection redesign. It shipped in the next cycle and support tickets for that screen dropped in the weeks that followed.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For workflow and process questions, use a Problem-Process-Outcome frame: start with what problem you were solving and for whom, walk through your specific steps (research, ideation, testing), and close with a measurable or observable outcome. Avoid narrating your portfolio without anchoring it to a real user problem.

For multi-sided product questions (such as organizers versus attendees), name the tension explicitly, show you understand both users' jobs-to-be-done, and explain the principle you used to resolve the conflict. Common principles: primary user priority, severity of friction, or business impact of the trade-off.

For 'how would you approach X' hypothetical questions, follow a Discover, Define, Design, Validate structure: say what you would learn first, how you would frame the problem, what solutions you would explore, and how you would test before shipping. Show curiosity, not just a pre-formed final answer.

For metrics questions, connect a design goal to a behavioral signal. For a check-in experience: time-to-check-in per attendee, queue abandonment, or support requests during the live event window. Avoid vanity metrics like total page views or app opens.

For conflict and constraint questions, lead with your reasoning process. Interviewers want to see that you can hold a design position while staying open to new information, not that you always got your way.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Explara interviewers, based on candidate reports, typically look for four qualities.

Comfort with complexity. Explara's product has many user types and high-stakes moments. Interviewers want evidence you have handled complex flows before and made them feel simple to the end user. If your portfolio only shows landing pages or simple forms, add a project that demonstrates systems thinking.

Cross-functional maturity. Designers at event-tech companies work closely with engineering during fast release cycles. Candidates who can show they collaborated under pressure, made trade-offs clearly, and communicated decisions without friction tend to move forward past the portfolio stage.

User empathy backed by evidence. Saying 'I am user-centric' is not enough. Interviewers respond better when you cite a specific research method you used, what you learned from it, and how it changed your design direction in a concrete way.

Ownership and initiative. Explara is a growth-stage company. Interviewers probe whether you waited to be told what to design or proactively identified problems. Examples where you spotted an issue before it became a stakeholder request stand out more than examples where you executed a handed-down brief well.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Start with the product itself. Sign up as both an organizer and an attendee on Explara's platform before your first round. Map three flows that work well and two that feel rough. Be ready to reference these specifically. Interviewers notice when a candidate has clearly used the product versus read about it.

Select the right portfolio projects. Pick two to three projects that map to event-tech challenges: forms, dashboards, registration flows, or marketplace-style products. Practise narrating each in under five minutes using the Problem-Process-Outcome frame described in the Answer Frameworks section.

Prepare for the design challenge. Candidates typically report a timed design exercise, either take-home or live. Practise thinking aloud: interviewers want to follow your reasoning, not just see a finished output. Common scenarios involve redesigning a registration flow or an organizer creation experience.

Prepare your salary anchor. Glassdoor, levels.fyi, and industry surveys are the right places to anchor your ask. The market bands in the Overview section give a starting frame. Explara's specific offers are not publicly disclosed, so go in with a range, not a single number.

Prepare questions for the interviewer. Have at least three ready. Strong ones: 'What does the design team's relationship with engineering look like day to day?' and 'What is the biggest design problem the team is focused on right now?'

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Showing only finished screens. Interviewers want to see your thinking, not just polished mockups. If your portfolio does not include sketches, research notes, or iteration history, add a narrative slide that walks through the messy middle of one project.

Generic answers about user-centric design. Phrases like 'I always put the user first' without a concrete example lose interviewers quickly. Every answer should include a real situation and a specific action you took, not a philosophy statement.

Ignoring the business context. Explara is a commercial product. Designs that ignore conversion, retention, or organizer revenue concerns can suggest you work in a bubble. Show that you understand design serves both users and the business at the same time.

Talking only about visual design. Product Designer roles at companies like Explara expect systems thinking, not just UI polish. If your answers stay at the level of colour and typography, you may not progress past the initial portfolio review.

Not preparing questions for the interviewer. Candidates who ask nothing are often read as uninterested. Thoughtful questions about team structure, design process, or current product challenges show genuine engagement with the role.

Hiding trade-off decisions. If you compromised on a design due to time or technical constraints, say so clearly and explain why it was the right call at the time. Hiding trade-offs makes interviewers distrust the full story you are telling.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Explara Product Designer interview typically have?

Candidates typically report two to four rounds: an initial screen with HR or a recruiter, a portfolio review with the design team or hiring manager, a design exercise (take-home or live), and a final round with a product or business stakeholder. The exact structure varies by seniority and team need, so confirm the format with the recruiter before each stage.

Is a take-home design challenge common at Explara?

Yes, candidates commonly report a design challenge as part of the process. It may be a take-home task with a day or two to complete, or a live exercise during the interview itself. Practise narrating your thinking aloud since interviewers often evaluate your process as much as the output. A rough sketch with clear reasoning beats a polished screen with no explanation.

What tools should I know for a Product Designer role at Explara?

Figma is the standard tool candidates report using at most Indian product companies, including event-tech firms. Familiarity with prototyping, basic design system organisation, and handoff practices (annotations and specs for developers) is expected. Knowledge of user research tools or analytics platforms is a plus but is not always required for mid-level roles.

How should I negotiate salary for a Product Designer role at Explara?

Research market ranges using Glassdoor, levels.fyi, and industry surveys before your negotiation conversation. The broad India market bands run 14-24 LPA for mid-level and 26-40 LPA for senior roles, though Explara's specific offers are not publicly disclosed. Come in with a range backed by data, be clear about your total expectations including any variable component, and avoid anchoring too low too early.

Does Explara hire freshers or only experienced designers?

Explara's open roles at a given time may span levels. The entry-level band in the Product Designer market starts at 6-12 LPA for 0-2 years of experience, suggesting growth-stage companies do hire junior designers. Always check the specific job description for minimum experience requirements, since role-level requirements vary between openings even within the same company.

How does the design team at Explara typically work with product and engineering?

Candidates and employees commonly describe growth-stage event-tech teams as cross-functional and fast-moving. Designers typically work closely with product managers during discovery and with engineers during sprint cycles. Expect involvement from problem framing through to QA rather than being handed a brief and asked only for mockups, so comfort with ambiguity and quick iteration is valued.
