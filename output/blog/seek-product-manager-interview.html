01 Overview

Overview

Seek is a leading employment marketplace operating across Australia, New Zealand, and parts of Asia. Their product teams work on connecting job seekers with employers at scale, which makes this a strong destination for PMs who care about marketplace dynamics, data quality, and meaningful social impact.

As of July 2026, knok's job radar shows 70 open Product Manager roles at Seek, with the broader Indian PM market showing 2,009 active listings. Bangalore leads with 271 openings, followed by Delhi at 177.

Candidates report that Seek's interview process typically spans multiple rounds covering behavioural questions, product thinking, and analytical discussions. Hiring managers look for people who can balance user empathy with business outcomes and who are comfortable making decisions under ambiguity. Understanding Seek's core product before your first call gives you a clear edge.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions are frequently reported by candidates who have interviewed for PM roles at Seek:

  1. Tell me about a product you built from zero to launch. What was your biggest challenge?
  2. How do you decide what to build next when you have many competing feature requests?

  4. Describe a time you used data to change a product direction.

  10. How do you balance short-term revenue goals against long-term user trust?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time you used data to change a product direction.

*Situation:* I was PM for a B2B SaaS onboarding flow. Drop-off during sign-up was cutting into activation numbers.

*Task:* My goal was to find where users were leaving and redesign the flow to improve completion rates.

*Action:* I pulled funnel data and found that most users exited at the profile-setup step, which displayed several optional fields as if they were mandatory. I ran a small round of user interviews to confirm that confusion was the main blocker, then worked with design to remove non-essential fields from the first screen and launched an A/B test.

*Result:* Activation improved meaningfully within the first sprint after launch. The simplified flow became a template for onboarding screens across the rest of the product.

---

Q: Tell me about a product bet that did not pay off. What did you learn?

*Situation:* I championed a social sharing feature that let job seekers share open roles with friends.

*Task:* My hypothesis was that peer referrals would bring in more quality applications for employers.

*Action:* We built and shipped the feature over a few sprints. I set up tracking and monitored engagement for a full quarter.

*Result:* Uptake was very low. Post-launch interviews revealed that most job seekers did not want to publicly signal they were looking for work. I shut down the feature, documented the insight, and used it to re-evaluate two other planned social features. The key lesson: validate social behaviour assumptions with qualitative research before committing engineering time.

---

Q: How do you handle a product decision that benefits one market but hurts another?

*Situation:* A new ranking algorithm I introduced improved results for one user segment but reduced relevance for another segment.

*Task:* Stakeholders were split. I needed to make a clear call: ship as is, delay, or find a middle path.

*Action:* I wrote a trade-off document comparing impact by segment, short-term vs long-term revenue implications, and alignment with company strategy. I proposed three options with a clear recommendation and presented it to leadership for sign-off.

*Result:* We shipped a modified version that limited the downside for the second segment. Both teams felt the process was fair, and the trade-off document became a reusable template for future cross-market decisions.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions. Every 'tell me about a time' question works best with Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task brief. Spend most of your time on Action, where you show how you think and decide. End with a specific, concrete Result.

CIRCLES for product design questions. When asked to improve or design a product: Clarify the goal, Identify users, Report user needs, Cut scope, List solutions, Evaluate trade-offs, Summarise. This structure stops you from jumping to solutions before you understand the problem.

North Star metric first for metrics questions. Name the single metric that best captures user value, then list supporting metrics that catch regressions elsewhere. Avoid listing many metrics without a hierarchy or a clear primary one.

Structured trade-off document for cross-market questions. Seek operates across multiple markets, so expect questions about competing priorities. A useful structure: state the options, list costs and benefits for each, align to company strategy, make a recommendation, and note what you will monitor after the decision.

Outside-in framing for strategy questions. When asked about entering a new category, start with the user problem, then size the opportunity, then assess Seek's right to win (assets, distribution, data), then outline the key risks. This shows you think before you build.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Clarity of thinking over breadth of ideas. Seek interviewers typically want to follow your reasoning step by step. A structured answer with one clear recommendation beats a list of many possibilities with no decision.

Marketplace intuition. Seek is a two-sided marketplace connecting employers and job seekers. Candidates who demonstrate they understand supply, demand, and liquidity dynamics in marketplace products tend to stand out. If your background is in SaaS or consumer apps, prepare an example of how you think about marketplace trade-offs.

Data fluency without data obsession. You should be comfortable discussing metrics, funnels, and A/B tests, but also know when qualitative signals should override quantitative ones. Saying 'the data said X but users told us Y, so we dug deeper' shows product maturity.

Ownership and accountability. Candidates report that Seek's culture values PMs who take responsibility for outcomes, including failures, rather than attributing them to other teams. Interviewers typically probe for this quality directly.

Connection to the mission. Seek's stated purpose is to help people live more fulfilling working lives. Candidates who can connect their personal motivation to that mission, without sounding rehearsed, tend to leave a stronger impression.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Product knowledge.
Use Seek as a job seeker and explore the employer side. Note what the recommendation engine surfaces, how search filters work, and where the experience feels weak. Read publicly available coverage of Seek's product direction from 2024-2026. List a few areas you would improve and why.

Week 2: Behavioural stories.
Write out several STAR stories covering: a launch you owned, a failure and what you learned, a data-driven decision, a stakeholder conflict, and a cross-functional win. Practise saying each out loud, aiming for a natural two to three minute delivery per story.

Week 3: Product and metrics practice.
Practise one product design question per day using the CIRCLES framework. For each question, define your success metric before describing the design. Practise out loud since Seek interviews typically involve talking through your thinking in real time.

Week 4: Mock interviews and final prep.
Do at least a couple of mock interviews with a peer or a PM community. Review your STAR stories once more. Prepare a few thoughtful questions to ask your interviewer about Seek's current roadmap, team structure, or biggest product challenges.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping to solutions without clarifying the problem. In product design questions, many candidates start describing features before defining the user or the goal. Seek interviewers typically expect you to ask a clarifying question or two before diving in.

Vague results in STAR answers. Saying 'the product improved' is weak. Even without exact numbers, describe direction and scale: 'activation improved meaningfully in the first month' or 'stakeholder complaints dropped to near zero after the process change'.

Ignoring the two-sided marketplace. Candidates sometimes optimise only for the job seeker side. Remember that employers are also customers. A strong answer acknowledges the impact on both sides of the marketplace.

Over-engineering the framework. Using CIRCLES or STAR is helpful, but rigidly reciting every step can make you sound robotic. Use the framework as a guide, not a script.

Not preparing questions to ask. Candidates who ask no questions, or ask very generic ones, tend to leave a weaker impression. Prepare a couple of specific questions about the team or the current roadmap.

Underselling failures. Seek's culture values learning from mistakes. Candidates who deflect failure questions or give vague answers about what went wrong miss a chance to demonstrate self-awareness and growth.

How many rounds does Seek's PM interview typically have?

Candidates report that the process typically includes a recruiter screen, one or two product thinking rounds, a behavioural interview, and sometimes a case study or take-home exercise. The exact structure varies by level and team. It is worth asking your recruiter to walk you through the format at the start of the process.

Based on knok's job radar data, PM salaries in India broadly range from 12-20 LPA at the Associate PM level, 24-40 LPA for mid-level PMs with 3-6 years of experience, 40-60 LPA for Senior PMs, and 55-90+ LPA for Group or Principal PMs. Seek-specific figures are not publicly reported at scale, so treat these as market reference points. Glassdoor and levels.fyi may have community-submitted data for Seek specifically.

Do I need marketplace or HR-tech experience to get a PM role at Seek?

Not necessarily. Candidates report that Seek values strong product thinking and user empathy over domain-specific experience. That said, showing you understand two-sided marketplace dynamics, even through self-study or adjacent work, gives you an edge. If your background is in SaaS or consumer apps, prepare an example of how you have thought about supply-demand trade-offs.

What kind of case study or take-home exercise should I expect?

Candidates report that Seek sometimes includes a product case exercise, either as a take-home or a live discussion.
