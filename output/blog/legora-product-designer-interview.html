01 Overview

Overview

Legora is an AI-powered platform built for legal professionals, helping lawyers research, draft, and review documents faster. As a Product Designer here, you sit at the intersection of complex legal workflows and interfaces that need to feel simple and trustworthy. This is not a role for generic UX work. You are translating expert-level legal thinking into tools that practitioners use under real time pressure.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up most often in Legora Product Designer interviews, based on candidate reports. They reflect the company's focus on AI-native products, expert users, and clear design rationale.

  1. Walk us through a portfolio project where you simplified a complex, information-heavy workflow. What trade-offs did you make?
  2. How do you design for expert users (such as lawyers) who know their domain deeply but may not be comfortable with new technology?
  3. Legora surfaces AI-generated legal research. How do you design AI output so that users trust it and act on it?
  4. Describe a time you worked closely with engineers during implementation. How did technical constraints shape your final design?
  5. Tell us about a design decision that turned out to be wrong. What happened next?
  6. How do you handle it when a stakeholder's opinion conflicts with what your user research is telling you?
  7. What does your zero-to-one design process look like, from the first discovery conversation to the final handoff?
  8. How would you approach designing for dense, text-heavy content like legal documents, where readability and hierarchy are critical?
  9. How do you validate designs in a specialised domain where recruiting the right users for testing can be difficult?
  10. Legora serves legal professionals across different regions and practice areas. How do you design for a distributed but highly specialised user base?
  11. How do you prioritise competing design requests when engineering bandwidth is limited?
  12. How would you design the onboarding experience for someone using a legal AI tool for the very first time?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for every behavioural question. Keep the Situation brief and spend most of your time on Action and Result.

---

Q: Walk us through a portfolio project where you simplified a complex, information-heavy workflow.

*Situation:* I was the solo designer on a B2B reporting tool used by accountants. The main dashboard displayed dozens of data fields in a single view, and users consistently described it as 'overwhelming' in feedback calls.

*Task:* Reduce cognitive load for casual users while keeping all functionality accessible to power users who relied on every field.

*Action:* I ran six user interviews to map which fields were checked daily versus occasionally. I then designed a progressive disclosure pattern: a top card showing five key metrics, with a 'Show all details' expansion below. I tested two prototypes with real users across two rounds of iteration before handoff.

*Result:* Task completion time for the most common reporting action improved noticeably in usability testing. Support tickets about 'can't find X' dropped in the sprint after launch, per our internal tracking.

---

Q: How do you design AI-generated content so that users trust and act on it?

*Situation:* At a previous company, we shipped an AI summarisation feature for long compliance documents. Early feedback showed users were ignoring the summaries because they were unsure how accurate they were.

*Task:* Design a UI that made AI output feel credible and verifiable, without adding friction to the core task.

*Action:* I added inline source citations so each summary sentence pointed back to the original paragraph. Instead of a confidence percentage (which tested poorly with legal-adjacent users), I used plain-language phrases like 'based on four matching clauses.' I validated this with five moderated user sessions before shipping.

*Result:* Adoption of the summary feature climbed in the following sprint review. Qualitative feedback shifted from 'I don't trust this' to 'this actually saves me time.'

---

Q: How do you handle it when a stakeholder's opinion conflicts with your user research?

*Situation:* A product lead insisted our new onboarding flow should open with a feature tour, citing competitor benchmarks. Our research showed new users wanted to upload a document and get a result immediately.

*Task:* Present a data-backed alternative without dismissing the stakeholder or damaging the working relationship.

*Action:* I reframed the conversation around a shared goal: reducing time-to-value. I built two lightweight prototypes and invited the stakeholder to observe a live usability session. Watching users skip the tour to reach the upload screen made the finding concrete, not just a slide on a deck.

*Result:* The stakeholder agreed to proceed with the upload-first flow. It shipped in the next sprint, and onboarding completion improved, tracked in our analytics dashboard.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions. Situation (one to two sentences of context), Task (your specific responsibility), Action (what you personally did, not what the team did), Result (a concrete outcome, even if qualitative). Keep the Situation short and spend most of your time on Action and Result.

Design critique structure. When asked to review an existing screen or flow, answer in this order: what the user is trying to do, what is working and why, what is creating friction and why, and what you would change with a clear rationale. Lead with user goals, not aesthetics.

Trade-off framing. Legora interviewers care about judgment. When explaining a design choice, name the trade-off directly: 'I chose X over Y because for this user at this moment, clarity mattered more than completeness.' This signals mature thinking rather than preference-based decisions.

Domain learning approach. For questions about designing in an unfamiliar domain, show a structured method: talk to domain experts first, observe actual workflows before sketching anything, start with low-fidelity concepts, test early with real practitioners.

Prioritisation signals. When asked how you prioritise, name the inputs you use: user impact, frequency of the task, engineering cost, and alignment with company goals. Show that you use data and conversation, not just gut feel.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Genuine curiosity about legal work. Legora is not a generic SaaS tool. Interviewers want to see real interest in how legal professionals work, not just a willingness to apply an existing UX toolkit to a new context. Candidates who ask sharp questions about legal workflows tend to stand out.

Comfort with AI-native products. Legora's core product is AI-powered. Interviewers probe how you think about designing for probabilistic, sometimes imperfect AI output. They want designers who understand trust, transparency, and how to communicate uncertainty through UI.

Rigour in process, clarity in rationale. You should be able to explain every significant decision in your portfolio. Vague answers like 'it felt cleaner' are a red flag. Specific answers grounded in user research or testing signal the depth they are looking for.

Cross-functional fluency. Product Designers at Legora work closely with engineers and product managers. Interviewers want to see that you can speak both languages: scope and velocity conversations with PMs, implementation constraints with engineers.

Low ego under feedback. Candidates report that interviewers challenge design decisions directly during portfolio reviews. Engage thoughtfully rather than defending reflexively. Show that you treat critique as information, not as a personal attack.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Know the product and the domain first. Sign up for a Legora demo or free trial if one is available. Spend time in the actual product and take notes on flows that feel smooth and flows that create friction. Research how legal research actually works: what do lawyers do repeatedly, where do they lose time, what makes document review painful. This context shapes every answer you give.

Sharpen three to four portfolio stories. For each project, write out the full STAR arc and practise saying it in under four minutes. Remove any project you cannot explain clearly to someone outside your domain. Prioritise projects that show complexity handled well, not just visual polish.

Prepare for the design challenge. Spend the first quarter of your time on discovery: who is the user, what are they trying to do, what constraints matter. Document your thinking as you go. Interviewers typically care as much about your process as the final output.

Prepare sharp questions for the panel. Ask the PM how design and product align on priorities. Ask the engineer how designs are handed off and what breaks down in practice. Ask a designer what a good week looks like in the role. Questions signal genuine interest and help you assess fit.

Day before the interview. Review Legora's recent product updates and any public design or engineering content. Refresh your memory on your portfolio. Sleep well.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Leading with aesthetics, not user goals. Many candidates open portfolio walkthroughs with 'I made it look cleaner.' Legora interviewers want to hear about user problems first. Reframe every story around what the user needed, then explain what you designed and why.

Shallow domain preparation. Saying 'I know the legal industry is complex' without any specifics signals you did not do the work. At minimum, understand what legal research involves, what contract review looks like, and why accuracy matters so much in this context.

Claiming the team's work as your own. In collaborative projects, be precise: say 'I was responsible for X' and 'the team together achieved Y.' Interviewers probe this directly, and inconsistencies undermine credibility.

Defensive responses to critique. If an interviewer pushes back on a decision during a portfolio review, do not defend it reflexively. Acknowledge the perspective, explain your original reasoning, and if their point is valid, say so. Intellectual honesty is a strong signal in design interviews.

Ignoring constraints in design challenges. Ambitious solutions that would take months to build can actually be a red flag. Show that you understand scope: note which parts are MVP and which are future iterations.

Not asking questions. A Product Designer who does not ask questions during an interview raises a flag. Asking 'what does success look like in this role after six months?' or 'how does design currently influence the product roadmap?' shows strategic thinking and genuine interest in the role.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Legora Product Designer interview typically have?

Candidates typically report three to five rounds. These usually include a recruiter or HR screen, a portfolio walkthrough with the design team, a design challenge (take-home or live), and a final cross-functional panel. Some candidates report an additional culture or values conversation. The exact structure can vary by team and seniority, so confirm the process with your recruiter early.

Is a take-home design challenge common, and how much time should I spend on it?

Candidates commonly report receiving a take-home brief as part of the process. Most briefs come with a suggested time limit, and staying within it is itself a signal about how you work under constraints. Focus on documenting your thinking clearly. A well-reasoned partial solution typically beats a polished but unexplained one.

Do I need prior legal tech experience to apply?

Not necessarily. Candidates without a legal background do receive offers, but you need to show genuine curiosity about the domain and a structured approach to learning unfamiliar industries quickly. Spend time understanding legal workflows before your interview, and be ready to explain how you would approach a new expert domain as a designer.

What salary can I expect as a Product Designer at Legora?

Based on knok jobradar data for India (July 2026), Product Designer roles broadly range from 6-12 LPA at entry level, 14-24 LPA at mid level, 26-40 LPA at senior level, and 36-55+ LPA at lead or principal level. Legora-specific compensation may differ based on scope and negotiation. Check publicly reported figures on Glassdoor for a more current benchmark on this specific company.

How important is visual design skill versus UX process skill?

Both matter, but candidates report that Legora interviewers weight process and thinking more heavily than visual polish. You should be able to produce clean, usable interfaces, but the bigger differentiator is whether you can explain why every significant design decision was made. A strong process story with solid (not stunning) visuals will typically beat beautiful work with thin rationale.
