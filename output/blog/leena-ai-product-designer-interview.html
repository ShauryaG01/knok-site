03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk me through a product you designed end-to-end.

*Situation:* My company was receiving a high volume of support tickets from employees who could not find HR policy information on their own.

*Task:* I was asked to design a self-service knowledge portal that would reduce ticket volume and improve the employee experience.

*Action:* I started with user research, interviewing several employees across departments to understand how they searched for information. I mapped their journey, identified key drop-off points, and ran a card-sorting exercise to fix the information architecture. I built low-fidelity wireframes, tested them with a small group of users, iterated based on feedback, and handed off detailed specs to engineering with a component library built in Figma.

*Result:* After launch, support tickets related to policy queries dropped noticeably, as reported by the HR team internally. The portal became a reference case study used in sales demos.

---

Q: How do you design for users who are not tech-savvy?

*Situation:* I was designing a mobile app for factory-floor workers at a logistics client. Many users had limited smartphone experience.

*Task:* My goal was to create a daily task-management flow that required zero training to pick up.

*Action:* I used progressive disclosure to show only what the user needed at each step. I replaced text-heavy instructions with icons and short video tooltips. I ran usability tests on-site with actual workers, which revealed that some could not read small text clearly. I increased font sizes and added high-contrast mode. I also simplified onboarding so it could be completed quickly without a formal training session.

*Result:* The client confirmed a meaningful reduction in training time compared to the previous tool, and first-session completion rates were strong according to internal reporting.

---

Q: Tell us about a design that did not perform as expected.

*Situation:* I redesigned a leave-request flow to make it faster, compressing a multi-step process into just two screens.

*Task:* The goal was to reduce the time employees spent submitting leave requests.

*Action:* After shipping, I noticed through session recordings that many users were abandoning mid-flow. I ran a few quick user interviews and discovered that employees actually wanted to review their leave balance and team calendar before submitting. My 'simpler' flow had removed that context. I added a contextual sidebar that showed leave balance and team availability without requiring users to navigate away.

*Result:* Abandonment dropped after the fix. The lesson I took was that speed and simplicity are not the same thing. Users need the right information at the right moment, not fewer screens.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For portfolio and process questions, use a structure candidates call 'Problem, People, Process, Proof.' Start by describing the problem and who was affected. Then explain the research or discovery you did (who you talked to, what you found). Walk through your key design decisions and why you made them. Finish with what happened after launch, even if the result is qualitative.

For design challenge questions, state your assumptions out loud before you start sketching. Interviewers at companies like Leena AI are evaluating how you think, not just what you draw. Say something like: 'I am assuming the primary user is an HR manager, not an employee, and I am designing for desktop first. Let me know if I should adjust.' This shows structured thinking and saves you from solving the wrong problem.

For 'how do you handle conflict' questions, use a three-part answer: what each stakeholder wanted, how you found the shared goal underneath the disagreement, and what you decided or proposed. Avoid framing it as 'I convinced them I was right.' Frame it as 'We aligned on the user need and worked backward from there.'

For 'measure success' questions, connect your design decisions to something observable: task completion, support ticket volume, time-on-task, or user satisfaction scores. You do not need to cite specific numbers from past jobs if they are confidential. Saying 'our usability score improved meaningfully on the next round of testing' is honest and credible.

For conversational UI questions (especially relevant at Leena AI), explain how chatbot design differs from screen design. Key points: error states matter more because there is no undo button in a conversation, tone and language are part of the design, and fallback flows (when the bot cannot answer) need as much attention as the happy path.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Systems thinking over pixel craft. Leena AI's product is used by HR teams managing hundreds or thousands of employees. Interviewers want to see that you think about design at a system level: how a change in one flow affects another, how a component scales across different user roles, and how your decisions hold up under edge cases.

Comfort with enterprise and B2B complexity. Consumer design and enterprise design have different constraints. Enterprise users often have no choice but to use the tool (it is their company-mandated software), so the bar for clarity and error recovery is high. Show that you understand this context and have worked within it before.

Research-backed decisions. Candidates who say 'I felt this was clearer' without evidence tend to get pushed back on in interview. Bring examples of user interviews, usability tests, or data that shaped your choices, even if the scale was small. The habit of validating assumptions matters more than the sample size.

Collaboration stories. Product Designers at Leena AI work closely with product managers, engineers, and customer success teams. Expect at least one question about how you work across functions. Be specific about what you did, not just that 'the team worked well together.'

A point of view on AI-assisted experiences. Since Leena AI is building AI-native HR tools, showing genuine interest in how AI changes UX (and where it creates new design problems) will stand out. This does not mean you need to be an AI expert. It means you should have a considered opinion and be able to discuss it calmly.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Study Leena AI's product. Sign up for a trial or watch product demo videos on their website. Note a few UX decisions you find interesting or would change. You will likely get a chance to discuss these in your portfolio round, and it signals genuine interest.

Step 2: Prepare portfolio stories using the Problem, People, Process, Proof structure. Choose one story that shows your research skills, one that shows your ability to work with complex flows or data, and one that shows how you handled a setback or surprise. These three cover most of what interviewers probe.

Step 3: Practise explaining your design decisions out loud. Record yourself walking through a project. Watch it back and check whether you are explaining why you made each choice, not just what you did. Most candidates describe outputs; strong candidates describe reasoning.

Step 4: Read up on conversational UI and chatbot design patterns. Search for publicly available case studies on HR chatbots or employee experience platforms. You do not need to be an expert, but you should be able to discuss the specific challenges of designing conversational flows.

Step 5: Prepare questions to ask the interviewer. Good questions for Leena AI: How does the design team give and receive feedback? How does design influence roadmap decisions? What does the handoff process with engineering look like? These show you are thinking seriously about how the team actually works.

Step 6: Do a take-home assignment dry run. Candidates report Leena AI sometimes includes a design challenge. Practise a timed exercise: pick an HR tool you use (even a simple one like a leave-request form) and redesign one flow within a set time. Focus on clearly articulating your choices, not on making it look polished.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Showing work without explaining the why. Walking through screens without explaining the decisions behind them is the most common mistake in portfolio reviews. Interviewers want to know what problem you were solving and what alternatives you considered, not just the final output.

Underselling research. Many designers do user research but describe it vaguely: 'I talked to some users.' Be specific. How many people did you talk to? What did you find? How did it change your design? Specificity signals rigour.

Ignoring edge cases in design challenges. When given a design prompt, candidates often design only the happy path. At an enterprise product company like Leena AI, interviewers notice when you forget error states, empty states, and accessibility considerations.

Claiming sole ownership of work done by a team. Interviewers often probe with 'what was your specific contribution?' Use 'I' when describing your decisions and 'we' when describing team effort. Mixing these up looks like overclaiming.

Not knowing the product. Arriving at a Leena AI interview without having explored the product or at least watched a demo signals low interest. You do not need to have used their enterprise software, but you should be able to describe what it does and why it matters.

Treating culture-fit questions as formalities. Questions like 'how do you handle feedback?' or 'describe a conflict with a colleague' are evaluated seriously. Prepare a real example for each, not a generic answer.
