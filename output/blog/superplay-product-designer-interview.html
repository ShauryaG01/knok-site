01 Overview

Overview

SuperPlay is a mobile gaming studio known for casual titles built around strong social mechanics and rewarding core loops. Product Designers there work at the intersection of game design and UX, shaping onboarding flows, in-app store screens, live-ops features, and player-retention mechanics. The team values designers who think about the player experience across many sessions, not just the first one.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through a mobile game UX project you led from discovery to launch.

*Situation:* I was the sole UX designer on a casual puzzle game that had a high drop-off in the first few levels. Players were leaving before they experienced the core loop.

*Task:* My goal was to redesign the onboarding experience so players reached the 'aha moment' faster, without overwhelming new users with too many instructions at once.

*Action:* I started with session recordings and heatmaps to see exactly where taps were going wrong. I then ran moderated playtests with a small group of users who matched the target demographic. Based on the findings, I redesigned the tutorial to be contextual, showing hints only when a player hesitated rather than front-loading all instructions upfront. I prototyped three layout variants, got sign-off from the PM, and worked with engineers through a focused sprint.

*Result:* The redesigned onboarding shipped and early-level completion improved meaningfully in the weeks after launch. The PM cited it as one of the cleaner cross-functional deliveries of that quarter. The main lesson was that players learn better by doing than by reading.

---

Q: Describe a time you used player data to redesign a feature.

*Situation:* Our live-ops team noticed that players were ignoring a daily reward feature we had launched. Engagement was much lower than expected, and the team was debating whether to remove it entirely.

*Task:* I needed to understand whether the problem was discoverability, the reward value, or the interaction design itself before recommending a fix.

*Action:* I pulled funnel data with the analytics team and found that most players never saw the entry point because it sat below the fold on the home screen. I also ran a short in-app survey to understand perceived reward value. Armed with both, I proposed a redesign: a prominent but non-intrusive notification badge and a one-tap claim flow. I tested two layout options with a small group of internal testers before full rollout.

*Result:* Daily reward claims rose significantly after the change, and the feature moved from a low-priority backlog item to one the PM included in the next investor update. The key lesson was that discoverability problems often look like engagement problems until you pull the data.

---

Q: Tell us about a design you shipped that underperformed. What did you learn?

*Situation:* I led the redesign of a game's in-app store screen, convinced that a cleaner, more minimal layout would improve purchase conversion.

*Task:* The goal was to increase conversion on the starter pack offer, which was the game's primary monetisation surface.

*Action:* I stripped out a lot of visual 'noise', reduced the number of offers shown, and created what I felt was a more elegant layout. I presented it confidently to stakeholders, and it shipped after a short A/B test window.

*Result:* Conversion on the starter pack dropped compared to the original. A post-mortem showed that the visual richness I had removed was actually signalling value to players. The 'noise' was doing work I had not respected. I learned to treat existing patterns in gaming UX with more curiosity before removing them, and to run A/B tests long enough to account for novelty effects.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Most SuperPlay interview questions fall into three types. Knowing which framework to reach for saves you from rambling.

For portfolio and case study questions, open with the player problem or business problem, not with 'I was tasked with.' Interviewers want to hear that you start from understanding, not from output. Move through your specific choices and close with the outcome.

For behavioural questions (anything starting with 'tell me about a time') use STAR: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task brief, two or three sentences each. Spend most of your time on Action (the specific choices you made and why) and close with a concrete Result plus one honest reflection.

For opinion or craft questions (such as 'what makes a great onboarding?') use a three-part structure: state your principle, give an example from your own work or a game you respect, and explain how you would apply it at SuperPlay. This shows you have conviction and can connect abstract thinking to real execution.

For cross-functional questions about working with engineers or PMs, the key signal interviewers look for is that you treat constraints as design inputs, not obstacles. Describe how you share work-in-progress early, not just polished finals.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

SuperPlay interviewers are typically looking for a few qualities beyond a polished portfolio.

Deep craft in mobile game UX. They want to see that you understand what makes casual game interactions feel satisfying: clear feedback, low friction, well-timed rewards. Generic app design experience alone is unlikely to be enough without a clear translation to game mechanics.

Comfort with data. Product Designers at SuperPlay are expected to read dashboards and partner with analysts. Candidates who can speak naturally about retention curves, funnel stages, and session-length data stand out from those who rely on instinct alone.

Collaboration instinct. SuperPlay ships fast. Interviewers look for designers who share work early, give and receive feedback generously, and can articulate trade-offs clearly to non-designers.

Player empathy backed by evidence. Saying 'I care about users' is table stakes. Showing how you ran a playtest, interpreted a session recording, or changed a design decision based on a real insight is what builds trust with the panel.

Ownership of outcomes. The best candidates connect their design decisions to game metrics. If you redesigned a flow, say what happened to the metric it was meant to move, even if the result was mixed.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know the product. Play Dice Dreams and any other SuperPlay title you can find. Take notes as a designer. Where is the onboarding strong? Where do you feel friction? Which monetisation surfaces feel natural versus intrusive? You will almost certainly be asked for your take on their product, and 'I played it last night' signals low preparation.

Week 2: Prepare your case studies. Pick three projects from your portfolio that map to mobile, gaming, or fast-paced consumer products. For each, practise telling the story in under four minutes: the problem, your specific choices, and the outcome. If you have metrics, use them. If you do not, describe the qualitative signal you tracked instead.

Week 3: Practise cross-functional scenarios. SuperPlay teams include PMs, engineers, and live-ops specialists. Prepare two or three stories about navigating trade-offs with non-designers. Focus on how you communicated and what you agreed to, not just what you designed.

Before the interview: Research the 27 open roles at SuperPlay to understand which team or product area your role sits in. Prepare genuine questions about their design process, how they measure UX success, and how the design team works with game PMs. Interviewers notice when questions are generic.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Bringing a web or enterprise portfolio without translation. If your work is not in mobile or gaming, that is fine, but you must do the translation work yourself. Explain explicitly how your skills apply to a live game with daily active users and real monetisation pressure.

Skipping the 'why' behind design decisions. Candidates who walk through visuals without explaining the problem they were solving, or the alternatives they rejected, come across as executors rather than designers.

Being vague about your role on team projects. Say 'I specifically did X' rather than 'we did X.' Interviewers need to assess your individual contribution, and collective language makes that hard.

Underestimating the data conversation. If you have not looked at a retention curve or a day-one and day-seven metric before, spend time on it before your interview. SuperPlay is a data-informed studio and this gap shows quickly.

Not playing the games. Candidates who have clearly not engaged with SuperPlay's products signal low interest. This is one of the easiest and highest-impact preparation steps, and skipping it is a common, avoidable mistake.

Rehearsing answers too rigidly. Interviewers at a gaming studio tend to be direct and curious. If your answers sound scripted, they will probe harder. Practise the structure, not the exact words.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many interview rounds does SuperPlay typically have for Product Designers?

Candidates report a process that typically involves a portfolio review, one or two design-focused conversations, and a cross-functional discussion with a PM or engineer. Some candidates also report a short take-home or live design exercise depending on the role. The exact number of rounds can vary by team, so it is worth asking your recruiter at the start of the process.

Do I need mobile gaming experience to get a Product Designer role at SuperPlay?

Gaming experience is a strong advantage, but candidates from consumer mobile apps have broken in by clearly connecting their work to the mechanics SuperPlay cares about: onboarding, retention loops, and monetisation surfaces. The key is doing that translation work explicitly in your portfolio presentation, not leaving it to the interviewer to figure out on their own.

What salary can I expect as a Product Designer at SuperPlay?

Salary data specific to SuperPlay is not publicly available in detail. Based on knok jobradar data for Product Designer roles across India, mid-level designers (3-5 years) commonly see ranges of 14-24 LPA, while senior designers (6-9 years) are in the 26-40 LPA band. Actual offers depend on your experience level, location, and the specific team's budget.

Does SuperPlay hire Product Designers remotely or only on-site?

SuperPlay's primary offices are based outside India, but candidates report that some product and design roles have had remote or hybrid arrangements for India-based hires. Check the specific job listing for location requirements and ask your recruiter early in the process to avoid surprises at the offer stage.

What should my portfolio include for a SuperPlay application?

Focus on mobile projects, ideally with a game or consumer app component. For each case study, show the problem clearly, your specific design decisions (not just final screens), and any outcome data you can share. A portfolio with three deep, well-told case studies will outperform one with ten surface-level examples. If your background is not in gaming, add a short section explaining how your skills transfer to a live game context.

How long does the SuperPlay hiring process typically take?

Candidates report timelines ranging from two to four weeks from first contact to offer, though this can vary depending on the role's urgency and the team's interview schedules. Following up politely after each stage is reasonable and generally well received by the team.
