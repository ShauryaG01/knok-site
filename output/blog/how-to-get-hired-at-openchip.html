03 Interview Process

Interview Process

The process at Openchip follows a pattern common to semiconductor companies, though exact stages can vary by role and team. Here is what candidates commonly report across similar chip-design firms.

Recruiter screening: The first touchpoint is usually a short call with an HR or talent acquisition person. They check your background, confirm your interest, and explain the role. This is conversational, not technical.

Technical phone screen: A senior engineer or hiring manager will ask technical questions relevant to the role. For hardware roles this often covers RTL concepts, logic design, verification methodologies, or physical design fundamentals. For software roles expect questions on embedded C, firmware, or system-level programming.

Technical assessment or assignment: Some teams send a take-home task or an online coding or design exercise. This is your chance to show practical ability beyond textbook answers.

Panel or loop interviews: The deeper rounds typically involve multiple interviewers covering domain knowledge, system thinking, and sometimes a design problem you work through live. Expect questions that probe how you approach tradeoffs, not just whether you know the right formula.

Hiring manager and leadership round: Before an offer, many candidates speak with a senior leader or the hiring manager directly. This round often focuses on your career goals, how you handle ambiguity, and whether you are a good fit for the team's direction.

Offer and background check: If selected, you will receive a verbal offer followed by a formal letter. Background and reference checks are standard.

Timelines vary. Some candidates move through in a couple of weeks; others report a longer process if multiple teams are involved.

04 What They Look For

What They Look For

Openchip, like most semiconductor companies, values deep technical grounding above most other things. For engineering roles, that means genuine hands-on knowledge, not surface familiarity.

For hardware and chip design roles: Strong RTL skills (Verilog or SystemVerilog), understanding of the full chip development flow, experience with simulation and verification tools, and familiarity with industry-standard EDA tools. Physical design candidates should know floorplanning, place-and-route, and timing closure concepts.

For verification roles: UVM methodology, functional coverage, assertion-based verification, and the ability to write testbenches that actually find bugs.

For embedded and firmware roles: Proficiency in C or C++, understanding of hardware-software interfaces, and experience debugging at the system level.

Across all technical roles: Candidates who can explain their reasoning clearly, who have owned deliverables end-to-end, and who are comfortable with ambiguity tend to stand out. Chip design projects are long cycles, so the team looks for people who are thorough, not just fast.

Soft skills that matter: Communication, especially the ability to work across hardware and software boundaries, and a track record of following through on complex projects. Experience in a startup or small team environment is often viewed positively at growth-stage companies.

05 How To Prepare

How To Prepare

Study the fundamentals specific to your role. For hardware roles, revisit digital design fundamentals, FSMs, timing analysis, and setup and hold constraints. For verification, brush up on UVM phases, sequences, and coverage closure. For embedded roles, practice low-level programming problems and review memory-mapped I/O concepts.

Build a story around one or two real projects. Interviewers at chip-design companies respond well to candidates who can walk through a specific project: what the problem was, what choices were made, what tradeoffs were accepted, and what the result was. Vague answers about 'working on SoC design' are far less convincing than a concrete example.

Look up Openchip's products and focus areas. Understand what kinds of chips or IP they work on. Connecting your experience to their actual product domain shows genuine interest and helps interviewers see the fit quickly.

Practice live design problems. Some rounds will ask you to sketch a design or walk through a verification plan on the spot. Practice talking through your thinking out loud, because interviewers are evaluating your process as much as your answer.

Prepare thoughtful questions. Asking about the tape-out timeline, the verification methodology the team uses, or how cross-functional collaboration works signals that you understand the domain and are serious about the role.

Have your resume reflect real ownership. List tools, methodologies, and specific contributions. Avoid vague phrases like 'assisted with' or 'involved in.' Chip companies want to see what you personally built, verified, or shipped.

06 Culture And Values

Culture And Values

Semiconductor companies operate on long development cycles, tight design constraints, and the understanding that mistakes at the chip level are expensive to fix. That shapes culture in specific ways.

Teams in this space tend to be detail-oriented and process-driven. Precision matters. Engineers are expected to document their work thoroughly, because designs pass through multiple hands across long projects. If you prefer moving fast and iterating loosely, chip design culture can feel slower than software startups, but the payoff is working on technology that ships in silicon and lasts for years.

At growth-stage companies like Openchip, you will likely find a blend of startup energy and engineering rigour. Smaller teams mean more ownership and more visibility. You may wear multiple hats, especially early on. This suits people who want to see the broader picture of a chip development project, not just one narrow slice.

Collaboration across hardware and software boundaries is typical. If you are someone who enjoys understanding the full stack, from RTL down to silicon or from firmware up to application, you will find that valued here.

Work culture in the Indian semiconductor ecosystem, which has grown considerably through 2024-2026, tends to emphasise technical depth, continuous learning, and delivery accountability. Companies in this space invest in people who take ownership of their craft and keep growing their domain expertise.

How many open roles does Openchip have right now?

As of early July 2026, knok jobradar listed 37 open roles at Openchip. That is a healthy number for a chip design company and suggests active hiring across multiple functions and levels. Check the listings regularly because roles open and close as projects move forward.

What qualifications do I need to apply for a hardware engineering role at Openchip?

Most hardware engineering roles at chip design companies require a degree in electronics, electrical engineering, or a related field, along with hands-on experience in RTL design, verification, or physical design depending on the specific position. Freshers with strong final-year projects in VLSI or chip design sometimes get through as well. The key is demonstrating real technical ability, not just a degree.

Is there a coding round in the interview process?

It depends on the role. Verification and embedded software roles are more likely to include a coding assessment focused on C, Python, or SystemVerilog. Pure RTL or physical design roles may instead give you a design problem or ask you to review a timing report. Prepare for both possibilities and confirm with the recruiter what to expect for your specific role.

How long does the hiring process typically take?

Semiconductor company hiring processes tend to be thorough. Candidates at similar firms commonly report a process spanning a few weeks to over a month, depending on how many interview rounds are involved and how quickly the team can coordinate. Following up politely with the recruiter after each stage is fine and shows continued interest.

Can freshers or recent graduates apply to Openchip?

Growth-phase semiconductor companies do hire freshers, especially for verification, design, or embedded roles where strong academic projects can substitute for industry experience. If Openchip does not have a fresher-specific listing, applying to junior or associate-level roles and making your project work the centrepiece of your resume is a good approach.
