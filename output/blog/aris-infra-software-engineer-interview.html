03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format for all behavioural and experience-based questions: Situation (brief context), Task (your specific responsibility), Action (what YOU did), Result (measurable outcome where possible). Here are three examples you can adapt.

Q: Tell me about a time you improved the performance of a slow API.

*Situation:* At my previous company, our order listing API was taking several seconds to respond during peak hours and the product team was receiving client complaints.

*Task:* I was asked to investigate and fix the issue before a major client demo.

*Action:* I profiled the API using query logs and found multiple N+1 query problems in the ORM layer. I rewrote those queries with proper joins, added database indexes on the relevant foreign key columns, and introduced a cache layer for the most frequently requested filters.

*Result:* Response time dropped to well under a second. The demo went smoothly, the client renewed, and I documented the pattern so the team could apply it to other endpoints.

---

Q: Describe a time you had to make a technical trade-off under time pressure.

*Situation:* We were building a vendor onboarding feature with a hard go-live deadline a few weeks away.

*Task:* I had to choose between a fully automated document verification flow or a semi-manual one to meet the deadline.

*Action:* I chose the semi-manual approach for the first release, clearly documented the technical debt, and set up a basic queue so the manual step could be replaced in a later sprint. I aligned with the product manager on the plan upfront.

*Result:* We launched on time with no client disruption. The automated flow shipped the following sprint. The product manager appreciated the transparency about the trade-off.

---

Q: Tell me about a bug that was hard to reproduce.

*Situation:* Users on our mobile app reported intermittent checkout failures, but the bug did not appear in staging.

*Task:* I was assigned to investigate with no clear reproduction steps and no server-side errors in the logs.

*Action:* I added detailed client-side event logging and used feature flags to isolate affected users. After collecting logs for a couple of days, I found the bug only triggered on slow networks where concurrent API calls sometimes completed in the wrong order, causing a race condition.

*Result:* I added an idempotency check on the server and a loading lock on the checkout button. The bug was eliminated, and we also caught a couple of similar race conditions in the same flow during the code review.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For technical design questions, use the 'clarify, scope, design, trade-offs' structure. Start by asking about scale, key users, and constraints. Then outline high-level components before diving into details. Always discuss trade-offs, for example consistency vs. availability, or build vs. buy.

For behavioural questions, use STAR. Keep the Situation and Task brief. Spend most of your answer on Action (specifically what you did, not the team) and Result (a measurable or qualitative outcome). Weak STAR answers often have a rich Situation and a vague or missing Result.

For 'why this company' questions, connect your experience directly to the problem Aris Infra is solving. Construction and building materials procurement is a large, largely offline market being digitised. If you have worked on B2B platforms, logistics, or supply chain software, draw that line explicitly. Show you are interested in the domain, not just in 'working at a startup.'

For coding rounds, candidates report that Aris Infra interviewers care as much about your reasoning as your final solution. Start with a brute-force approach, state its time and space complexity, and then optimise. Think out loud throughout so the interviewer can follow your logic.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Startups like Aris Infra typically look for a few things beyond raw technical ability.

Ownership and initiative. Can you take a vague problem, break it down, and deliver without constant hand-holding? Give examples where you drove something to completion independently, including handling ambiguity and unblocking yourself.

Product sense. Do you understand why you are building something, not just how? Engineers at growth-stage startups often interact directly with clients or internal ops teams. Showing curiosity about the business context behind a technical decision is a strong signal.

Practical engineering judgment. They want engineers who ship clean, maintainable code and know when to be pragmatic. Demonstrating that you understand technical debt and can balance speed with quality is valued. Over-engineering is a red flag at this stage.

Clear communication. Can you explain a complex technical decision to a non-technical stakeholder? This matters especially in a domain like construction, where buyers and suppliers may not be tech-savvy. Practise explaining your past work in plain terms before the interview.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Technical Foundations

Revise core data structures and algorithms with a focus on arrays, hash maps, trees, graphs, and dynamic programming. Practice medium-difficulty problems on a coding platform of your choice. Also revise SQL: joins, indexes, query optimisation, and window functions. These come up often in backend-focused interviews.

Week 2: System Design and Domain Knowledge

Study practical system design concepts: REST API design, relational database schema design, caching strategies, message queues, and the basics of microservices. Spend time reading about procurement workflows and supply chain basics. Even a surface-level understanding of how materials procurement works will help you answer domain questions credibly and show genuine interest in what Aris Infra is building.

Week 3: Behavioural Prep and Mock Interviews

Write down 5-6 strong STAR stories covering: a technical challenge you solved, a disagreement with a colleague or manager, a project you led or owned, a mistake you made and what you learned, and a trade-off you managed under pressure. Do at least a couple of mock interviews with a friend or on a mock interview platform. Practise your 'why Aris Infra' answer until it sounds natural, not rehearsed.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping straight into code without clarifying requirements. Interviewers at product startups often give deliberately vague problems to see if you ask the right questions. Spend a couple of minutes clarifying scope, constraints, and expected scale before writing a single line.

Generic 'why this company' answers. Saying 'I want to work at a startup for the growth' is not enough. Candidates report that interviewers probe for genuine interest in the construction and procurement domain. Know what Aris Infra builds and why it matters.

STAR answers with no result. Many candidates describe what they did but skip the outcome. Always end with a result, even if it is qualitative, for example: 'the team adopted this pattern for all new services' or 'the client renewed after the fix.'

Over-engineering system design answers. Early-stage startups want pragmatic, right-sized solutions. Proposing a globally distributed multi-region setup for a domestic procurement tool signals poor product judgment. Match your design to the stated constraints.

Not asking questions at the end. Candidates who have no questions come across as disengaged. Prepare 2-3 questions about the engineering culture, current technical challenges, the product roadmap, or how the team handles incidents and on-call.
