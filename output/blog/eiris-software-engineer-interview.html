01 Overview

Overview

EIRIS is a research and data company focused on ESG (Environmental, Social, Governance) analysis and responsible investment insights. Their software engineering team typically builds data pipelines, research platforms, and client-facing tools that process large volumes of financial and sustainability data. As of mid-2026, knok's job radar shows 1 open Software Engineer role at EIRIS, so competition is sharp and thorough preparation matters.

The interview process at EIRIS typically spans a few rounds: an initial screening call with HR or a recruiter, one or two technical rounds covering coding and system design, and a final round with a senior engineer or team lead. Candidates report the overall tone is collaborative rather than adversarial, with interviewers genuinely interested in how you think through problems rather than trying to trip you up.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These are questions candidates commonly report from EIRIS Software Engineer interviews, shaped by the company's focus on data quality, research platforms, and financial technology.

  1. Walk us through a data pipeline you built from scratch. How did you handle failures?
  2. How would you design a system that processes and stores large volumes of ESG or financial data reliably?
  3. Explain the difference between REST and GraphQL. When would you choose one over the other?
  4. Describe a time you had to deal with dirty or incomplete data. How did you clean and validate it?
  5. How do you approach writing unit tests for business-critical logic?
  6. Have you worked with any scheduling or workflow tools such as Airflow or cron jobs? Walk us through an example.
  7. How would you optimize a slow SQL query on a large dataset?
  8. Describe your experience with cloud platforms (AWS, GCP, or Azure). What services have you used and why?
  9. Tell me about a time you had to refactor legacy code. What was your approach and what did you learn?
  10. How do you stay current with new technologies? Give a recent example of something you learned and applied.
  11. If a client-facing dashboard is showing incorrect data, how do you debug and resolve it under pressure?
  12. How do you prioritize tasks when you have multiple bugs and a feature deadline at the same time?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time you built or improved a data pipeline.

*Situation:* At my previous company, we had a batch ETL pipeline pulling data from multiple external APIs. It would silently fail overnight, and the team would only discover missing data during the next morning's standup.

*Task:* I was asked to make the pipeline reliable and add alerting so failures surfaced immediately rather than the next day.

*Action:* I refactored the pipeline to add retry logic with exponential backoff for transient API errors, added schema validation at each ingestion step, and set up a lightweight alerting system using email notifications on failure. I also wrote integration tests against a mock API so regressions would be caught before deployment.

*Result:* The overnight failure rate dropped sharply, and when failures did occur, the team was notified within minutes rather than hours. This freed up meaningful debugging time each week across the whole team.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you optimized a slow database query.

*Situation:* A reporting dashboard at my last job was timing out for enterprise clients with large datasets. Users complained that a key report took a very long time to load and they were losing confidence in the product.

*Task:* I needed to bring query time down to an acceptable level without breaking existing functionality or requiring downtime.

*Action:* I used EXPLAIN ANALYZE to identify a missing composite index and a join that was doing unnecessary full-table scans. I added the index, rewrote the join, and pushed an aggregation step to a materialized view that refreshed nightly.

*Result:* Query response time dropped significantly. Industry surveys commonly cite 80-90% reductions for similar index and join optimizations, and our improvement was in that range. No functionality broke, and the fix went out with zero downtime.

---

Q: Describe a time you had to debug a production issue under pressure.

*Situation:* On a Friday evening, a client reported that their compliance dashboard was showing stale data while our monitoring showed the pipeline had 'completed successfully.'

*Task:* I had to identify why fresh data was not reaching the dashboard even though the pipeline reported no errors.

*Action:* I traced the data flow from ingestion through to the front-end cache layer. I found a misconfigured cache TTL that was serving day-old data even after a successful pipeline run. I pushed a config fix to reset the TTL and added a cache-bust step at the end of the pipeline.

*Result:* The client saw live data quickly after I started investigating. I wrote a postmortem and added a cache-freshness check to our monitoring so the same silent failure could not repeat.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Two frameworks work well for EIRIS interviews.

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is best for behavioral questions. Keep Situation and Task brief (a sentence or two each) so you spend most of your time on Action and Result. Quantify Results wherever you can, but if you do not have exact numbers, use ranges or phrases like 'significantly reduced' or cite industry benchmarks.

C4 for system design (Context, Constraints, Components, Concerns) helps you structure open-ended design questions. Start by clarifying context (what data, what scale, what clients), name your constraints (latency, cost, consistency), then walk through the components of your design, and finish by addressing failure modes and trade-offs. EIRIS engineers work with financial and ESG data, so showing awareness of data quality, auditability, and compliance is a strong differentiator here compared to a generic product startup.

For coding rounds, think out loud. Interviewers at companies like EIRIS typically care as much about your reasoning as your final solution. State your assumptions, name the trade-offs of different approaches, and ask clarifying questions before you start writing code.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

EIRIS interviewers are typically looking for technical depth, ownership, and domain awareness across all rounds.

Technical depth means you can go beyond surface-level answers. If you say you used a message queue, be ready to explain why you chose it, what alternatives you considered, and how you handled message ordering or failures.

Ownership shows up in behavioral questions. Interviewers want to hear that you noticed a problem, took initiative, and saw it through to a result rather than waiting for someone else to act. Use 'I' rather than 'we' when describing your specific contributions, even when the work was collaborative.

Domain awareness matters more at a data-focused company like EIRIS than at a generic product startup. You do not need deep ESG knowledge, but showing you understand concepts like data lineage, audit trails, idempotent pipelines, and data quality validation will set you apart. Candidates who ask thoughtful questions about EIRIS's data products tend to leave stronger impressions in final rounds.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused one-week plan that candidates have found effective:

Days 1-2: Review core data structures and algorithms. Focus on arrays, hashmaps, trees, and graph traversal, since these cover the majority of coding questions at mid-level software engineer interviews. Practice on a platform you are already comfortable with.

Days 3-4: Brush up on SQL. Write queries involving JOINs, window functions, and aggregations on sample datasets. Since EIRIS handles structured data, SQL questions are commonly reported in their technical rounds. Also review one system design topic: design a data ingestion pipeline that handles failures gracefully.

Day 5: Research EIRIS. Read their website, look at what ESG data products they offer, and note any recent news. Prepare a few specific questions to ask the interviewer about the team's tech stack, deployment cadence, and data quality processes.

Day 6: Practice behavioral answers using STAR. Prepare stories for: a time you fixed a bug in production, a time you disagreed with a teammate, and a time you took initiative on a task no one assigned you.

Day 7: Do a mock interview with a friend or record yourself answering questions out loud. Rest well before the actual interview.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

  1. Jumping into code without clarifying requirements. Interviewers at data-focused companies often embed an ambiguity in the problem deliberately. Spending a few minutes asking questions upfront shows maturity and saves you from solving the wrong problem entirely.
  1. Vague answers to behavioral questions. Saying 'our team improved performance' without explaining your specific role and a concrete result leaves interviewers unconvinced. Prepare specific examples in advance so you are not improvising stories on the spot.
  1. Ignoring data quality in system design. For a company like EIRIS, a design answer that treats data as always clean and complete will raise red flags. Always address validation, error handling, and what happens when upstream data is late or malformed.
  1. Not asking questions at the end. Candidates who ask no questions signal low interest. Prepare at least a couple of genuine questions about the role or the team.
  1. Overclaiming on past work. If an interviewer probes deeper and your answer unravels, trust is lost. Be honest about your team contributions and your individual scope from the start.
  1. Skipping the 'why.' Whether explaining a technical choice or describing a career move, interviewers want to understand your reasoning, not just what you did. Always be ready to explain why you made the decision you made.

How many rounds does the EIRIS Software Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically includes an HR screening call, one to two technical rounds covering coding, SQL, or system design, and a final round with a senior team member or hiring manager. The exact structure can vary based on the level you are applying for and what the team needs at the time. It is worth asking the recruiter upfront so you know what to expect and can pace your energy accordingly.

What programming languages does EIRIS typically ask about?

Candidates have reported Python and Java coming up in technical rounds, given EIRIS's focus on data processing and backend services. Being comfortable with SQL is equally important given the data-heavy nature of the work. It is worth confirming the team's primary stack during your HR screening call so you can tailor your preparation accordingly.

Is there a take-home assignment in the EIRIS interview process?

Some candidates report receiving a short take-home coding or data problem, though this is not universal. Typically, if there is one, it involves processing or transforming a sample dataset. Clean, readable code with sensible variable names matters as much as a correct solution, so treat it carefully and do not rush.
