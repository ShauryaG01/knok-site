02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates report that CommIT EM interviews are consistently behavioural and situational, with technical depth probed through discussion rather than live coding. These are the questions most commonly flagged:

  1. Walk me through how you built or scaled an engineering team from scratch.
  2. Tell me about a time your team missed a major deadline. What happened and what did you do next?
  3. How do you balance shipping features against paying down technical debt when product is pushing hard for speed?
  4. Describe a situation where two senior engineers on your team had a serious conflict. How did you resolve it?
  5. How do you run performance reviews, and what do you do when a strong individual contributor begins underperforming in a tech lead role?
  6. CommIT moves at pace. Tell me about a time you had to make a major call with incomplete information.
  7. How do you keep yourself technically credible without becoming a bottleneck or a micro-manager?
  8. Describe how you have worked with product managers to push back on scope without damaging the relationship.
  9. Walk me through your hiring process. What made you choose the last person you brought onto your team?
  10. How do you measure team health, and what early warning signs tell you something is going wrong?
  11. Tell me about a time you had to manage someone out. What was your process and what did you learn?
  12. How do you think about career growth for senior engineers who have no interest in moving into management?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a time your team missed a major deadline.

*Situation:* My team was building a payments integration for an enterprise client. The original timeline was ten weeks. By week six it was clear we were going to miss by at least three weeks.

*Task:* I needed to manage client expectations honestly, stabilise the team, and get delivery back on track without losing the account.

*Action:* I contacted the client immediately rather than waiting. I presented a revised schedule with clear milestones and daily written updates. Internally I dropped two lower-priority features after aligning with product, ran a short daily blocker session, and paired directly with the two engineers stuck on the hardest module to unblock them faster.

*Result:* We delivered the core integration one week past the original deadline, which the client accepted because of the consistent communication throughout. The two deferred features shipped in the following sprint. The client renewed their contract.

---

Q: How do you handle a serious conflict between two senior engineers?

*Situation:* Two senior engineers disagreed sharply on the architecture for a new service. The debate had turned personal and was slowing the whole team down.

*Task:* Resolve the technical question and repair the working relationship before it damaged team culture.

*Action:* I met each engineer separately first to understand their reasoning without the other person present. I then set up a structured design review where both proposals had to be written down with trade-offs listed explicitly. I brought in a neutral senior engineer from another team for a third perspective. I facilitated the meeting on technical merits and made the final call clearly so neither person felt it was a win or a loss.

*Result:* The team adopted a hybrid approach. Both engineers stayed and continued to work well together. The written proposal process became our default for major architecture decisions going forward.

---

Q: How do you stay technically credible without micro-managing?

*Situation:* When I moved into my first EM role, the team was used to a very hands-on tech lead. Stepping back completely risked losing credibility and missing real technical risks.

*Task:* Find a balance where engineers owned decisions but I stayed close enough to catch problems early.

*Action:* I blocked two hours a week for code review on non-critical PRs, purely to stay current. I joined architecture discussions as a facilitator and question-asker rather than the person proposing solutions. I introduced a lightweight RFC process so significant decisions were visible to me before they were locked in.

*Result:* Engineers consistently rated autonomy highly in quarterly pulse checks. I caught two significant scalability issues early through the RFC process before they reached production. No engineer felt their implementation choices were being second-guessed.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for all behavioural questions. Every 'tell me about a time' question at CommIT calls for the STAR structure: Situation (brief context), Task (what you personally owned), Action (what YOU did, not the team), Result (specific outcome). Keep Situation and Task together under two minutes. Invest most of your answer in Action and Result, because that is where interviewers evaluate you.

Trade-off framing for strategic questions. When asked about balancing tech debt, scope, or competing priorities, there is no single correct answer. Show that you think in trade-offs. Name the tension clearly, walk through the factors you weigh (team capacity, business risk, customer impact), then explain how you make and communicate the call.

Who, what, how for people management questions. Structure people and hiring stories around: who you were dealing with (their level, what made the situation complex), what the specific challenge was, and how you handled it step by step. Avoid generic statements like 'I give regular feedback.' Show the specific conversation with a specific person and a specific outcome.

A clear management point of view. CommIT interviewers often ask directly how you work as a manager. Have a rehearsed but genuine answer covering your communication norms, how you run one-on-ones, and how you handle disagreement. Managers who sound self-aware and intentional about their own style tend to advance further in the process.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

CommIT Engineering Manager interviews are designed to filter for a specific kind of leader. Candidates who advance typically demonstrate all of the following:

Technical grounding. You do not need to be writing production code daily, but you need to engage credibly on architecture, trade-offs, and engineering quality. Interviewers will probe whether you understand the 'why' behind technical decisions or are simply nodding along.

Ownership without excuses. When something goes wrong in your stories, CommIT interviewers want to see you own your part clearly. Deflecting blame to product, senior leadership, or 'unclear requirements' is a consistent red flag. Taking responsibility and showing what you changed afterward is what advances candidates.

Genuine people instinct. This goes beyond knowing the theory of one-on-ones and performance frameworks. Interviewers want to see curiosity about what actually motivates individual engineers, and evidence that you adjust your approach per person rather than per playbook.

Bias toward action under ambiguity. CommIT moves quickly. Candidates who describe waiting for full clarity or escalating uncertain decisions tend not to advance. Show that you make reasonable calls, communicate them clearly, and course-correct fast when you are wrong.

Stakeholder communication. Engineering Managers at CommIT work closely with product and sometimes directly with clients. Interviewers look for candidates who can translate between technical and non-technical audiences without dumbing things down or hiding behind jargon.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Two to three weeks before:
Write down ten to fifteen stories from your career covering delivery under pressure, conflict resolution, hiring, performance management, technical decision-making, and stakeholder communication. For each story, note a specific and concrete outcome. Vague results like 'the team improved' will hurt you.

One week before:
Research CommIT's engineering blog, LinkedIn posts from their engineering leaders, and any publicly available product announcements. You do not need to know every product in depth, but you should be able to speak to what CommIT builds and what engineering challenges that implies. Candidates who reference CommIT's actual products typically get stronger engagement from interviewers.

Three to five days before:
Practice your top five stories out loud, timed. STAR answers for EM roles should run two to three minutes each. Record yourself once. You will immediately notice if you are being too vague or cutting short on the Result. Practice the trade-off framing for at least two strategic questions.

Day before:
Prepare three to four questions to ask the interviewer. Strong choices: how CommIT defines success for an EM in the first six months, how the engineering organisation is structured, and what the biggest challenge the team you would join is currently working through. Avoid asking about salary in early rounds.

Day of:
For video rounds, which candidates report are common at CommIT, check your setup ahead of time: stable connection, neutral background, good lighting. Speak at a measured pace. Nerves tend to make people rush, which compresses the Result part of STAR answers, where you make the strongest impression.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Leading with 'we' instead of 'I.' Engineering Managers often default to 'we did this, we built that.' Interviewers are evaluating you, not your team. Be specific about what you personally decided, initiated, or changed.

Over-indexing on technical depth. Candidates transitioning from Staff or Principal Engineer roles sometimes spend too much time on technical details and not enough on leadership and people management stories. CommIT interviewers are equally interested in both halves of the role.

Giving textbook answers to people questions. 'I give regular feedback and run one-on-ones every week' tells an interviewer nothing they have not heard a hundred times. Give a specific example with a specific person and a specific outcome.

Not asking clarifying questions before answering. EM interview questions are often deliberately broad. Candidates who launch straight into an answer sometimes address the wrong version of the question entirely. A quick check like 'just to confirm, you are asking about a scenario where X?' signals maturity and saves you from going in the wrong direction.

Avoiding the hard stories. Many candidates choose only their success stories. CommIT interviewers typically ask directly about failures, missed deadlines, and difficult personnel situations. A story set with only positive outcomes reads as either inexperienced or evasive.

Not knowing your numbers. 'The team improved velocity' is weak. 'Cycle time dropped from twelve days to five over two quarters' is strong. Go back through your career and attach a specific metric to every major outcome you plan to discuss.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the CommIT Engineering Manager interview typically have?

Candidates typically report three to five rounds for EM roles at CommIT. These commonly include a recruiter or HR screening call, one or two leadership and behavioural rounds, a technical discussion, and a final round with a senior engineering leader. Some candidates also report an early hiring manager intro call. The exact structure varies by team, level, and whether the role is a manager, senior manager, or director track.

Is there a live coding round for Engineering Manager roles at CommIT?

Most candidates report that there is no hands-on coding round for EM positions at CommIT. However, there is typically a technical discussion where you are expected to engage credibly on system design, architectural trade-offs, and engineering quality decisions. Being able to reason through a high-level design or explain the thinking behind a past technical call is important, even though you are not writing code live.

What salary can I expect as an Engineering Manager at CommIT?

Based on knok jobradar data, the Engineering Manager band at CommIT is broadly 35-60 LPA, Senior Manager is 55-90 LPA, and Director-level roles go 90-150+ LPA. Actual offers depend on your current compensation, years of relevant experience, and the size and scope of teams you have managed. For additional data points, Glassdoor and levels.fyi carry publicly reported CommIT compensation figures from current and former employees.

How long does the CommIT EM hiring process take from first contact to offer?

Candidates typically report a process running three to six weeks from first contact to offer, though timelines vary depending on team urgency, notice period considerations, and how quickly rounds are scheduled. Some candidates have reported faster turnarounds when a team had an immediate opening. Politely following up after each round is reasonable and is generally expected in this process.

Should I prepare differently if I am coming from a startup versus a large company?

Yes. Startup candidates should emphasise breadth: the range of decisions you made, how you operated under ambiguity, and how you built process from scratch. CommIT interviewers tend to value startup backgrounds where candidates have managed without a large support structure around them. Candidates from large companies should emphasise speed, the ability to make calls without extensive consensus, and experience navigating complex stakeholder groups. In both cases, specific stories with specific outcomes matter far more than general statements about your management style.

How important is domain expertise for CommIT EM roles?

Candidates report that CommIT places more weight on leadership capability and engineering fundamentals than on specific domain knowledge. That said, being able to speak to the engineering challenges relevant to the team you are joining (scale, reliability, distributed systems, and so on) does make a positive difference. Research the specific team in advance and frame your past experience in terms of the problems they are actually solving.
