01 Overview

Overview

Sardine is a fraud, compliance, and identity platform used by fintechs, crypto companies, and banks to catch payment fraud, screen for AML risk, and verify users in real time. A Solutions Engineer here sits at the intersection of technical depth and customer success: you own the technical side of the sales cycle, run product demos, guide API onboarding, and help prospects see exactly how sardine fits their stack.

Candidates report a process that typically runs three to four rounds, starting with a recruiter screen, moving into a technical discussion, and ending with a customer-scenario or case study exercise. Sardine currently has 35 open roles across functions, which signals active hiring. For context, 1,270 Solutions Engineer positions are open across India as of mid-2026, so demand is strong and competition is real.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through how you would demo sardine's fraud detection API to a risk team at a mid-size neobank.

*Situation:* At my previous company I was asked to demo our risk API to a neobank's fraud and product teams who had already seen three competitors and were skeptical of generic walkthroughs.

*Task:* My goal was to run a live, scenario-based demo that mapped directly to their fraud patterns rather than a canned slide deck.

*Action:* I spent two days before the call reviewing their public product flows and common fraud vectors for UPI-based neobanks. I set up a sandbox pre-loaded with realistic transaction sequences, including a synthetic account-takeover scenario. During the demo I narrated each API response in plain language and let their lead engineer ask questions live while I adjusted parameters in real time.

*Result:* They agreed to a proof-of-concept the following week. Their risk lead said it was 'the first demo that actually felt like our problem.' The POC converted to a signed contract within a few weeks.

---

Q: A client's integration is throwing a high rate of false positives on legitimate transactions. How do you diagnose and fix this?

*Situation:* A fintech client we had recently onboarded reported that a noticeable share of genuine customers were being flagged and blocked, which was hurting their activation rate.

*Task:* I needed to find the root cause quickly, since every blocked legitimate user was lost revenue for the client.

*Action:* I pulled a sample of flagged events from their dashboard and grouped them by device type. A large share came from a single Android OEM whose WebView sends unusual device signals that the default ruleset treats as suspicious. I walked the client's engineering team through adjusting the confidence threshold for that device segment and adding an exception rule scoped to verified returning users on that device family.

*Result:* False positives on those legitimate transactions dropped sharply within two days of the config change. I documented the pattern and shared it internally so the fix could be offered proactively to other clients on similar stacks.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you lost a deal or a client escalated a serious issue. What happened and what did you change?

*Situation:* Early in my Solutions Engineer role, I was running a proof-of-concept for a lending platform that wanted fraud scoring on their loan application flow.

*Task:* The POC had a defined window to show measurable lift in fraud catch rate. I was the primary technical contact.

*Action:* Midway through, the client's team hit a blocker with webhook signature verification that I had not flagged upfront as a common integration pain point. I had assumed they would catch it in the documentation. The POC slipped by two weeks, the client's procurement timeline reset, and we lost the deal to a competitor with a faster integration path.

*Result:* After that I built a 'common blockers' checklist covering auth flows, webhook verification, and rate limits, which I now share with every client at kickoff. The next three POCs I ran all went live on schedule.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (for behavioral questions). Start with a brief Situation and Task to set the scene, then spend most of your time on the Action, which is where interviewers learn how you think. End with a concrete Result. Keep the Situation short: two to three sentences is enough.

Discovery before demo. For any question about presenting or positioning sardine, lead with what you would ask or learn before opening a slide or API console. This signals a consultative mindset. Name two or three specific questions you would ask about the client's current fraud stack, transaction volumes, and main pain points.

Technical explainer (for 'explain X to a non-technical person' questions). Use a short everyday analogy, state the problem it solves in one sentence, then describe the mechanism in plain terms. Hold off on jargon until the interviewer introduces it.

Situation, Complication, Resolution (for problem-solving questions). Useful when STAR feels forced. Describe what was expected to happen, what went wrong, and the exact steps you took to resolve it. This works well for technical debugging and client escalation scenarios.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Sardine's Solutions Engineers work at the crossroads of fraud domain expertise, technical depth, and customer success. Interviewers typically look for four things.

Domain knowledge. You should speak fluently about fraud vectors (account takeover, payment fraud, synthetic identity fraud), AML obligations, and KYC workflows. Fintech or payments experience is a strong signal. If your background is adjacent, show that you have done the reading before the interview.

Technical credibility. Expect questions on REST APIs, webhooks, SDKs, and data schemas. You do not need to write production code, but you must be comfortable reading API docs, debugging integration issues, and explaining technical trade-offs to an engineering team.

Customer empathy. Sardine sells to risk teams, product teams, and engineering teams at the same time. Interviewers want to see that you adapt your communication style to the audience and that you tie every technical decision back to the client's actual business outcome.

Honesty under uncertainty. If you do not know the answer to a domain or technical question, say so clearly and explain how you would find out. Candidates who bluff on specifics are quickly spotted in a specialist interview.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: product and domain foundation. Read sardine's public documentation, blog, and any available case studies. Map their three core pillars (fraud detection, AML compliance, identity verification) to real client problems. Review the RBI's KYC guidelines and India's PMLA requirements at a high level, since these are likely to come up in any discussion about Indian fintech clients.

Week 2: technical readiness. Sardine publishes developer documentation publicly. Practice reading through API responses and be ready to explain fields like risk scores, device signals, and confidence thresholds in plain language. Rehearse explaining behavioral biometrics and device fingerprinting to a non-technical listener until it feels natural.

Week 3: scenario and case study prep. Candidates report at least one customer-scenario exercise, typically a live role-play or a written case study. Practice the discovery-before-demo framework: prepare a list of five to six questions you would ask before ever opening a demo environment. Run a five-minute mock demo of any product you know well, to build the muscle of narrating a live technical walkthrough under pressure.

Throughout. Research sardine's recent product announcements and publicly reported partnerships. Know the names of companies commonly cited as competitors in the fraud-detection space, as discussed on Glassdoor and in industry analyst coverage. Prepare two or three thoughtful questions for the interviewer that show you have done this research.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Being too generic. Giving answers about 'solutions engineering in general' without anchoring to fraud, compliance, or fintech. Sardine is a specialist product, and interviewers expect specialist context, not a generic enterprise SaaS pitch.

Skipping discovery. Jumping straight to 'I would show them the demo' without explaining what you would ask first. This signals a product-pusher mindset rather than a consultative one, which is a red flag for an SE role at a company whose product requires careful technical fit assessment.

Over-claiming technical depth. Saying you can write integration code when you cannot, or describing product features you have not verified. Technical rounds typically include live API or schema questions, so gaps in claimed expertise surface quickly.

Ignoring the business outcome. Describing integrations in purely technical terms without connecting the work to fraud reduction, revenue protection, or compliance coverage. Always close the loop: explain what the technical action means for what the client actually cares about.

Weak failure answers. Giving a 'challenge that turned out fine with no real lesson' to the 'tell me about a failure' question. Interviewers want genuine reflection and a visible change in behavior afterward. A recycled success story dressed as a failure is one of the most common and most visible mistakes in this format.

Frequently asked

How many interview rounds does the sardine Solutions Engineer process typically have?

Candidates report three to four rounds in most cases: a recruiter screen, a technical discussion, and a customer-scenario or case study exercise. Some roles include an additional round with a senior stakeholder. The exact structure can vary by region and team, so ask your recruiter at the start to confirm what to expect.

Do I need a software engineering background to apply?

You do not need to write production code, but a working knowledge of REST APIs, webhooks, and JSON is important. Expect questions about reading API documentation, debugging integration issues, and explaining technical concepts to non-engineering stakeholders. A background in fintech, risk, or payments pre-sales typically carries more weight than pure software engineering depth.

How important is knowledge of Indian regulatory requirements like RBI norms and PMLA?

Quite important if you are targeting Indian fintech clients. Candidates report that interviewers ask about KYC norms, AML obligations under PMLA, and RBI compliance guidelines. You do not need to be a compliance specialist, but you should be able to explain why a neobank or lending platform faces these requirements and how a fraud and identity platform helps them meet them.

Is prior fraud or risk domain experience mandatory?

Not mandatory, but it is a strong advantage. Candidates with backgrounds in payments, banking technology, or enterprise SaaS pre-sales also do well. What matters most is the ability to learn fraud domain concepts quickly and connect them to client business problems. If your background is adjacent, invest your preparation time in closing the domain knowledge gap before the first round.
