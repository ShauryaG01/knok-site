03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format for all behavioral questions: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep the Situation and Task brief. Spend the most time on Action, since that is where you show what you personally did and how you thought through the problem.

Q: Tell me about a time you fixed a performance problem in a production system.

*Situation:* A key API endpoint used by our mobile app was responding very slowly during peak traffic hours, causing visible loading delays for users.

*Task:* I was asked to investigate and resolve the issue without taking the service offline.

*Action:* I added query profiling to the endpoint and found an N+1 query problem introduced during a recent feature addition. I rewrote the query using a join, added a composite index on the most-filtered columns, and introduced a short-lived cache for data that rarely changes.

*Result:* Response time dropped sharply. The mobile team confirmed a clear improvement in user experience, and the fix held through subsequent traffic peaks without regression.

---

Q: Describe a time you worked across teams to deliver a feature.

*Situation:* We were building a notification system that required input from the backend platform team, the frontend team, and the data team for event tracking.

*Task:* I was the backend engineer responsible for the API contracts and the push notification service.

*Action:* I drafted API specs early and ran a short alignment session with each team to surface blockers before they became problems. I built versioned endpoints so the frontend team could test against staging while I was still finalising business logic. I agreed on an event schema with the data team upfront so analytics would be live from day one.

*Result:* The feature shipped on schedule with no integration issues at launch. The data team began reporting on notification engagement immediately, and the product team used those insights in the next planning cycle.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you made a technical trade-off under pressure.

*Situation:* We had a firm product deadline for a new search filter feature, but the cleanest solution required refactoring a core module that would take weeks.

*Task:* As the engineer leading the feature, I had to decide whether to push back on the timeline or find a more pragmatic path.

*Action:* I proposed a two-phase approach: ship a working implementation using a targeted workaround that met the product requirements, paired with a scheduled refactor in the next sprint. I documented the technical debt clearly in our tracker and got explicit sign-off from the engineering manager so the follow-up work was protected.

*Result:* We shipped on time. The workaround caused no user-facing issues during that window, and the refactor completed as planned in the following sprint.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For behavioral questions: STAR

STAR stands for Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task brief. Spend the most time on Action: what you personally did, the reasoning behind your choices, and any obstacles you navigated. Close with a concrete Result. Avoid vague actions like 'I helped the team' and weak closings like 'it went well.' Be specific about your contribution and what actually changed.

For system design questions: Clarify first, then build

Before drawing any component boxes, ask clarifying questions. Who are the users? What is the expected scale? What are the consistency and availability requirements? Then walk through your design top-down: API layer, business logic, data storage, async processing if needed. Call out trade-offs at each step rather than waiting for the interviewer to probe.

For coding questions: Think aloud throughout

State your approach before writing any code. Mention time and space complexity as you go. Handle edge cases out loud. If a brute-force solution comes to mind first, say so, then explain how you would optimise it. Candidates typically report that Seek interviewers care as much about your reasoning process as about the final working answer.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Seek's engineering work centres on search relevance, large-scale data processing, and product features that serve a very large and diverse user base. Interviewers are typically looking for a combination of technical depth, clear communication, and demonstrated ownership.

Technical depth: Can you design a system that handles real scale? Can you write clean, correct code and reason about its complexity? Do you understand trade-offs between different storage or processing approaches?

Ownership and impact: Interviewers at Seek typically want to hear about impact, not just activity. 'I implemented feature X' is weaker than 'I identified the bottleneck, proposed the approach, built it, and the outcome was Y.' Use 'I' rather than 'we' when describing your specific contribution.

Collaboration: Much of Seek's engineering work involves coordination across product, data, and platform teams. Candidates who demonstrate a clear track record of working well across functions tend to stand out.

Curiosity: Given Seek's focus on AI-driven matching and personalisation, interviewers often probe whether you actively follow relevant developments and apply what you learn in your day-to-day work.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Coding fundamentals

Practise data structures and algorithms covering arrays, strings, trees, graphs, and dynamic programming. Aim for clean, readable solutions rather than just passing tests. Practice explaining your approach out loud as you code, since this is what interviewers observe.

Week 2: System design

Study how large-scale search and recommendation systems are built. Key concepts to cover: caching strategies, message queues, database indexing and sharding, horizontal scaling, and consistency trade-offs. Sketch a few end-to-end designs relevant to Seek: a job search backend, a notification service, or a real-time applicant analytics pipeline.

Week 3: Behavioral preparation

Write out STAR stories for at least six situations from your own experience: a performance win, a cross-team collaboration, a conflict or disagreement, a time you took ownership outside your role, a time you learned something new quickly, and a situation where you made a hard technical trade-off.

Week 4: Mock interviews and research

Do timed mock sessions for both coding and system design. Read about Seek's products so you can connect your answers to challenges that are genuinely relevant to them. Prepare two or three thoughtful questions to ask your interviewers. 07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

  1. Jumping straight into code. Always discuss your approach first. Interviewers want to see how you think, not just whether you can produce a working answer.
  1. Saying 'we' throughout. Use 'I' when describing your specific contribution. 'We built the system' tells the interviewer nothing about what you did.
  1. Skipping clarifying questions in system design. Assuming scale, consistency requirements, or user behaviour without asking makes your design look unfocused and generic.
  1. Ignoring behavioral preparation. Behavioral rounds typically carry significant weight. Arriving with no prepared STAR stories is a common and costly mistake.
  1. Giving vague results. 'The team was happy' or 'it went well' are weak closings. Anchor your result to something concrete: a metric improved, a deadline met, a decision unblocked.
  1. Not having questions for the interviewer. Candidates who ask nothing signal low engagement. Prepare at least two questions about the team, the technical challenges they are working on, or how success is measured in the role.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does a Seek Software Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates typically report three to four rounds: an initial recruiter call, one or two technical rounds covering coding and system design, and a final round with behavioral questions. The exact structure varies by team and seniority level. Treat any specific round count you hear as approximate until your recruiter confirms the format for your role.

Does Seek use competitive programming questions or practical coding problems?

Candidates generally report a mix of both. Early technical rounds may include algorithmic problems, but Seek's interviews tend to lean toward practical scenarios that reflect real engineering work, such as designing an API or diagnosing a slow query. Preparing for both styles will serve you well.

What salary can I expect as a Software Engineer at Seek?

Based on the broader market, Software Engineer roles in India typically range from 6-12 LPA at entry level, 15-25 LPA at mid level, 28-45 LPA at senior level, and 40-65+ LPA at lead or staff level. For Seek-specific figures, check Glassdoor or levels.fyi, since actual compensation depends on the team, location, and your negotiation.

How important is system design for a mid-level Software Engineer interview at Seek?

Candidates at mid level (3-5 years) typically report that system design is included in the process, with depth calibrated to their experience. You will likely be asked to design a component or a service rather than an entire platform. Show structured thinking: clarify requirements first, walk through your design, and explicitly call out trade-offs at each step.

How should I research Seek before my interview?

Study Seek's main products, including job search, employer tools, and candidate profiles, and think about the engineering challenges behind each: search relevance, recommendation systems, and large-scale data processing. Reading any publicly available engineering content from Seek gives you concrete talking points. Reviewing current Seek job postings also helps you understand which skills and technologies each team prioritises right now.

Does Seek care which programming language I use in the coding round?

Candidates report that interviewers at Seek generally care more about problem-solving ability and code clarity than the specific language used. That said, confirm the preferred language for your team with your recruiter before the coding round, since preferences can vary. Pick the language you are most fluent in so your thinking comes through clearly.
