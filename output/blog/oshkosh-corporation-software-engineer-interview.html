01 Overview

Overview

Oshkosh Corporation is a US-based manufacturer of specialty trucks, defence vehicles, fire apparatus, and refuse collection equipment. Their software teams build embedded vehicle control systems, telematics platforms, and safety-critical firmware that must perform reliably in demanding field conditions. Candidates report a structured hiring process that typically includes a recruiter screen, one or two technical rounds, and a discussion with the hiring manager.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time you worked on safety-critical software.

*Situation:* I was on a team developing firmware for an actuator control module on a commercial vehicle. The module had to detect faults and move the actuator to a safe state automatically if anything went wrong.

*Task:* I was responsible for implementing the watchdog mechanism and the fault-handling state machine to meet the project's safety requirements.

*Action:* I mapped out all failure modes with the systems engineer, coded a hardware watchdog refresh loop with a health-check gate, and built a layered fault classification that escalated from warning to safe-state shutdown. I also wrote unit tests for each fault path and ran them in a hardware-in-the-loop setup before handing the module to the safety review team.

*Result:* The module passed the safety review in the first submission cycle with no major findings, and it was carried forward into the production build.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you collaborated closely with hardware engineers.

*Situation:* My team was integrating a new CAN-based telemetry board into an existing vehicle platform. The hardware was arriving in batches while software development was already underway.

*Task:* I had to write and validate the CAN driver layer before the final board revision was ready, using only the hardware specification and an early prototype.

*Action:* I set up a bench environment with the prototype, worked daily with the hardware engineer to understand timing tolerances and known errata, and built a transport abstraction layer so higher-level code would not change when the production board arrived. I also wrote integration tests against a CAN bus analyser.

*Result:* When the production board arrived, we needed only minor register-map adjustments. Integration completed on schedule and the prototype demo ran without issues.

---

Q: How have you handled changing requirements mid-project?

*Situation:* Midway through a telematics project, the product team decided to switch the primary host interface from UART to Ethernet because of bandwidth needs identified during customer trials.

*Task:* I had to assess the impact, communicate it to the team, and deliver the updated module without missing the sprint milestone.

*Action:* I ran a quick dependency trace, identified the files that touched the transport layer, and proposed isolating the transport behind a thin interface so higher layers stayed unchanged. I documented the design change, got team sign-off, made the code updates, and extended the unit-test suite to cover both transport paths temporarily.

*Result:* The change was absorbed within the same sprint, the milestone was met, and the cleaner interface made adding a second transport much faster later on.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions. Every 'tell me about a time' question needs a Situation (one or two sentences of context), Task (your specific responsibility), Action (what you personally did, step by step), and Result (what changed because of your work). Keep each component tight. Interviewers lose track when the situation runs too long.

Explain-Diagnose-Fix for technical debugging questions. When asked how you debug, walk through: what you observed first, how you narrowed down the cause (tools, logs, oscilloscope readings, code review), what the root cause turned out to be, and how you fixed and verified it. This shows structured thinking rather than trial and error.

Trade-off framing for design questions. Oshkosh software often runs in constrained environments. For any design question, name the constraints first (memory budget, latency ceiling, safety requirement), explain why you chose your approach over the obvious alternative, and state what you gave up. Interviewers want to see that you know there is always a trade-off.

Anchor results to outcomes. You do not need precise metrics, but saying 'the fault response time came within the required ceiling' is stronger than 'it got faster'. Use whatever concrete outcome data you genuinely have.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Domain awareness. Oshkosh ships products into defence, fire and emergency, and construction. Candidates who understand that a software bug in a vehicle control system has physical consequences stand out. You do not need prior automotive or defence experience, but you should be able to discuss what 'reliable' means in a physical system.

Embedded and systems fundamentals. Expect questions on RTOS concepts, interrupt handling, memory management in C or C++, and communication protocols such as CAN. Even roles that are not purely embedded will touch these areas because the software ultimately drives hardware.

Safety mindset. Functional safety is central to Oshkosh products. Candidates who mention fault detection, defensive coding, edge-case testing, and review processes get noticed. You do not need to be a certified safety engineer, but knowing why safe states and watchdog timers exist matters.

Cross-functional collaboration. Oshkosh teams typically include mechanical, electrical, and systems engineers alongside software. Interviewers want to hear that you can read a hardware spec, talk productively with a hardware engineer, and adapt your software when the hardware changes.

Problem-solving clarity. Whether the question is algorithmic or system design, interviewers want to follow your reasoning. Think out loud, state your assumptions, and confirm you understood the question before diving in.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Company and domain context. Read through Oshkosh Corporation's product pages to understand their vehicle lines and the markets they serve. Note the kinds of software challenges each product area creates. Review your own resume for any projects involving hardware, real-time systems, or safety, and prepare short stories around them.

Week 2: Technical fundamentals. Refresh embedded C or C++ (pointers, memory layout, the volatile keyword, bit manipulation), RTOS concepts (task scheduling, semaphores, mutexes, priority inversion), and communication protocols (CAN, SPI, I2C, UART). Practise writing and explaining small code samples aloud. If you have not worked with CAN before, read a short primer so you can discuss it at a conceptual level.

Week 3: Behavioural preparation. Write out STAR stories for: a safety or reliability challenge, a cross-functional collaboration, a difficult debugging situation, a time requirements changed, and a time you disagreed with a teammate and resolved it. Practise each story so it fits within about two minutes.

Days before the interview. Review the job description line by line and match each requirement to a story or project from your experience. Prepare a few questions to ask the interviewer, such as what a typical sprint looks like for the team or what the biggest technical challenge they are working on right now. Confirm the interview format (panel, pair coding, system design) so you are not surprised on the day.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating it like a pure product software interview. Oshkosh software runs in physical machines. Candidates who only talk about web backends or mobile apps and never connect their experience to hardware, reliability, or physical constraints tend to get screened out early.

Vague safety answers. Saying 'I always write safe code' is not enough. Interviewers want specifics: what fault conditions did you handle, what tests did you write, what review process did you follow.

Skipping the trade-off. In design and architecture questions, jumping straight to your answer without discussing the constraints and alternatives signals shallow thinking. Name the constraints, then explain your choice.

Underselling cross-team work. If you have ever coordinated with a hardware, QA, or systems team, that experience is directly relevant. Many candidates skip it and focus only on solo coding, missing a signal the interviewer is actively looking for.

Not asking questions. Oshkosh typically interviews for fit on both sides. Candidates who ask nothing signal low interest. Prepare at least two thoughtful questions about the team's work or technical challenges.
