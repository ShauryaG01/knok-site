01 Overview

Overview

Niyo is a Bangalore-based fintech company known for the Niyo Global travel card and NiyoX digital savings account. Their focus is making international banking and everyday money management simple for young Indian professionals. As a UI/UX Designer here, you would likely own flows across mobile apps, including onboarding, KYC screens, card management, and spending dashboards.

As of July 2026, knok's job radar shows Niyo has 15 open roles, and there are 29 active UI/UX Designer postings across companies nationally. Niyo's interview process typically has 2-4 rounds: candidates report an initial screening call, a portfolio review or design assignment, a cross-functional panel with product managers and engineers, and in some cases a final leadership round. Treat these as a general guide since process details change.

What sets Niyo interviews apart is the emphasis on designing for trust and simplicity in a regulated, mobile-first context. Interviewers want to see how you handle real constraints, not just clean Figma files.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly, based on what candidates report and what Niyo's product context demands.

  1. Walk us through a product you designed end-to-end. How did you move from research to final handoff?
  2. Niyo's users include first-time international travelers and young professionals. How would you design an onboarding flow for someone using a travel card for the very first time?
  3. Describe a time you had to design under a regulatory or compliance constraint. How did you keep the experience user-friendly?
  4. How do you design for trust in a financial app? Which visual or interaction patterns do you rely on?
  5. Tell us about a time engineering pushed back on your design during implementation. How did you handle it?
  6. How would you approach redesigning a KYC flow to reduce drop-off without removing any required steps?
  7. Niyo serves both tech-savvy users and people who are new to digital banking. How do you design for that range within a single product?
  8. Describe your usability testing process. What tools do you use and how do you recruit participants?
  9. How do you explain a design decision to a stakeholder who does not have a design background?
  10. Tell us about a design that did not perform as expected after launch. What did you learn and what did you change?
  11. How do you stay current on fintech UX patterns and what competitors like Wise, Jupiter, or Fi are doing?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time you had to design under a regulatory or compliance constraint. How did you keep the experience user-friendly?

*Situation:* I was the lead designer on a digital wallet onboarding flow that required users to complete a video KYC step as mandated by the RBI.

*Task:* My goal was to make the video KYC step feel reassuring and simple rather than bureaucratic, since user research showed that first-time users often dropped off at this point.

*Action:* I mapped every point of uncertainty using session recordings and interviews with a small group of test participants. I replaced legal-heavy copy with plain-language explanations, added a 'what happens next' progress indicator, and designed an illustrated pre-KYC checklist so users knew exactly what to keep ready. I worked closely with the legal team to confirm which copy was safe to simplify and which had to stay verbatim.

*Result:* The revised flow saw a clear reduction in mid-flow exits during internal testing. The product team adopted the plain-language approach across two other compliance screens after seeing the outcome.

---

Q: Tell us about a time engineering pushed back on your design during implementation. How did you handle it?

*Situation:* I had designed a smooth card-number reveal animation for a virtual card screen, with a copy-to-clipboard interaction that was part of an animated sequence.

*Task:* The developer flagged that the animation approach would require a custom native module and would add significant time to the sprint. I needed to find a path that preserved the feel without the overhead.

*Action:* I scheduled a quick call with the developer to understand the specific constraint, then went back to Figma and prototyped 3 alternative interactions. I presented them with side-by-side notes explaining the tradeoff on user delight versus development effort for each option. We agreed on a lighter micro-animation that the developer could build with a standard library component.

*Result:* The feature shipped on time. The simpler animation still tested well with users, and the collaboration built enough trust that the developer started flagging constraints earlier in future sprints.

---

Q: Tell us about a design that did not perform as expected after launch. What did you learn?

*Situation:* I redesigned the home screen of a financial app, moving the primary CTA higher and simplifying the card layout based on a heatmap analysis.

*Task:* The goal was to increase taps on the 'Add Money' button, which was a key activation metric for new users.

*Action:* I shipped the redesign after an internal review. Post-launch analytics showed that while 'Add Money' taps improved slightly, a secondary action that users relied on daily had dropped in visibility and was now getting far fewer taps.

*Result:* I had optimised for one metric without considering the full screen ecosystem. I ran a follow-up round of user interviews, rebuilt the hierarchy to surface both actions clearly, and now I always map the full set of user jobs on a screen before moving any single element.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For design case study questions, use the Problem-Process-Outcome structure. Start with the user problem and business context in 1-2 sentences, walk through your process covering research, ideation, testing, and iteration, then end with what shipped and what you measured or learned. Interviewers at product companies like Niyo want to hear your thinking, not just see polished screens.

For behavioral questions, use STAR: Situation (set context briefly), Task (your specific responsibility), Action (what you did and why, with enough detail to be credible), Result (what actually happened, even if imperfect). Be honest about failures. A reflection-heavy answer about a miss often lands better than a win with no nuance.

For 'how would you design X' questions, resist jumping to solutions. Open by saying you would start by understanding who the user is and what they are trying to accomplish. Then walk through your research approach, key insights you would look for, design principles you would apply, how you would test, and how you would measure success. Showing structured thinking matters more than the specific solution you land on.

For portfolio walkthroughs, lead with context covering the product, the user problem, and the constraints you were working within. Then show process artifacts like research notes, sketches, wireframes, and prototypes, not just the final UI. Fintech interviewers pay close attention to how you handled edge cases and error states.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on Niyo's product context and what candidates report, interviewers typically look for a few specific things.

Mobile-first depth. Niyo's products live on mobile. They want designers who think in gestures, thumb zones, and loading states, not desktop layouts adapted for a small screen.

Fintech domain fluency. You do not need prior banking experience, but you should understand concepts like KYC, UPI flows, card management, and why trust signals matter in financial UI. Showing you have studied Niyo's own app and know where it does well and where it could improve is a strong signal.

Research-backed decisions. Niyo's users range from frequent international travelers to people who have never held a forex card. Interviewers want to see that you ground design choices in actual user insight, not personal preference or aesthetic taste.

Cross-functional maturity. Fintech products involve legal, compliance, product, and engineering teams in every decision. Candidates who show they have navigated these constraints and built solid working relationships with non-designers stand out clearly.

Honest self-assessment. Interviewers frequently ask about failures or designs that did not work out. The ability to reflect clearly and show what you changed is valued as much as a list of wins.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know the product.
Download and use the Niyo Global and NiyoX apps. Take notes on onboarding, card management, notifications, and error states. Write down 3 things you think work well and 3 things you would improve. Be ready to discuss these in your interview with specific observations, not general impressions.

Week 2: Prepare your portfolio.
Select 2-3 case studies that show your process, ideally including at least one from a financial or regulated product. For each, prepare a 5-7 minute walkthrough that covers the user problem, your research approach, key design decisions, and the outcome. Make sure your process artifacts are visible, not just the final screens.

Week 3: Practice design exercises.
Practice live design prompts such as 'redesign the KYC flow' or 'design a spending summary screen.' Practice talking through your thinking out loud before opening any design tool. Interviewers are evaluating your process as much as the output you produce.

Throughout: Build your STAR stories.
Prepare 4-5 behavioral stories covering: a constraint you navigated, a stakeholder conflict you resolved, a research insight that changed your direction, a design failure, and a successful collaboration with engineering. Practice these out loud so they feel natural rather than recited.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Showing only final screens. A common mistake is presenting a polished portfolio without any process artifacts. Niyo interviewers are product-minded and want to see your thinking, your early sketches, and your test findings, not just the visually finished output.

Not knowing the Niyo product. Candidates who have not used the app before the interview signal low interest. Take time to download it, use it, and form a genuine opinion on the UX before you walk in.

Ignoring compliance and edge cases. Fintech design is full of mandatory steps and error states. If your case study covers only the happy path, interviewers will probe on error handling and compliance flows. Have answers ready for those scenarios.

Vague impact claims. If you say a redesign 'increased conversions significantly,' be ready to explain exactly how you measured it. Unsupported numbers hurt credibility. It is better to say 'we saw a clear improvement in internal testing and the team decided to ship' than to cite a figure you cannot defend.

Rushing to the solution. In 'how would you design X' questions, jumping straight to a UI solution without discussing the user, the context, and the constraints signals shallow thinking. Slow down, ask clarifying questions, and show your framework before touching any design tool.

Frequently asked

How many rounds does Niyo typically have for a UI/UX Designer role?

Candidates report 2-4 rounds typically. This usually includes a recruiter or HR screening call, a portfolio review or design assignment, a panel discussion with the product and design team, and sometimes a final round with a senior leader. Round count and structure can vary based on seniority and team needs, so confirm the specifics with your recruiter after you apply.

Is there a design assignment or take-home task?

Many candidates report receiving a design assignment, either as a take-home task or a live exercise during the interview. Prompts are typically product-focused, such as redesigning an existing Niyo flow or designing a new feature for a specific user segment. You will generally be expected to walk through your thinking and process, not just submit screens.

What tools should I know before interviewing at Niyo?

Figma is the standard expectation for most product design roles in India's fintech space. Familiarity with prototyping in Figma, working knowledge of Principle or ProtoPie for advanced interactions, and experience with a research or usability testing tool are commonly valued. Confirm with your recruiter if any specific tool is required for the assignment round.

Does prior fintech experience matter for this role?

Prior fintech experience is helpful but not always required, particularly at mid-level. What matters more is that you understand the constraints of designing in a regulated, high-stakes environment and that you have studied Niyo's own product before the interview. Candidates from e-commerce or health-tech backgrounds with strong research and systems thinking skills can also do well.
