01 Overview

Overview

CGI is a global IT and business consulting firm with a strong delivery presence in India, running operations across cities like Bangalore, Hyderabad, and Chennai. As of July 2026, knok's job radar shows 21 open Business Analyst positions at CGI, part of a wider market of 398 BA roles across India. That level of active hiring makes CGI one of the more consistent recruiters for this profile right now.

Candidates report that CGI's interview process typically runs two to three rounds: an initial screening (phone or video), one or two technical and domain-focused interviews, and a final HR discussion. The exact format varies by business unit and project type. Questions lean heavily on real project experience rather than textbook theory. Interviewers want to see how you have gathered requirements from difficult stakeholders, written clear documentation, managed scope changes, and worked alongside development teams to deliver.

CGI positions itself as a client-proximity firm, meaning BAs often interact directly with client teams rather than working through account managers. This value shows up in interview questions about stakeholder management, conflict resolution, and professional communication. If you are a fresher, prepare examples from internships or academic projects. If you are experienced, have three to four strong project stories ready, each covering a different core BA competency.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions reflect commonly reported patterns from CGI BA interview experiences. Practise all of them, but invest extra time in the ones involving stakeholder management and scope, as those come up most frequently.

  1. Walk me through a project where you gathered requirements from multiple stakeholders who had conflicting priorities. How did you manage it?
  2. How do you structure a Business Requirements Document (BRD) or Functional Requirements Specification (FRS)? Take us through your process step by step.
  3. CGI serves clients across banking, government, telecom, and healthcare. How do you get up to speed quickly in a new domain?
  4. Describe a time you identified a gap in a client's existing process and proposed an improvement. What was the outcome?
  5. How do you handle scope creep during an active project? Give a specific example from your experience.
  6. Which tools have you used for requirement tracking and documentation (JIRA, Confluence, Azure DevOps, or others)? How did you use them on a real project?
  7. Tell us about a time you had to push back on a stakeholder request. How did you handle the friction without damaging the relationship?
  8. How do you verify that what the development team has built actually matches the requirements you documented?
  9. CGI values direct client relationships. Describe a situation where you managed a difficult client or key end-user.
  10. How do you prioritise requirements when there is not enough time or budget to deliver everything on the list?
  11. Describe your experience in an Agile team. What was your specific role in sprint ceremonies such as backlog refinement and sprint planning?
  12. What does good documentation mean to you, and how do you ensure your artefacts remain useful after you hand them off to another team?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use these as a starting point and replace the project details with your own experience.

Q: Walk me through a project where you gathered requirements from stakeholders with conflicting priorities.

*Situation:* I was the BA on a CRM implementation project for a financial services client. The sales team wanted a lead conversion dashboard, while the operations team needed detailed case management workflows. Both groups reported to different VPs and neither was initially willing to compromise.

*Task:* My job was to produce a single, agreed set of requirements that the development team could build against without the project stalling in stakeholder debate.

*Action:* I ran separate discovery sessions with each group using a structured interview template so both sets of inputs were captured in the same format. I then mapped their needs against the business objectives in the project charter and applied MoSCoW prioritisation to separate must-haves from nice-to-haves. I facilitated a joint workshop where I presented a merged requirements list with trade-offs clearly labelled, and I set up a shared tracker so both teams could see the status of every item.

*Result:* Both stakeholder groups signed off on the requirements document, and the project delivered on schedule. Post-launch feedback from both teams was positive. This is typically the kind of outcome CGI clients expect: requirements clarity before a single line of code is written.

---

Q: How do you handle scope creep during a live project?

*Situation:* During a government portal project, the client's project owner began adding new feature requests in weekly calls, well after the requirements had been baselined. The development team was already mid-sprint.

*Task:* I needed to protect the agreed scope while keeping the client relationship intact and not disrupting the dev team's momentum.

*Action:* I pulled up the signed-off requirements document in the very next call and walked the client through exactly how the new request differed from what was baselined. I logged it formally in the change request register and produced a quick impact assessment covering estimated additional effort, revised timeline, and cost. I framed it not as a refusal but as: 'This sounds valuable. Let us evaluate it properly.' I then presented the options to the client: defer to phase two, reduce scope elsewhere to accommodate, or approve a formal change order.

*Result:* The client approved two of the four new requests as formal additions and deferred the rest. The core delivery stayed on track, and the client later commended the team for transparent scope management.

---

Q: Describe a time you managed a difficult client relationship.

*Situation:* On a retail analytics engagement, the client's IT head was consistently unresponsive to review requests and often challenged my process maps openly in group calls.

*Task:* I needed his sign-off to move the project forward, and the friction was starting to affect team morale.

*Action:* I requested a one-on-one meeting rather than a group call to understand his concerns directly. It turned out that the existing process, which my analysis had flagged as inefficient, had been designed by his own team, and he felt the critique was personal. I reframed my process maps to position the current state as a solid foundation and improvements as additions rather than replacements. I also started looping him into early draft reviews instead of presenting polished documents that left little room for his input.

*Result:* Within a month, his engagement in reviews improved noticeably and he became one of the project's internal advocates. Candidates report that this kind of stakeholder empathy is something CGI interviewers probe for explicitly.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Having a clear framework ready keeps your answers structured and signals analytical thinking to the interviewer.

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the baseline for every behavioural question. Keep Situation and Task brief. Spend the majority of your answer on Action and always close with a concrete Result. Never skip the Result as it is often the part interviewers remember most.

MoSCoW is the standard framework for prioritisation questions. It stands for Must have, Should have, Could have, and Won't have this time around. If asked how you prioritise requirements, naming MoSCoW and explaining how you apply it in practice signals real BA experience.

As-Is and To-Be Process Mapping works well for process improvement questions. Describe the current state, identify the pain points, then describe the future state your recommendation creates. This structure keeps your answer logical and easy to follow.

SWOT is useful when the question is about evaluating options or assessing a proposed change rather than recounting a past experience.

Five Whys is a root-cause analysis technique worth mentioning when an interviewer asks how you diagnose a business problem. It shows analytical depth beyond surface-level observation.

A quick reference for choosing the right framework by question type:

Question typeRecommended framework
Behavioural ('tell me about a time')STAR
PrioritisationMoSCoW
Process improvementAs-Is / To-Be
Problem diagnosisFive Whys
Evaluating options or proposalsSWOT
05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

CGI BA interviewers are typically looking for a few things beyond technical knowledge of BA artefacts.

Clear communication is the top signal. BAs sit between business and technology, so interviewers specifically watch for whether you can explain a complex problem in plain language without leaning on jargon. Avoid industry buzzwords unless the interviewer uses them first.

Real, personal ownership of your work matters significantly. Interviewers probe whether you were the person doing the work or just observing. Be specific about what you personally contributed. Use 'I' rather than 'we' when describing your role in a project outcome.

Stakeholder and client empathy is a CGI cultural value. Candidates report being asked about difficult stakeholders or client pushback in almost every interview. Have at least two stories ready where you navigated a relationship challenge professionally and the outcome was positive.

Documentation discipline comes up repeatedly. CGI engagements often involve formal handoffs between distributed teams, sometimes across geographies. Showing that you treat documentation as a deliverable (version control, review cycles, plain language) stands out from candidates who treat it as an afterthought.

Adaptability across domains is valued because CGI serves clients in multiple industries. If you have worked in more than one sector, highlight it. If not, describe concretely how you approach learning a new domain quickly.

Agile fluency is increasingly expected even in organisations with a mix of waterfall and Agile delivery. Know user stories, acceptance criteria, and sprint ceremonies well enough to discuss your actual contribution to them.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A structured preparation period spanning a couple of weeks covers what most candidates need for a CGI BA interview.

Phase 1: Audit your project experience. List every project you have worked on and identify the top three to four that best show requirements gathering, stakeholder management, scope handling, and delivery. For each, fill out a STAR template so you are not constructing the story in real time during the interview.

Phase 2: Refresh your BA artefacts. Review what goes into a BRD, FRS, use case document, and user story with acceptance criteria. If you have real samples from past work, re-read them. If not, study standard templates and understand the purpose of each section.

Phase 3: Research CGI specifically. Read about CGI's service lines, particularly IT consulting, systems integration, and managed services. Note any publicly reported recent projects or industry wins in India. This helps you ask informed questions and signals genuine interest in the company.

Phase 4: Practise answers out loud. Reading a STAR story silently and saying it aloud are very different things. Record yourself answering the twelve questions listed in this guide and listen back. Watch for vague language, filler words, and stories that run too long.

Phase 5: Prepare your own questions. Interviewers typically report that candidates who ask thoughtful questions leave a stronger impression. Good topics include the type of projects you would join in the first few months, how BA work is split between client-facing and documentation tasks, and how the team structures collaboration between BAs and developers.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

These are patterns that candidates report costing them offers at BA interviews generally, and at CGI specifically.

1. Vague answers with no specifics. Saying 'I worked with stakeholders to gather requirements' tells an interviewer very little. Name the stakeholders by role, describe what made the situation complex, and explain exactly what you did. Specificity is what separates strong candidates.

2. Confusing BA and PM responsibilities. Business Analysts and Project Managers have overlapping but distinct roles. If you managed timelines and resources, own it honestly. But do not claim BA credit for PM work, or the reverse. CGI interviewers typically probe this distinction directly.

3. Not knowing your own resume in detail. If your resume mentions an ERP implementation, be ready to discuss the modules, the client industry, and your specific contribution. Getting vague about your own projects is a significant red flag.

4. Skipping the Result in STAR answers. Many candidates describe what they did but never close with the outcome. Always end with what happened: did the project deliver on time? Did the stakeholder relationship improve? Did the process change get adopted by the business?

5. Over-crediting the team. Phrases like 'we delivered' and 'our team managed' leave the interviewer unsure of your personal contribution. Be precise about what you individually owned, even within a team effort.

6. Not preparing questions to ask. CGI values client engagement and intellectual curiosity. Leaving the end-of-interview question moment blank sends a signal of low interest. Prepare at least three genuine questions about the role or team.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does a CGI Business Analyst interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically involves two to three rounds. This usually starts with an initial screening call from HR or a recruiter, followed by one or two technical and domain-focused discussions with a hiring manager or senior BA, and then a final HR round. The exact format varies by business unit and location, so treat this as a general guide rather than a guarantee.

Does CGI use case study interviews for the BA role?

Candidates for CGI BA roles more commonly report scenario-based questions (such as 'how would you handle scope creep') rather than formal structured case interviews of the kind used by pure management consulting firms. Some panels in CGI's consulting-aligned teams do use brief business scenarios, so it is worth preparing for both. Your priority should be strong STAR-format answers grounded in real project experience.

What salary can a Business Analyst expect at CGI in India?

Specific salary data for CGI is not available in our current dataset. For context, Glassdoor and levels.fyi publicly report a range for BA roles in India that varies considerably by years of experience, city, and domain specialisation. We recommend checking Glassdoor and LinkedIn Salary Insights for current figures before entering any negotiation discussion, and cross-referencing with industry surveys relevant to your sector.

Is Agile experience required for CGI BA interviews?

Agile familiarity is increasingly expected across most IT services firms, including CGI. Candidates report being asked about sprint ceremonies, user story writing, and acceptance criteria in a significant portion of BA interviews. A formal certification is not required, but you should be comfortable explaining your actual contributions to Agile ceremonies and how you write testable acceptance criteria.

How should freshers prepare for a CGI BA interview?

Freshers should draw on internship work, academic capstone projects, or any freelance or volunteer experience where they gathered requirements, mapped a process, or worked with a team to deliver something. The core skill CGI tests is structured thinking and clear communication, not years of experience. Be specific about your personal role even in small projects, and show that you understand BA artefacts like BRDs and user stories at least in concept.
