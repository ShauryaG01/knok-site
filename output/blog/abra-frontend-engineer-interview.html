02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Based on candidate reports and the Frontend Engineer role profile, these questions are most likely to come up in an abra interview:

  1. Walk me through a complex React component you built and the decisions you made around state management.
  2. How do you decide between local state, Context API, and an external state management library like Redux or Zustand?
  3. Explain how the browser renders a webpage from the moment it receives an HTML file. Where can a frontend engineer optimise this process?
  4. How have you diagnosed and fixed performance issues in a React app? What tools did you use?
  5. Describe a time you built a component that needed to be reusable across multiple teams or products.
  6. How do you approach accessibility in your UI work? Can you give a concrete example from a past project?
  7. Tell me about a time a feature you shipped caused a regression. How did you find it, fix it, and prevent a repeat?
  8. How do you structure CSS in a large codebase? Have you worked with BEM, CSS Modules, Tailwind, or a design system?
  9. How would you design the frontend architecture for a dashboard that shows real-time data from multiple APIs?
  10. What is your approach to code review? How do you give feedback without creating friction?
  11. How do you decide what to unit-test versus integration-test versus end-to-end test on the frontend?
  12. Tell me about a time you disagreed with a product or design decision. How did you handle it, and what was the outcome?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a time you improved the performance of a frontend application.

*Situation:* Our product's main listing page was loading slowly, and users in lower-bandwidth regions were dropping off before data appeared.

*Task:* I was asked to investigate and reduce load time without changing the product's core functionality.

*Action:* I used Chrome DevTools and Lighthouse to profile the page and found three main issues: a large unoptimised image bundle, a blocking third-party script, and unnecessary re-renders in a deeply nested component tree. I converted images to WebP format, moved the third-party script to load asynchronously, and introduced React.memo and useMemo at the right component boundaries to cut redundant re-renders.

*Result:* Our internally tracked Lighthouse scores improved meaningfully, and analytics showed a clear reduction in bounce rates on that page within the following sprint.

---

Q: Describe a reusable component you built that was adopted across multiple teams.

*Situation:* Multiple squads were each building their own modal and dialog components, causing visual inconsistency across the product and duplicated effort.

*Task:* I took ownership of designing and building a single shared modal that could cover all the different use cases without being rigid.

*Action:* I spoke with frontend engineers from each squad to understand the variants they needed. I built the component using a compound component pattern so consumers could compose the header, body, and footer independently. I wrote clear documentation with usage examples and set up Storybook stories for each variant.

*Result:* All three squads adopted the component within the next sprint. We removed several parallel implementations, and the design team had one source of truth to update going forward.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you pushed back on a product requirement.

*Situation:* We were asked to ship a complex filtering UI in a single sprint. In my assessment, the scope was too large to complete safely in that time.

*Task:* I needed to raise this concern in a way that did not block the project or come across as uncooperative.

*Action:* I prepared a short breakdown of what each part of the feature required technically, identified the specific delivery risks, and proposed a phased approach: ship the core filter first, then add advanced options in the following sprint. I shared this with the PM before the planning meeting so we could align before the wider discussion.

*Result:* The PM agreed to the phased plan. We shipped the core feature on schedule with no regressions, and completed the advanced filters in the next sprint as planned.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions. Structure every 'tell me about a time' answer as Situation (brief context), Task (what you were responsible for), Action (what you specifically did), and Result (what happened). Keep the Situation short so you spend most of your time on Action and Result, which is where interviewers learn the most about you.

Think-aloud for live coding. Interviewers want to see your reasoning, not just the final code. Before writing anything, pause briefly to confirm the inputs, outputs, and edge cases you plan to handle. Narrate your approach as you go. If you spot a bug mid-way, call it out yourself before the interviewer does. This shows self-awareness, which most interviewers value highly.

Tradeoff framing for system design. Frontend system design questions rarely have a single correct answer. Use this pattern: 'Here is my approach, here is why I chose it, and here is what I would do differently if the constraints changed.' This shows engineering maturity and keeps you from defending one choice too rigidly.

The so-what rule for technical depth. When you explain a concept, follow it with why it matters in practice. Do not just explain what the virtual DOM does. Follow it with how that affects decisions you make when optimising renders. Interviewers remember candidates who connect theory to real product impact.

Prepare a signal sentence for each project. For every major project on your resume, have one sentence ready that summarises the impact: what changed, for whom, and by how much (using publicly reported figures or Glassdoor data where available). This helps you answer 'tell me about your work' questions without rambling.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Candidates who have gone through frontend interviews at companies similar to abra report that interviewers look for a specific combination of qualities beyond raw technical skill.

Real ownership, not just task completion. Interviewers want to hear you say 'I decided' and 'I noticed' rather than 'we were told to.' They are assessing whether you take responsibility for outcomes and spot problems proactively.

Communication as a core skill. Frontend engineers at product companies work closely with designers, PMs, and backend teams every day. Interviewers watch whether you explain technical tradeoffs clearly and ask good clarifying questions rather than making assumptions.

Depth on your primary stack. If your resume says React, you should be able to explain reconciliation, the rules of hooks, and why list keys matter in renders. Surface-level answers on your own primary technology are a commonly cited reason candidates do not advance to the next round.

A genuine approach to quality. Testing, code review, accessibility, and documentation are not afterthoughts for strong candidates. Being able to speak to how you ensure quality, not just how you ship features, signals seniority regardless of years of experience.

Honesty and self-awareness. Candidates report that interviewers respond well to answers that acknowledge what went wrong and what you would do differently next time. Polished answers that never admit a mistake can feel rehearsed and unconvincing.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Weeks before the interview:

  1. Review JavaScript fundamentals: closures, the event loop, promises, and async/await. These come up in technical screens at all experience levels.
  2. Go deep on your primary framework. For React: hooks, context, memoisation, and rendering behaviour. Practice explaining these out loud in plain terms, not just using them in code.
  3. Pick two or three past projects that showcase different strengths such as performance work, cross-team collaboration, or product thinking. Prepare a STAR summary for each.
  4. Look for any publicly available engineering content from abra: blog posts, conference talks, or open-source repositories. This shapes your questions and signals genuine interest to interviewers.

In the days before:

  1. Practice live coding on a whiteboard or shared editor. Focus on DOM manipulation, array and object transformations, and small React component challenges.
  2. Prepare questions for the interviewer. Good ones include: 'What does the frontend code review process look like here?' and 'What is the biggest technical challenge the frontend team is tackling right now?'
  3. Review every project and tool listed on your resume. Interviewers often pick something you listed and go deep, so be ready for any of it.

On the day:

  1. For live coding, narrate your thinking before and while you write. A slightly imperfect answer where the interviewer could follow your reasoning is more impressive than a correct answer reached in complete silence.
07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Staying silent during live coding. Interviewers at product companies want to understand how you think, not just whether you reached the right answer. Candidates who code quietly and only speak at the end miss the chance to show their reasoning.

Overcomplicating the system design answer. A well-reasoned simple architecture beats a complicated one you cannot fully explain. Start with the simplest solution that meets the stated requirements, then layer in complexity with a clear reason for each addition.

Treating React as the whole of frontend. Strong candidates can speak to the browser, the network, accessibility, and performance, not just the framework. If all your answers reference only React concepts, it can signal a narrow view of the discipline.

Vague results in STAR answers. Saying 'it went well' or 'the team was happy' is a missed opportunity. Where you have real impact data from internal dashboards or publicly reported metrics, use it. Where you do not, describe the qualitative outcome clearly: what shipped, what changed, what risk was avoided.

Not asking questions at the end. Candidates who ask nothing are often perceived as less engaged or less senior. Prepare at least two genuine questions about the team's technical direction or engineering culture.

Padding your resume with tools you barely know. If you list a technology, be ready to go deep on it. Listing every framework you have touched once is riskier than listing fewer technologies with genuine command.

How many rounds does the abra Frontend Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically involves a recruiter or HR screen, a technical phone or video round, a coding task (take-home or live), a frontend system design discussion, and a final round with a hiring manager or team lead. The exact number of rounds can vary by team and the seniority of the role. Confirm the structure with your recruiter after your first call so you can prepare the right material for each stage.

What is the salary range for Frontend Engineers at abra?

Abra has not published official bands publicly. Across the broader Indian market, knok jobradar data shows Frontend Engineer salaries from 5-11 LPA at entry level (0-2 years), 12-22 LPA at mid level (3-5 years), 24-40 LPA at senior level (6-9 years), and 38-58+ LPA at Lead/Staff level. For abra-specific figures, check Glassdoor or levels.fyi for candidate-reported compensation data, and come to the offer stage prepared to negotiate based on your current CTC and any competing offers.

Does abra ask DSA (data structures and algorithms) questions in the Frontend Engineer interview?

Candidates report that abra's frontend interviews are more focused on practical JavaScript, React, and frontend system design than on classic algorithmic problems. That said, some rounds may include array or object manipulation tasks that overlap with DSA basics. It is worth being comfortable with common array methods, simple recursion, and basic traversal problems even if the primary focus is applied frontend work.

How should I prepare for a frontend system design round?

Think about the design in layers: component architecture, data flow, state management strategy, API design from the client perspective, performance considerations, and error handling. Practice by picking a real product feature such as a search bar, a news feed, or a checkout flow, and designing it out loud from scratch. Interviewers care more about how you reason through tradeoffs than whether you land on the single correct answer.

Is a take-home assignment common in abra's process?

Several candidates report receiving a take-home task, typically a small React or JavaScript project with a defined scope. Treat the submission as a reflection of your real working standards: clean code, a sensible file structure, a brief README explaining your decisions, and ideally some tests. Avoid over-engineering it. Reviewers typically look for code they would be comfortable maintaining in production, not a showcase of every pattern you know.
