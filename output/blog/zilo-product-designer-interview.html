02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly in Product Designer interviews at companies like zilo, based on candidate reports and standard product design interview patterns.

  1. Walk us through a project you owned end to end. What was the problem, who were the users, and how did you know the design worked?
  2. How do you start a design brief when the information given to you is vague or incomplete?
  3. Tell me about a time you disagreed with a PM or engineer on a design direction. What happened and how did it resolve?
  4. How do you balance what users want with what the business needs?
  5. Describe how you choose between qualitative research (interviews, usability tests) and quantitative methods (analytics, A/B tests).
  6. Show me a project where the first version did not land well. What did you change, and why?
  7. How do you know when a design is ready to ship?
  8. How would you redesign a part of our product that you think is not working well for users?
  9. How do you manage your work when you are juggling multiple projects at the same time?
  10. Walk me through your design tool stack and how you decide what tool fits the job.
  11. How do you present and defend design decisions to stakeholders who have strong opinions but limited design background?
  12. How do you measure whether a design you shipped actually solved the problem it was meant to solve?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format for every behavioral question: ground your answer in a real situation, state your specific task, describe concrete actions, and close with a measurable or observable result. Three worked examples follow.

Q: Walk us through a project you owned end to end.

*Situation:* Our app's onboarding completion rate was low, and new users were dropping off before reaching the core feature. The product team had a hypothesis but no user data to support it.

*Task:* I was given ownership of redesigning the onboarding flow, from research through final handoff, within a short design sprint.

*Action:* I ran several moderated usability sessions with new sign-ups and identified key points where users lost their way. I mapped these to the existing flow, ran a card sort to validate information hierarchy, and prototyped two alternative flows in Figma. I shared both with the PM and engineering lead early to surface technical constraints before investing in high-fidelity screens.

*Result:* The new flow shipped after two rounds of iteration. Analytics confirmed that completion through the first core action improved. The research artifacts were reused in two subsequent feature planning sessions.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you disagreed with a PM on a design direction.

*Situation:* The PM wanted a prominent promotional banner on the home screen to push a new paid feature. I was concerned it would hurt the daily experience for free-tier users who visited that screen multiple times a day.

*Task:* I needed to make the case for a less intrusive approach while keeping the business goal of driving paid conversions on the table.

*Action:* Rather than pushing back with just an opinion, I pulled session recordings to show how free-tier users moved through the home screen and confirmed the proposed placement was used heavily for core daily tasks. I suggested a contextual prompt that appeared only after a user completed a key action, which felt more relevant and less interruptive. I built quick prototypes of both approaches and we ran a short test with a handful of internal users.

*Result:* The PM agreed to test the contextual version first. Feedback from the internal test strongly supported it, and it went into the next sprint. The same interaction pattern was later reused for two other feature prompts.

---

Q: Describe a project where the first version did not work. What did you do?

*Situation:* I redesigned a filter system for a search results page. After launch, support tickets went up and session data showed users were applying fewer filters than before the redesign.

*Task:* I needed to understand what went wrong quickly and propose a fix without waiting for the next planning cycle.

*Action:* I pulled session recordings and observed users opening the filter panel, scrolling through it, and closing it without applying anything. The panel showed too many options at once with no clear hierarchy. I ran a few quick calls with users who had raised support issues and pinpointed the core confusion. Within a week I had a revised design that grouped filters into primary and secondary tiers, with the most-used options always visible.

*Result:* The revised version shipped in a follow-up sprint. Filter usage recovered and exceeded the pre-launch baseline, which the data team confirmed in a follow-up review.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For portfolio walkthroughs: Structure each case study as problem, research, decisions made and alternatives considered, final solution, and outcome. Spend at least a third of your time on the problem and research phase. Interviewers lose interest when designers jump straight to finished screens without explaining what they were trying to solve.

For live design challenges: Start by asking clarifying questions even if you feel pressure to start sketching immediately. Define the user, the core task they are trying to accomplish, and the constraints you are working within. Narrate your thinking as you go. Interviewers at product companies are evaluating your process, not whether the final sketch looks polished.

For 'tell me about a time' questions: Use STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) but keep Situation brief: one or two sentences. Spend the most time on Action, since that is where your skill is visible. Close with a concrete Result: what changed, what you learned, or how the team used your work afterward.

For 'how would you redesign our product' questions: Do not lead with criticism of what they have built. Frame it as: 'I noticed X user behaviour might be happening here, and here is how I would explore whether a change could help.' This shows product empathy and collaborative intent rather than judgement.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Product thinking, not just craft. Zilo, like most product-led companies, wants designers who understand the business goal and the user's job to be done, not only designers who make things look good. If your portfolio answers 'what' but not 'why,' you will struggle in the interview.

Research groundedness. Be ready to explain specifically how you gathered user insight. 'I talked to users' is not enough. What method did you use, what surprised you, and how did it change your design decisions?

Collaboration and influence. Candidates report that zilo asks a lot about how you work with engineers and PMs. They want evidence that you can disagree constructively, build shared understanding, and get your designs built as intended.

Ownership and measurable impact. Connect your design decisions to outcomes wherever possible. Even if you cannot share exact figures due to confidentiality, describe what metrics the team tracked and which direction they moved.

Self-awareness about failure. Every strong candidate has at least one project that did not go as planned. Interviewers trust candidates more when they can clearly explain what went wrong and what they changed as a result.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

One to two weeks before your first round:

Review your portfolio and pick three or four projects that each show a different skill: one heavy on research, one showing cross-functional collaboration, one with a measurable outcome, and one where something went wrong and you recovered. For each, prepare a clear walkthrough that leads with the user problem before showing any screens.

Spend real time using zilo's product as a user. Note one or two areas where you see a potential experience gap and think through how you would approach investigating it. Interviewers often ask 'what would you change about our product?' and a thoughtful, research-framed answer stands out clearly from generic feedback.

In the week before:

Practice your STAR answers out loud, not just in your head. Record yourself and watch it back. Most designers over-explain Situation and rush through Action, which is the opposite of what interviewers want.

If you receive a take-home brief, read it carefully for stated constraints. Submit work that is clearly annotated, since the reviewer may evaluate it without you present to explain your thinking. Include a short written rationale for your key decisions alongside the screens.

On the day:

For a live whiteboard or Figma exercise, ask clarifying questions first and write your assumptions down visibly. It is completely fine to say 'I would normally validate this assumption with user research before committing to this direction.'

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Showing finished screens without context. Interviewers who care about product thinking disengage when a designer opens Figma and starts showing polished UI without explaining the problem first. Always lead with the user problem and the constraints you were working within.

Claiming sole credit for team work. It is fine to say 'I led this' or 'I owned the research phase.' But if you describe the entire product as your solo work when you were one of several designers, experienced interviewers will catch it in follow-up questions. Be specific about your contribution.

Skipping the failure story. Many candidates avoid projects that did not go well. This is a mistake. Interviewers interpret a portfolio with no failures as either a limited body of work or a lack of honesty. Prepare one genuine story where you show what you learned and what you changed.

Reacting defensively to design critique. Zilo's interviewers may push back on your design choices during a portfolio review. This is intentional. Stay curious rather than defensive, and ask what concern sits behind the pushback before you respond.

Not having questions ready. Candidates who say 'I think you have covered everything' at the end of an interview signal low curiosity. Prepare two or three genuine questions about the team's design process, how they measure design impact, or what the biggest unsolved design challenge is right now.
