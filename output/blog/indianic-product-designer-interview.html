02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions are drawn from candidate reports and common patterns in design interviews at product-focused companies like indianic. The specific questions you face will vary by role level and interviewer, so treat this as a preparation guide, not a guaranteed list.

  1. Walk us through one product you designed end-to-end. What problem were you solving, and how did you approach it?
  2. How do you balance user needs with business goals when they pull in opposite directions?
  3. Describe a time when user research proved your early assumptions completely wrong. What did you do next?
  4. How do you approach designing for users across different regions of India, including people in smaller cities with slower connections or older devices?
  5. Tell us about a time you had to work within tight technical constraints. How did you collaborate with developers to reach a workable solution?
  6. How do you present and defend a design decision to a stakeholder who strongly disagrees with your direction?
  7. Describe your research process when time or budget is limited. What do you prioritise?
  8. How do you handle feedback that asks you to make a significant change mid-project? Walk us through a real example.
  9. Which design tools do you use regularly, and how do you decide which tool fits a given task?
  10. Tell us about a project where you simplified a complex workflow for users who were not tech-savvy.
  11. How do you measure whether your design actually worked after it shipped? What signals or metrics do you look for?
  12. Where do you see product design heading over the next couple of years, and how are you preparing for it?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time when user research proved your early assumptions wrong.

*Situation:* I was designing an onboarding flow for a B2B SaaS tool. My team assumed users would want a guided step-by-step tour, because similar products in our category used that pattern.

*Task:* My responsibility was to design an onboarding experience that got new users to their first 'aha moment' as quickly as possible.

*Action:* I ran moderated usability sessions with five early users. Every participant skipped the guided tour within the first two steps. When I asked why, they said they preferred to explore on their own and return to guidance only when stuck. I redesigned the onboarding to be contextual, showing tooltips only at points where users actually paused or clicked the wrong thing.

*Result:* Activation improved meaningfully in a follow-up test. More importantly, the research gave the whole team a more accurate picture of how our users actually learn new tools.

---

Q: How do you handle a stakeholder who strongly disagrees with your design direction?

*Situation:* A product manager wanted to add several promotional banners to a dashboard I had designed to be clean and task-focused. The disagreement was significant.

*Task:* I needed to either revise my design or make a credible case for keeping it, without damaging the working relationship.

*Action:* I asked the PM what outcome the banners were meant to drive. She explained the team needed to push feature adoption. I proposed an alternative: a single contextual nudge that appeared after a user completed a key task, rather than multiple static banners. I showed a quick wireframe comparison and tied the recommendation to a user quote from a past research session.

*Result:* The PM agreed to test my alternative first. The contextual nudge outperformed the banner concept in testing and became the shipped solution. She later said having the rationale backed by user data made the decision much easier to accept.

---

Q: Tell us about a project where you simplified a complex workflow for non-technical users.

*Situation:* I worked on an internal tool used by warehouse staff to log inventory discrepancies. The existing flow had many steps and required users to type in product codes under time pressure.

*Task:* I was asked to reduce errors and speed up the logging process for staff who were not comfortable with computers.

*Action:* I shadowed three warehouse workers during their shifts to understand where they got stuck. The biggest friction point was manual code entry. I redesigned the flow to use barcode scanning as the primary input, cut the required fields down to only what was essential, and added inline validation so errors surfaced immediately instead of at submission.

*Result:* The redesigned flow was significantly shorter and the operations team reported a clear drop in error-related escalations after launch. Workers said the new tool felt like it was built for their actual job, not just adapted from a desktop system.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use the STAR format for all behavioural questions. Structure your answer around Situation (brief context), Task (your specific responsibility), Action (what you personally did, not what the team did), and Result (a concrete or observable outcome). Keep the Situation and Task short. Spend most of your time on Action and Result, since that is where interviewers find the most signal.

Use a design process frame for 'how do you approach X' questions. A clean structure is: understand the problem, research and define, ideate and prioritise, prototype and test, ship and measure. You do not need to walk through every stage every time, but showing that you think in a structured way reassures interviewers that you would not jump straight to solutions.

For portfolio walkthroughs, lead with the problem. Candidates who open with 'so here is the screen I designed' lose interviewers quickly. Open instead with: 'The user problem was X, the business goal was Y, and here is how I approached it.' Then walk through decisions and trade-offs, not just the final output.

For disagreement or conflict questions, show you rely on evidence, not just opinion. Indianic interviewers, like most at product-focused companies, want to see that you make your case with something concrete, whether that is a user quote, a usability finding, or a tested prototype. Framing your recommendation as 'our research showed' or 'when we tested this' lands better than 'I felt' or 'I thought.'

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Candidates who have interviewed at indianic typically report that interviewers look for the following qualities:

Clear design thinking, not just visual skill. The portfolio matters, but interviewers probe the decisions behind screens. Being able to explain why you made a choice, what you considered and rejected, and what trade-offs you accepted signals strong product thinking.

Collaboration instinct. Indianic works across product, engineering, and client teams. Interviewers want to see that you have experience working across functions and that you can communicate with developers and business stakeholders without becoming defensive about your work.

User empathy grounded in real research. Saying you 'care about users' is not enough. Interviewers want to hear about specific research methods you have used, what you discovered, and how it changed your direction.

Business awareness. Product designers at companies like indianic are expected to understand that design decisions have commercial consequences. Showing that you think about metrics, adoption, and business goals alongside user needs makes a strong impression.

Composure under pushback. Candidates report that interviewers sometimes challenge design decisions deliberately, even sound ones, to see how you handle pressure. Stay calm, acknowledge the concern, and return to evidence rather than digging in defensively.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Portfolio and storytelling (start here)
Select three to four projects that show range: one with heavy research, one shaped by a tight constraint, and one with a clear observable outcome. For each, practise a short walkthrough that opens with the problem, not the screens. Record yourself or do a mock run with a peer.

Company research
Explore indianic's public case studies and product work to understand the types of clients and products their teams work on. Think about which of your past projects connect most directly to those challenges, and lead with those examples in your portfolio walkthrough.

Behavioural prep
List eight to ten situations from your past work: a stakeholder conflict, a research surprise, a tight deadline, a design that did not land as expected. Practise turning each into a STAR answer. Focus on being specific about what you personally did, not what the team did.

Design exercise readiness
Candidates report indianic sometimes includes a take-home or in-session design brief. Practise working through briefs out loud, asking clarifying questions before diving in, and presenting your reasoning alongside your output. Interviewers often care more about your process than your final screens.

Questions to ask
Prepare three to four thoughtful questions. Good ones include: 'How does the design team collaborate with product and engineering on a typical project?' or 'What does success look like for someone joining this role in the first few months?' Avoid questions whose answers are clearly on the company website.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping to the solution without framing the problem. Interviewers at product-focused companies flag candidates who skip problem definition. Always start your answer by explaining what you were trying to solve before describing what you designed.

Saying 'we' when asked what you personally did. If you say 'we redesigned the checkout flow,' an interviewer cannot tell what role you actually played. Be specific: 'I led the research and wireframing; a visual designer handled the final UI.'

Showing only polished final screens. Indianic interviewers find more signal in your early-stage thinking: rough wireframes, research notes, and iterations. If your portfolio only shows finished work, at least describe what came before and what you tried and rejected.

Leaving outcomes vague. Even without hard numbers, you can describe outcomes concretely: 'The operations team reported fewer escalations after launch' or 'every participant completed the revised flow without errors in testing.' Statements like 'users loved it' do not give interviewers anything to hold onto.

Arriving with no questions. Candidates who have nothing to ask signal low curiosity or low preparation. Prepare questions in advance and ask them genuinely.

Not tailoring your portfolio to the company. If indianic works heavily on enterprise or B2B products, leading with a consumer app case study is a missed opportunity. Review their public work and align your strongest examples to the type of challenges they actually face.
