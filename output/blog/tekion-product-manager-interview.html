01 Overview

Overview

Tekion builds cloud-native software for automotive retail, including Dealer Management Systems (DMS), CRM, and Finance and Insurance (F&I) tools used by car dealerships. Founded by Jay Vijayan, previously CIO at Tesla, the company is known for a fast-paced, engineering-led culture with a large India presence, primarily in Bangalore.

As of July 2026, knok jobradar tracked 16 open PM roles at Tekion. Candidates report the process is thorough and typically spans multiple rounds covering product sense, analytical thinking, and domain knowledge in automotive retail or enterprise SaaS. Interviewers pay close attention to whether you understand how dealerships operate, not just how software works.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates report these questions coming up frequently across Tekion PM interviews. They combine core PM skills with automotive-retail-specific scenarios.

  1. 'How would you redesign Tekion's DMS to reduce the time a service advisor spends on a single repair order?'
  2. 'Walk me through how you would prioritize features when the sales team and the service department have conflicting requests.'
  3. 'How do you define and measure success for a new F&I digitization feature?'
  4. 'Tekion's end users are often dealership staff who are not technical. How does that shape the way you write requirements and present roadmaps?'
  5. 'Tell me about a time you had to make a product decision with incomplete or ambiguous data.'
  6. 'A large competitor just launched a feature that dealerships love. As a PM, how do you respond?'
  7. 'How would you build an AI-powered pricing recommendation tool for used-car inventory, and what data inputs would you need?'
  8. 'Describe a time you worked closely with engineering to unblock a delayed feature. What did you personally do?'
  9. 'A key enterprise client, a large dealer group, is threatening to churn. What is your role as PM in this situation?'
  10. 'What metrics would you track to evaluate the success of Tekion's mobile app for service technicians?'
  11. 'How would you explain an API integration to a dealership operator who has no technology background?'
  12. 'Tell me about a product you admire outside automotive and how its core idea could improve a dealership workflow.'

For behavioral questions, Tekion interviewers typically follow up with 'what would you do differently?' so prepare a reflection for each story.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a time you had to make a product decision with incomplete data.

*Situation:* I was PM for a B2B SaaS platform where we needed to decide whether to build a bulk-upload feature for enterprise clients. Usage analytics only covered a short period and the sample size was small.

*Task:* I had to make a go/no-go recommendation before the next sprint planning session, without the luxury of waiting for more data.

*Action:* I ran quick interviews with four enterprise client success managers to understand how clients were handling bulk operations manually. I also reviewed support tickets tagged 'data entry' over the previous quarter. The qualitative signal was consistent even without large-scale quantitative data. I made a structured recommendation that explicitly named the data gaps and listed my assumptions, so the team could revisit them if anything turned out to be wrong.

*Result:* The feature shipped the following quarter. Adoption among enterprise accounts was strong, which the client success team flagged internally as a positive signal. More importantly, the habit of naming assumptions explicitly became a team norm we carried forward.

---

Q: Walk me through how you prioritized features when two teams had conflicting requests.

*Situation:* At a previous company, the sales team wanted a quick-quote widget on the customer portal, while the operations team wanted an order-status tracker. Both said their request was urgent.

*Task:* I had to allocate one engineering sprint between two high-visibility asks with a firm deadline.

*Action:* I mapped each request to a north-star metric: revenue at risk for the sales request vs. support cost reduction for the operations one. I got rough effort estimates from engineering for both. Internal support data showed that order-status questions were among the commonly cited reasons for escalations, while the quick-quote widget was tied to a new pilot with uncertain conversion. I proposed shipping the tracker first and the widget in the following sprint, and shared a written rationale with both teams so there was no ambiguity about the reasoning.

*Result:* Both teams accepted the plan. The tracker shipped on schedule. The process of sharing a written prioritization rationale became something the team adopted as a standard going forward.

---

Q: Describe a time you worked with engineering to unblock a delayed feature.

*Situation:* A payments integration feature was stuck because a third-party API was returning inconsistent responses in the staging environment. The delay threatened a commitment we had made to a key client.

*Task:* As PM, I was accountable for the delivery date and needed to find a path forward without waiting indefinitely for the vendor.

*Action:* I joined engineering standups daily for one week instead of my usual cadence. I found the team was waiting on the third-party vendor's support queue, which was slow to respond. I escalated directly to the vendor's account manager, since we were an enterprise client, and simultaneously worked with engineering to build a lightweight mock mode for internal QA so testing could continue without the live API.

*Result:* The vendor responded within a couple of days of the escalation. The feature launched with a short delay rather than the much longer one that had been projected. The mock mode we built also proved useful for future integrations.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For product design and improvement questions (such as 'How would you improve the DMS?'): Start by clarifying who the user is, what their job-to-be-done is, and what the specific pain point is. Then list potential solutions, evaluate trade-offs, and pick one with a clear rationale. Avoid jumping to features before naming the problem.

For prioritization questions: Use a simple impact-vs-effort approach. State the metric you are optimizing for first, then evaluate each option against it. Tekion interviewers appreciate when you tie features to either revenue impact or operational cost for the dealership, because those are the outcomes Tekion's clients actually care about.

For metrics questions: Name a north-star metric that reflects the user's core job, then add two or three supporting metrics and one guardrail metric (something you would not want to accidentally harm). For example, for a service-technician app, a north-star might be 'repair orders completed per technician per shift'; a guardrail might be 'error rate on repair order submission'.

For behavioral questions: Use the STAR structure (Situation, Task, Action, Result) and keep Situation and Task brief. Spend most of your answer on Action (what you specifically did, not what the team did) and Result (a concrete outcome, even if qualitative). Prepare a 'what I would do differently' line for each story, as Tekion interviewers typically ask for it.

For competitive or market questions: Acknowledge the competitor's move without panic. Show that you can separate 'what they shipped' from 'why it resonates with customers' and use that insight to decide whether Tekion should respond, learn from it, or double down on differentiation.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Domain curiosity, not just domain knowledge. You do not need automotive retail experience, but you need to show genuine curiosity about how dealerships make money, where they lose time, and why legacy software has been hard to replace. Candidates who ask good clarifying questions about the dealership context typically stand out.

Customer empathy for non-technical users. Dealership staff, including service advisors, finance managers, and lot attendants, are the actual end users of Tekion's software. Interviewers are sensitive to answers that assume users are comfortable with technology. Show that you design for the real person, not an idealized tech-savvy user.

Structured, data-informed thinking. You do not need to recall exact numbers under pressure, but your reasoning should be organized. Interviewers want to see that you can separate signal from noise and that you know what data to look for even when you do not have it yet.

Cross-functional ownership. Tekion sits at the intersection of sales, engineering, customer success, and enterprise clients. Interviewers look for PMs who can influence without authority and who communicate decisions clearly to multiple audiences at different levels.

Comfort with ambiguity. Tekion is a growth-stage company with a rapidly evolving product. Candidates who need a perfectly scoped brief before they can act are a poor fit. Show that you can move forward with partial information and course-correct quickly.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Learn the domain first. Read about how automotive dealerships operate: the difference between new and used car sales, what the service department does, and what an F&I manager handles. Understand why legacy DMS platforms have historically been hard to replace (data lock-in, deep workflow integration). Tekion's public blog and press coverage are a starting point.

Study Tekion's products closely. Look at Tekion's product pages for DMS, CRM, and the service module. Read customer reviews on G2 or Capterra to understand what dealerships praise and complain about. Map these pain points to potential interview questions so your answers feel grounded rather than generic.

Prepare your STAR stories. Write out at least five stories covering: a difficult prioritization decision, a time you used data to make a call, a conflict with a stakeholder, a product you launched end-to-end, and a time you changed direction after being wrong. Practice delivering each one clearly and under time pressure.

Practice frameworks and run mock interviews. Rehearse answering product design questions using the impact-vs-effort and north-star-metric approaches described in the frameworks section. Do at least two mock interviews with someone who will give honest feedback on both content and structure, not just encouragement.

Prepare questions for your interviewers. Ask about Tekion's roadmap direction, how PM decisions get made across teams, what the biggest product challenges are right now, and what a successful first few months in the role looks like. Thoughtful questions signal genuine interest and help you evaluate the role too.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

1. Not knowing what a DMS is. Walking into a Tekion interview without understanding what a Dealer Management System does is a common and avoidable mistake. It signals a lack of basic research about the company and the industry it serves.

2. Giving generic product answers. Saying 'I would talk to users and iterate' without connecting it to the dealership context shows shallow preparation. Every answer should be grounded in the automotive retail world.

3. Designing only for tech-savvy users. A common error is pitching AI dashboards or complex analytics tools as solutions for dealership staff who primarily work on a showroom floor or in a service bay. Interviewers notice when your solution ignores the real user.

4. Overclaiming in STAR answers. Saying 'I built this feature' when you led a team is a red flag. Use 'I' for decisions and actions that were genuinely yours, and 'we' for shared team outcomes. Tekion interviewers typically probe with follow-up questions to check accuracy.

5. Weak metrics thinking. Naming only vanity metrics like page views or downloads without connecting them to business outcomes is a gap that stands out. Always tie your success metric to something a dealership GM or Tekion's leadership would actually act on.

6. Coming without questions. Candidates who have nothing to ask signal low interest in the role. Prepare specific questions about Tekion's product strategy, how PMs collaborate with engineering, and what success looks like in the first few months.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Tekion PM interview process typically have?

Candidates report the process typically includes a recruiter screen, one or two product or case rounds, a behavioral round, and a final conversation with senior leadership. The exact structure can vary by level and team. Confirm the format with your recruiter early on so you can prepare without surprises.

Do I need automotive industry experience to get a PM role at Tekion?

Automotive experience is helpful but not always required, especially for associate or mid-level PM roles. What matters more is your ability to learn a complex domain quickly and design for non-technical users. Candidates without automotive backgrounds often succeed by doing thorough research on how dealerships operate before the interview.

What salary can I expect as a PM at Tekion in India?

Based on knok jobradar data, PM salaries in India range from 12-20 LPA at the associate level to 24-40 LPA for mid-level roles with three to six years of experience, and 40-60 LPA for senior PM positions. Group and Principal PM roles can reach 55-90 LPA and above. Your actual offer depends on your specific experience, the team, and how you negotiate.

Does Tekion give a take-home assignment or case study?

Some candidates report receiving a product case or take-home exercise, typically involving a product improvement or prioritization scenario. Candidates report this is more common for senior roles than for associate or mid-level positions. Check with your recruiter whether a case assignment is part of your specific process so you can prepare without being caught off guard.

How important are SQL or data skills for a Tekion PM role?

Tekion values data-driven PMs, but SQL proficiency is typically not a hard requirement at the PM level. You should be comfortable describing what data you would look at and why, and you should understand core concepts like funnels, retention, and cohort analysis. If SQL does come up in an interview, being honest about your current level and showing genuine curiosity to learn is generally well-received.
