02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These are the questions candidates report most often in knot iOS Engineer interviews. Prepare a concrete example from your past work for each one.

  1. Walk us through a complex iOS feature you owned end-to-end: the architecture decisions, trade-offs you made, and the final outcome.
  2. How do you manage memory in Swift? Explain strong, weak, and unowned references with a real scenario from your own code.
  3. knot's product is used at scale. How would you design a performant list view that fetches data from a remote API and handles pagination?
  4. Describe your experience with UIKit versus SwiftUI. For a new feature being built today, which would you pick and why?
  5. How do you handle concurrency in iOS? Walk through GCD, OperationQueue, and Swift async/await, and explain when you would use each.
  6. The app needs to work offline and sync when connectivity returns. How would you architect that feature?
  7. How would you debug a memory leak discovered in a production iOS app? Walk through your tooling and process step by step.
  8. How do you write testable iOS code? Describe your approach to unit testing and UI testing, and explain what you choose to mock.
  9. Walk us through the iOS app lifecycle and explain how it influences your architecture and state management decisions.
  10. How do you evaluate and adopt new Apple APIs announced at WWDC? Give an example of a recent adoption from your own work.
  11. Describe a time you disagreed with a technical decision in your team. What did you do, and what was the outcome?
  12. How would you migrate a large UIKit codebase to SwiftUI incrementally without breaking existing functionality?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format for all experience-based questions. Here are three worked examples.

Q: Walk us through a complex iOS feature you built end-to-end.

*Situation:* The chat screen at my previous company was dropping frames on older iPhones, and users were leaving negative reviews about the scrolling experience.

*Task:* I was asked to own the performance improvement end-to-end, from diagnosis to production release, within one sprint.

*Action:* I used Instruments to profile the app and found that auto-layout was recalculating cell heights on every scroll event. I switched to pre-calculated, cached cell heights, adopted diffable data sources to avoid full table reloads, and moved image decoding to a background queue using a custom cache layer.

*Result:* Frame drops on that screen fell to near zero in internal testing, and the approach became the team standard for all list-heavy screens going forward.

---

Q: How would you debug a memory leak found in a production iOS app?

*Situation:* After a feature release, our app's memory footprint kept climbing on long sessions, and Crashlytics was showing out-of-memory crashes on older devices.

*Task:* I had to identify the root cause and fix it before the next release, with no clear reproduction steps from users.

*Action:* I reproduced the session pattern in a simulator, then used Xcode's Memory Graph Debugger to trace the object graph. I found a delegate property declared as strong instead of weak inside a view controller. I then audited every closure in that module for captured self references and replaced them with weak captures where needed.

*Result:* Out-of-memory crashes stopped appearing in Crashlytics within two app versions, and memory growth on a long session dropped measurably in our internal load tests.

---

Q: Describe a time you disagreed with a technical decision in your team.

*Situation:* My team was about to ship a new feature using a third-party analytics SDK that I felt introduced a privacy risk, because it collected device identifiers by default.

*Task:* I needed to raise the concern without blocking the release timeline or creating unnecessary conflict.

*Action:* I prepared a short document comparing the SDK's default collection behaviour against Apple's App Privacy guidelines and shared it before the sprint review. I proposed either configuring the SDK to minimal collection mode or evaluating a privacy-first alternative, and made clear I supported the team's timeline.

*Result:* The team agreed to configure minimal collection mode, which we shipped with the feature on schedule. The approach became part of our SDK evaluation checklist going forward.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Having a go-to structure for each question type stops you from going blank mid-interview.

For technical 'how does X work' questions: Lead with the concept in one sentence, give a concrete code-level example from your own work, then mention a trade-off or common gotcha. Interviewers at product companies like knot want to see hands-on experience, not textbook definitions.

For architecture and design questions: Use a three-step structure. First, clarify the constraints (scale, offline support, team size). Second, walk through your chosen pattern and the reasoning behind it (MVVM vs VIPER, for instance). Third, acknowledge the trade-offs honestly. Saying 'VIPER adds boilerplate, but for a larger team it reduces merge conflicts on feature branches' signals engineering maturity.

For behavioral questions: Use STAR. Keep Situation and Task brief, and spend most of your time on Action, which should describe what you specifically did, not 'we'. Make Result concrete. If you cannot share a metric, describe a qualitative outcome: 'the approach became the team standard' or 'the crash rate dropped to near zero.'

For system design questions: Think in layers: data model, local persistence (Core Data, SwiftData, or Realm), networking layer (URLSession, Combine, async/await), UI architecture, and error handling. Mention testability at each layer. knot-scale products care about offline-first thinking and crash resilience, so bring those up proactively rather than waiting to be asked.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates report, knot iOS Engineer interviewers typically look for four things.

Depth over breadth. They want you to go deep on features you have actually built, not give a surface-level tour of every iOS framework. One well-explained, real-world example is worth more than five vague ones.

Ownership mindset. Questions about past work are partly a test of how you describe your role. Use 'I' not 'we' when explaining your specific contributions. Interviewers want to understand your individual impact, not the team's collective output.

Pragmatic architecture thinking. knot is a product company, which typically means shipping matters. Interviewers want to see that you balance correctness with delivery. Saying 'we could use VIPER but for this sprint MVVM was the right call' is a strong signal.

Communication clarity. iOS interviews often include a live coding or whiteboarding component. Candidates report that thinking out loud, naming trade-offs as you go, and asking clarifying questions scores higher than silently arriving at a perfect answer.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused four-week plan based on what knot iOS interviews typically cover.

Week 1: Swift and memory fundamentals. Review value types vs reference types, ARC, retain cycles, and generics. Write small programs that deliberately create and then fix memory leaks. Revisit closures and capture lists until you can explain them without notes.

Week 2: UIKit and SwiftUI. Build a small app using both frameworks. Practise building a table view with diffable data sources in UIKit, then recreate the same screen in SwiftUI using a List. Focus on state management: ObservableObject, @State, and @Binding.

Week 3: Concurrency, networking, and persistence. Implement a feature that fetches paginated API data using async/await, stores it with Core Data or SwiftData, and shows cached results when offline. This maps directly to the offline-sync scenario that commonly appears in knot interviews.

Week 4: Mock interviews and system design. Do at least two full mock interviews out loud with a peer or on a practice platform. Prepare your three strongest project stories and rehearse them until they feel natural. 07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

These are the patterns that most often hurt candidates in iOS Engineer interviews at product companies like knot.

Describing 'we' instead of 'I'. When asked what you did, saying 'we built' hides your individual contribution. Interviewers need to assess your specific skills, so be precise about your role.

Treating UIKit and SwiftUI as opposites. Many candidates say 'UIKit is outdated, I only use SwiftUI' or the reverse. Most production apps use both. Show you are comfortable mixing them and can explain when each is the right choice.

Skipping edge cases in live coding. Candidates report that interviewers pay close attention to how you handle nil values, network errors, and empty states. Do not jump straight to the happy path without at least naming the edge cases you would handle.

Over-engineering architecture questions. Proposing VIPER for a simple settings screen signals a mismatch between design complexity and practical need. Start simple, then add layers only when the requirements demand it.

Not asking clarifying questions. In design and system questions, diving in without confirming scope (scale, team size, existing constraints) typically leads to answers that miss the point entirely. Ask first, then design.

Leaving Result vague in STAR answers. 'It went well' is not a result. Even without a specific number, describe a qualitative outcome: 'the crash rate dropped to near zero in the following release' is concrete and credible.
