02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Dream DevOps interviews blend infrastructure fundamentals, incident-response thinking, and collaboration signals. Based on candidate accounts, these questions come up repeatedly:

  1. Walk us through a CI/CD pipeline you built or maintained from scratch.
  2. How do you handle a production outage when you cannot immediately identify the root cause?
  3. What is your approach to container orchestration, and when would you choose Kubernetes over a simpler setup?
  4. Describe how you have used infrastructure as code. Which tools did you use and why did you pick them?
  5. How do you manage secrets and sensitive configuration in a cloud-native environment?
  6. A developer says deployments are too slow. How do you diagnose and fix it?
  7. How do you approach cloud cost optimisation without sacrificing reliability?
  8. Explain your monitoring and alerting philosophy. What makes an alert 'good' versus 'noisy'?
  9. How would you design a zero-downtime deployment for a stateful service?
  10. Tell us about a time you had to push back on a developer request for the sake of security or stability.
  11. How do you keep your skills current in a field where tooling changes every year?
  12. How would you onboard a new engineer to your infrastructure codebase in two weeks?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for behavioural and scenario questions. Here are three examples tailored to questions Dream commonly asks.

---

Q: Walk us through a CI/CD pipeline you built or maintained from scratch.

*Situation:* Our team was deploying a monolith manually through shared server credentials. Every release took hours and deployments often broke because of differences between staging and production.

*Task:* I was asked to modernise the release process and make deployments both faster and repeatable.

*Action:* I set up GitHub Actions as the CI layer, with separate pipelines for unit tests, integration tests, and Docker image builds. I wrote Terraform scripts to provision a staging environment on AWS that mirrored production exactly. For deployment, I used ArgoCD to sync Kubernetes manifests from a Git repository, so every merge to the main branch triggered an automatic, auditable rollout.

*Result:* Deployment time dropped considerably. Rollbacks, which previously required manual steps from a senior engineer, became a single Git revert that any team member could trigger.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you had to push back on a developer request for the sake of security or stability.

*Situation:* A developer asked me to give a new microservice admin-level IAM permissions so they could 'get it working quickly' before a demo.

*Task:* I needed to keep the timeline credible without creating a security hole that would be forgotten and persist in production.

*Action:* I declined the broad permissions and offered to pair with the developer that same afternoon. We identified the specific API actions the service actually needed, wrote a scoped IAM policy, and tested it together. I also created a Terraform module template so future services could start from a least-privilege baseline without anyone having to figure it out from scratch.

*Result:* The service launched on time with a correct policy. The template was adopted by two other teams the following sprint.

---

Q: Explain your monitoring and alerting philosophy. What makes an alert 'good'?

*Situation:* When I joined my previous team, there were alerts configured across every service, but on-call engineers were ignoring most of them because they fired too frequently with no clear action required.

*Task:* My goal was to restore trust in the alerting system so that when a page fired, the on-call engineer knew it was real and knew exactly what to do.

*Action:* I audited every alert and asked three questions for each: does this indicate a user-facing problem, is there a clear runbook action, and has it fired falsely in the past month? Alerts that failed any question were either tuned or removed. I replaced most cause-based alerts (CPU above a threshold) with symptom-based ones (error rate, latency, availability).

*Result:* Alert volume dropped substantially. The on-call rotation became noticeably quieter and the team started trusting pages again, which meant real incidents got caught faster.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For technical concept questions: Start with the 'what' (define the concept in one sentence), move to the 'when' (the scenario where it actually matters), then the 'how' (your personal experience or a concrete example). Avoid abstract definitions that float without grounding them in something you have actually done or seen.

For behavioural questions: Use a condensed STAR approach. Keep Situation and Task together in two or three sentences, spend most of your time on Action (specific steps you personally took, not 'we as a team'), and close with a Result that is concrete even if you cannot quote an exact number.

For system design questions: Start by clarifying requirements (scale, SLA expectations, team size). Sketch the architecture top-down: entry point, compute layer, storage, observability. Call out the tradeoffs you are making at each step. Dream interviewers typically want to see a reasoned process, not a single 'correct' answer delivered with false confidence.

For conflict or push-back questions: Acknowledge the other person's goal first. Explain the risk in plain terms without jargon. Propose an alternative that gets them most of what they want. The interviewer is looking for someone who can hold a technical position while keeping the working relationship intact.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Candidates who have interviewed at Dream report a consistent theme: interviewers care less about which specific tool you use and more about whether you can explain your reasoning. A common observation is that the hands-on round often involves a broken environment or a misconfigured deployment that you fix live, so comfort with ambiguity matters as much as technical knowledge.

Automation-first thinking is valued. If you describe a process as manual without also explaining what would need to be true before you could automate it, expect a follow-up. Dream's engineering culture (based on publicly available engineering blog posts and candidate reports) treats operational toil as a bug to be fixed, not a permanent way of working.

Security awareness is weighted heavily. Expect at least one question about secrets management, least-privilege IAM, or network segmentation. Candidates report that vague answers like 'we used Vault' without explaining the why or the how get probed until you demonstrate depth or reveal a gap.

Communication across roles also counts. DevOps at Dream sits close to product and backend teams. Interviewers want to see that you can translate infrastructure constraints into language a developer can act on, and that you treat developers as partners rather than 'those people who keep requesting prod access.'

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Foundations and gaps

  1. Map your experience against the salary bands and role level you are targeting. Entry (0-2 years), mid (3-5 years), and senior (6-9 years) interviews at Dream are reported to differ significantly in expected depth and ownership.
  2. Revisit core concepts: Linux process management, networking basics (DNS, TCP handshake, load balancing), and container fundamentals. These appear in every technical screen regardless of seniority.
  3. Spin up a personal AWS or GCP project if you do not have one. Practice writing Terraform for a simple web application with a database backend. Recruiters report that candidates without any hands-on IaC experience struggle in the practical round.
  4. Write out your 3 strongest incident stories in STAR format. Each should highlight a different skill: one for technical diagnosis, one for communication under pressure, one for a systemic fix that prevented recurrence.

Week 2: Practice and polish

  1. Do at least 3 mock system design sessions out loud, even if it means talking to yourself or recording a voice note. The gap between thinking an answer and saying it clearly is significant.
  2. Review Dream's engineering blog and any publicly available talks from their infrastructure team. Interviewers appreciate candidates who connect company-specific context to their own experience.
  3. Prepare 2 or 3 thoughtful questions to ask the interviewer. Good ones focus on team structure, incident culture, or how infra and product collaborate. Skip anything clearly answered on the careers page.
  4. The day before each round, re-read your own resume from an interviewer's perspective. Every bullet point is fair game for a follow-up question.
07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Talking about 'we' instead of 'I'. Interviewers are assessing your specific contribution. When you say 'we built a pipeline,' they will ask 'what was your part specifically?' Prepare to own your piece of every story clearly.

Skipping the 'why'. Naming a tool (Kubernetes, Prometheus, Terraform) without explaining the reasoning behind the choice signals that you followed a tutorial rather than made a decision. Always say why you chose it over the alternatives available to you.

Treating the hands-on round like a written exam. Candidates report that Dream's practical round rewards thinking out loud and asking clarifying questions more than reaching a perfect answer quickly. If you go silent and then produce a solution, interviewers may worry about how you behave in a real incident.

Under-preparing for behavioural rounds. Many DevOps candidates focus entirely on technical prep and freeze on questions like 'tell me about a time you disagreed with a senior engineer.' Prepare these stories in advance, not in the interview room.

Not asking about on-call. On-call expectations vary widely across companies. Not asking signals that you have not thought it through. Ask directly: 'How is on-call structured and how often does the team get paged?' It shows maturity, not weakness.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Dream DevOps interview typically have?

Candidates typically report 3-4 rounds: a recruiter or HR screen, a technical concepts round, a hands-on practical or system design session, and a final conversation with a senior engineer or engineering manager. The exact structure can vary by team and role level. It is worth asking your recruiter at the start of the process how many rounds to expect and what each one covers.

What tools does Dream focus on in the technical round?

Based on candidate reports, the technical round covers Kubernetes, Docker, CI/CD pipelines (GitHub Actions, Jenkins, or similar tools), Terraform or other IaC tools, and cloud platforms (primarily AWS). Monitoring tools like Prometheus, Grafana, or Datadog also come up regularly. Rather than memorising commands, prepare to explain the reasoning behind your tool choices and describe specific problems each tool helped you solve.

Is there a coding round for DevOps Engineers at Dream?

Candidates report that Dream does not typically include a full data-structures-and-algorithms coding round for DevOps roles. The practical component is more likely to involve scripting (Bash or Python), fixing a broken Kubernetes deployment, or writing Terraform for a given scenario. Strong scripting fluency and comfort with reading error logs matter more than competitive programming skills for this role.

What salary can I expect as a DevOps Engineer at Dream?

Dream-specific offer data is not publicly reported in enough volume to quote directly. For the broader DevOps market in India, knok jobradar data from July 2026 shows ranges of 6-12 LPA at entry level (0-2 years), 15-28 LPA at mid level (3-5 years), 30-50 LPA at senior level (6-9 years), and 45-70+ LPA at lead or staff level. Once you have an offer, it is reasonable to cite publicly reported market data for your experience band when negotiating.

How do I stand out in the system design round?

Candidates who stand out typically do three things: they clarify requirements before drawing anything, they name tradeoffs explicitly (for example, 'I am choosing this approach over that one because of reliability at our stated scale'), and they bring observability into the design from the start rather than as an afterthought. Interviewers at Dream are reported to value the reasoning process as much as the final architecture diagram.
