01 Overview

Overview

Nium is a Singapore-headquartered fintech company that powers cross-border payments, card issuing, and banking-as-a-service for enterprises globally. Data Engineers here build and maintain pipelines that move, transform, and serve payment transaction data, supporting compliance, product analytics, and real-time risk monitoring.

As of mid-2026, knok jobradar tracked 542 open Data Engineer roles across India, with the largest concentration in Bangalore (92 roles), followed by Delhi (66) and Hyderabad and Pune (23 each). Nium had 39 open Data Engineer roles at that time, one of the highest counts among Indian fintech employers, reflecting the company's rapid growth across Asia-Pacific and Middle East corridors.

Candidates report that the Nium interview process typically runs three to four rounds: a recruiter or HR screen, a technical round covering SQL and Python, a system design round focused on data pipelines, and a final round with a hiring manager or senior engineer covering technical depth and behavioural questions. The process places a notable emphasis on fintech domain knowledge and data reliability, so generic preparation is rarely sufficient.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions are compiled from candidate reports and Nium's publicly stated engineering focus areas. Expect a mix of coding, system design, and behavioural questions across rounds.

  1. Walk me through how you would design a real-time data pipeline for cross-border payment transactions at scale.
  2. How do you handle schema evolution in a payments data warehouse without breaking downstream consumers or reports?
  3. Write a SQL query to find the top 5 countries by total transaction volume from a payments table. How would you optimise it for a large dataset?
  4. What is your experience with Apache Kafka or Flink, and how have you applied them to financial data streams?
  5. How would you build a reconciliation pipeline between an internal ledger and external banking partner records?
  6. Describe how you would design a multi-currency transaction data model to support regulatory reporting across several jurisdictions.
  7. How do you guarantee exactly-once semantics in a payments ingestion pipeline, and when would you relax that requirement?
  8. Describe a time you discovered a data quality issue in a production pipeline. How did you investigate and fix it?
  9. How would you partition a large transactions table to make queries by settlement date and country efficient?
  10. What tools and practices do you use for data lineage, observability, and alerting in a fintech data stack?
  11. How do you handle late-arriving records in a batch pipeline where downstream settlement reports have already been generated?
  12. Nium operates under multiple regulatory frameworks. How would you design a data pipeline that can serve both GDPR and India's DPDP compliance requirements?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for all experience-based questions. Here are three examples tailored to Nium's domain.

Q: Describe a time you built or improved a data pipeline for a high-volume payments use case.

*Situation:* At my previous company, the analytics team reported on payment transactions using a nightly batch job, which meant dashboards were several hours stale during peak business windows.

*Task:* I was asked to reduce data latency and build a pipeline that could support near-real-time reporting for the operations team.

*Action:* I introduced Kafka as the event streaming layer, consuming from the payments service's transaction topic. I wrote PySpark Structured Streaming jobs to enrich each event with currency exchange rates and merchant category codes, then landed the output into a Delta Lake table using a medallion architecture. I also added a Great Expectations data quality layer at the bronze-to-silver step to catch malformed records before they reached reporting.

*Result:* Data latency dropped from several hours to a matter of minutes. The operations team could act on transaction anomalies in near real-time, and the quality checks caught upstream issues before they reached client-facing dashboards.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you debugged a critical data quality issue in production.

*Situation:* A vendor updated their core banking API and, without prior notice, changed the encoding of a key transaction field. Our pipeline silently passed null values downstream for a portion of settlement records.

*Task:* I needed to identify the root cause, prevent further data loss, and restore integrity for already-processed records.

*Action:* I added schema validation checks at the ingestion boundary using Great Expectations, which immediately surfaced the encoding mismatch. I traced the field-level change by comparing a sample of raw API responses before and after the vendor update date. I then wrote a backfill job to reprocess affected records using corrected transformation logic, and added an alert so any future schema drift would page the on-call engineer before records reached the silver layer.

*Result:* The pipeline was fully recovered within a single business day. The backfill restored all affected records, and we put a formal schema-change communication process in place with the vendor going forward.

---

Q: How have you designed a data model to support compliance or regulatory reporting?

*Situation:* Our compliance team needed jurisdiction-specific transaction reports on a regular cadence, but the existing model stored all transactions in a single flat table with no clean separation by regulatory region.

*Task:* I was tasked with redesigning the model so the compliance team could self-serve their reporting without raising engineering tickets each time.

*Action:* I designed a star schema with a central fact_transactions table partitioned by settlement_date and country_code. I added slowly changing dimension tables for currency rates, regulatory jurisdiction mappings, and merchant categories. I documented the model in dbt with clear column descriptions and built pre-aggregated reporting views scoped by region and reporting period.

*Result:* Compliance report query times improved significantly, and the team went from needing engineering support for every filing to generating reports independently. The model was later extended to cover additional regulatory frameworks without structural changes.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For system design questions (pipelines, data models, architecture):
Start by clarifying requirements: ask about scale, latency tolerance, consistency needs, and downstream consumers before proposing anything. Sketch the components (ingestion, transformation, storage, serving) and explain trade-offs. For a payments context, always address idempotency, exactly-once semantics, and auditability. Nium interviewers want to see that you prioritise correctness before performance.

For SQL questions:
Think aloud. State your assumptions about table structure and indexes before writing. Produce a clean, correct query first, then discuss optimisation (partitioning, indexing, avoiding full scans). For fintech SQL, address deduplication and handling of NULL or partial records explicitly.

For behavioural questions (STAR method):
Situation: set the scene in one to two sentences. Task: describe what you were specifically responsible for. Action: use 'I' not 'we', and explain the concrete steps you took and why you chose them. Result: describe the measurable or qualitative impact clearly. Nium values engineers who can explain their decisions, not just their outcomes.

For domain questions (payments, fintech):
If you have direct payments experience, lead with it. If not, bridge from a related domain: 'In my previous role with financial data, I applied similar principles around reconciliation and audit trails.' Show awareness of concepts like settlement cycles, multi-currency handling, and idempotency.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on candidate reports and Nium's engineering blog and job postings, interviewers are typically looking for the following qualities.

Technical depth in data engineering fundamentals: Strong SQL (window functions, aggregations, query optimisation), proficiency in Python for data transformation, and hands-on experience with at least one distributed processing framework such as Spark or Flink.

Streaming and real-time data experience: Nium's business requires low-latency data for fraud detection, reconciliation, and reporting. Candidates with hands-on Kafka or Kinesis experience are at an advantage, especially at mid and senior levels.

Data quality and observability mindset: Interviewers want engineers who treat data correctness as a first-class concern. Mentioning tools like Great Expectations, dbt tests, or custom alerting frameworks signals maturity in this area.

Payments or fintech domain awareness: You do not need to be a payments expert, but understanding settlement, reconciliation, multi-currency data handling, and why idempotency matters in financial pipelines shows you can ramp quickly.

Communication and cross-team collaboration: Data Engineers at Nium work closely with compliance, product, and engineering teams. Interviewers will probe whether you can explain technical decisions clearly to non-technical stakeholders.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Core technical skills
Revise SQL thoroughly: window functions (RANK, LAG, LEAD), CTEs, aggregations with GROUP BY and HAVING, and query optimisation basics. Practise writing queries on payment-style schemas (transactions, accounts, settlement tables). Revise Python for data work: pandas, PySpark basics, and writing clean transformation logic.

Week 2: System design and streaming
Study the medallion architecture (bronze, silver, gold) and when to use batch versus streaming. Learn or revise Apache Kafka: producers, consumers, topics, consumer groups, and at-least-once versus exactly-once delivery. Practise designing a pipeline end-to-end, from raw event ingestion to a clean, query-ready layer.

Week 3: Fintech domain and Nium-specific prep
Read about how cross-border payments work: correspondent banking, real-time payment rails (UPI, SWIFT, etc.), and multi-currency settlement. Look at Nium's public engineering content to understand their stack and values. Prepare three to four STAR stories covering pipeline design, debugging, data quality, and cross-team collaboration.

Week 4: Mock interviews and review
Complete at least two full mock interviews covering SQL, system design, and behavioural questions. Review your weakest areas. Re-read the job description for the specific Nium role you applied to, and prepare two to three thoughtful questions for the interviewers about their data stack and team structure.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping into SQL without reading the question carefully: Many candidates write a query before checking for edge cases like NULLs, duplicates, or the correct level of aggregation. In a payments context, duplicates and partial records are common, and interviewers notice if you skip this step.

Designing pipelines without addressing correctness guarantees: Describing a Kafka-to-Spark-to-warehouse pipeline is not enough. Nium processes financial data, so interviewers expect you to address idempotency, exactly-once semantics, and failure recovery explicitly.

Generic STAR answers with no domain relevance: Saying 'I improved pipeline performance' is weak. Tying your answer to payment settlement data, reconciliation, or regulatory reporting shows you understand why Nium's work differs from general analytics.

Not researching Nium's business model: Candidates who cannot explain, at a basic level, what Nium does (cross-border payments, card issuing, banking-as-a-service) come across as unprepared. Spend time on their public product pages and engineering blog before the interview.

Staying silent during system design: Interviewers want to follow your reasoning. Asking no clarifying questions, or designing in silence and presenting a final answer at the end, is a common miss. Talk through trade-offs as you go.
