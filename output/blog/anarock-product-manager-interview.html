02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions reflect Anarock's focus on real estate technology, two-sided marketplace dynamics, and data-driven product decisions.

  1. Anarock serves both home buyers and channel partners (brokers). How would you prioritise a feature that benefits buyers but reduces revenue from channel partners?
  2. Walk through how you would design a home-loan eligibility tool for first-time buyers on Anarock's platform.
  3. How would you define and measure success for Anarock's property listing pages? What is your north-star metric and what are your guardrail metrics?
  4. India's real estate market differs sharply across cities. How would you build a product strategy that accounts for the gap between a Mumbai buyer and a buyer in a Tier-2 city?
  5. Describe a product you improved by analysing user behaviour data. How did you surface the insight and what did you change?
  6. A competitor like MagicBricks or NoBroker launches a feature Anarock does not have. How do you decide whether to build a similar feature or ignore it?
  7. How would you improve the journey from 'property search' to 'site visit booked' on Anarock's app?
  8. Anarock focuses on new-launch projects. How would you build a feature that helps buyers compare two new-launch projects side by side?
  9. Tell me about a time you used data to change your team's direction on a roadmap decision.
  10. How would you approach a premium subscription tier for serious home buyers on Anarock? What would justify the price?
  11. What is your framework for deciding which bugs to fix before launch versus shipping and fixing after?
  12. If Anarock wanted to expand into rental listings, how would you write a one-page product brief to pitch this to leadership?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How would you prioritise features for home buyers versus channel partners when they conflict?

*Situation:* At my previous company, we ran a two-sided marketplace where sellers wanted more listing controls but buyers wanted a cleaner, less cluttered search experience.

*Task:* I had to decide whether to add a 'featured placement' tool for sellers or improve search filters for buyers, when engineering capacity allowed only one.

*Action:* I mapped each feature to the company's primary revenue driver. Featured placements drove direct monetisation from sellers, but buyers who left due to a cluttered experience hurt long-term retention. I ran a user survey and found that buyer drop-off at the search step was the bigger conversion leak. I proposed a sequenced plan: improve buyer search first, then add seller placement controls in the following sprint to avoid cannibalising search quality.

*Result:* Buyer-to-inquiry conversion improved noticeably in the cycle after the change, and seller adoption of placement tools was stronger once the platform had a larger active buyer base.

---

Q: Describe a product you improved by deeply analysing user behaviour data.

*Situation:* Our team noticed users were dropping off midway through a multi-step onboarding flow, but no one had investigated exactly where the drop was happening.

*Task:* My task was to identify the specific drop-off point and propose a fix without committing to a major redesign.

*Action:* I pulled funnel data step by step and found the steepest drop happened on the 'verify phone number' screen, specifically on slower network connections. I spoke with a handful of users in that cohort and found the OTP timer was too short for low-bandwidth conditions. I worked with the engineer to extend the OTP window and add a 'resend OTP' shortcut above the fold.

*Result:* Onboarding completion in low-connectivity regions improved in the sprint following the fix, and support tickets about failed OTPs dropped sharply.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you used data to change your team's direction on a product decision.

*Situation:* The team was confident that adding video tours to listings would be our highest-impact feature for the quarter.

*Task:* I was asked to validate this assumption before we committed the engineering team for a full sprint cycle.

*Action:* I looked at session recordings and found that users who watched existing virtual tours had only marginally higher site-visit rates than users who did not. Meanwhile, users who used the 'compare projects' feature converted at a much higher rate. I built a one-page brief showing this data and walked the team through the funnel numbers.

*Result:* The team shifted the roadmap to prioritise a better compare-projects tool. It was a harder conversation, but the product lead agreed the data was clear and we avoided investing in a feature with weaker conversion evidence.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

CIRCLES (for design questions)
Comprehend the situation, Identify the user, Report user needs, Cut through prioritisation, List solutions, Evaluate trade-offs, Summarise. This is especially useful for 'design a feature for Anarock' questions where you need to show structured thinking before jumping to solutions.

STAR (for behavioural questions)
Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep the Situation and Task brief. Spend the most time on Action (what you specifically did) and Result (a concrete outcome, even if qualitative). Vague results like 'the product improved' will not impress senior interviewers.

Metric Trees (for success-measurement questions)
Start with the business goal, such as more site visits booked. Break it into user funnel stages: impressions, clicks, leads, site visits. Then define one north-star metric and a small number of guardrail metrics that prevent gaming. For Anarock, a guardrail could be 'qualified lead rate' to avoid inflating raw lead counts.

PRD Sketch (for 'pitch this feature' questions)
Problem statement, target user, success metric, proposed solution, risks. Keep it to a few sentences per section. Interviewers are testing whether you can separate the what from the how.

Competitive Response Framework
When asked about a competitor's feature: first ask 'does this attack our core metric or a peripheral one?', then 'can we solve the same user need differently?', then 'what is the build versus buy versus partner cost?'. This shows strategic thinking rather than a reflex to copy.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Anarock operates at the intersection of real estate services and technology, so interviewers typically look for a specific set of qualities beyond generic PM skills.

Domain curiosity. You do not need prior real estate experience, but candidates who understand how home buying works in India, including documentation, the loan process, broker roles, and RERA, consistently stand out. Even a few hours of research before the interview is visible to the interviewer.

Two-sided market thinking. Anarock's platform serves buyers and channel partners simultaneously. Interviewers want to see that you understand the incentives of both sides and can design products that balance them without hurting either.

Data-first habits. Candidates report being asked to quantify or measure almost every product decision. Practice framing answers around metrics, funnels, and trade-offs rather than gut instinct.

Communication clarity. PMs at Anarock work with internal sales teams, technology teams, and external channel partners. Interviewers want to see that you can translate user problems into clear briefs that non-technical stakeholders can understand and act on.

Ownership mindset. Be ready to speak about a product or feature you drove end to end. 'We' answers raise flags. Use 'I' when describing your specific contribution, even when the work was collaborative.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Use Anarock's product as a real user.
Spend time on Anarock's website and app. Search for a property, use the site-visit booking flow, and note where the experience feels smooth or broken. Jot down a few things you would change and why. This direct observation is far more valuable than reading about the product.

Step 2: Study the real estate market context.
Read about RERA regulations, the role of channel partners in Indian real estate, and how prop-tech companies earn revenue through listing fees, transaction commissions, and subscription tiers. This context sharpens your answers to strategy and prioritisation questions.

Step 3: Build your metric story.
For each role in your work history, be ready to state the north-star metric for your product and one specific change you made that moved it. Practise saying these out loud so they feel natural, not scripted.

Step 4: Run through the top questions.
Pick the questions from the 'Most Asked Questions' section and answer them aloud. Use the STAR or CIRCLES frameworks to structure each answer. Speaking aloud reveals gaps in your reasoning that silent reading does not.

Step 5: Prepare smart questions to ask.
Anarock interviewers typically appreciate candidates who ask about the product roadmap, the biggest user problem the team is currently solving, and how success is measured for the PM role. Avoid asking only about compensation in early rounds.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Ignoring the two-sided nature of the platform.
Candidates who design features only for home buyers and forget channel partners signal they have not done basic product research. Always ask yourself: how does this change affect the other side of the marketplace?

Giving generic PM answers.
Saying 'I would run an A/B test' without describing what you would test, on which metric, and with what hypothesis, tells the interviewer nothing. Go one layer deeper every time.

Jumping to solutions before defining the problem.
When asked to design a feature, many candidates start naming features immediately. Interviewers want to see you define the user problem first. A few clarifying questions before you start designing will make your answer noticeably stronger.

Confusing activity with impact in STAR answers.
Describing what you did, such as creating a Jira board or running weekly sprints, is not the same as describing what changed as a result. Always close with a result, even if it is qualitative, such as 'stakeholder confidence in the roadmap improved' or 'the team shipped without rollbacks for the first time that quarter'.

Underestimating domain preparation.
Real estate has specific vocabulary: carpet area versus built-up area, RERA registration, ready-to-move versus under-construction. Using these terms correctly and naturally signals genuine preparation. Stumbling on them in a prop-tech interview can hurt your credibility even if your PM fundamentals are strong.
