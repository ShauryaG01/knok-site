01 Overview

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

The following questions are drawn from candidate reports and reflect Shipsy's engineering focus on logistics, distributed systems, and carrier integrations.

  1. Walk me through how you would design a real-time shipment tracking system at scale.
  2. How do you handle race conditions when multiple services try to update the same order record simultaneously?
  3. Explain how you identified and resolved a slow database query in a production system.
  4. Describe a production incident you debugged under time pressure.
  5. How would you design the API contract between a carrier integration service and an order management service?
  6. What is your experience with message queues such as Kafka or RabbitMQ, and when would you choose one over another?
  7. How do you guarantee exactly-once delivery in an event-driven logistics pipeline?
  8. Describe your approach to writing and reviewing code for a service that other teams depend on.
  9. How would you model a database schema for shipments that can have multiple status updates and multiple carriers?
  10. Explain CAP theorem and describe how it applies to a shipment-status data store.
  11. How do you approach performance optimisation when a backend service starts missing its response-time SLA?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a production incident you debugged under time pressure.

*Situation:* At my previous employer, our order management service began dropping shipment status updates during a peak sales window.

*Task:* I was on-call and responsible for restoring normal processing without impacting active deliveries.

*Action:* I checked our monitoring dashboards and spotted consumer lag climbing on a specific Kafka topic. I pulled the consumer-group logs, read through the consumer configuration, and found the batch poll size was set far too low for the traffic spike we were seeing. I updated the config, validated it in staging, and deployed the fix within the same on-call shift.

*Result:* The backlog cleared quickly and status updates resumed flowing normally. No shipments were flagged as stuck by the carrier SLA checks. We documented the updated threshold in our runbook to prevent a repeat.

---

Q: How have you optimised a slow database query?

*Situation:* A logistics partner reported that our shipment-search API was timing out for their account, which had an unusually large shipment history.

*Task:* I needed to bring the response time within our SLA without rewriting the entire service.

*Action:* I ran EXPLAIN ANALYZE on the offending query and found a sequential scan on a large table because the filter columns (carrier_id and created_date) had no composite index. I added the index and rewrote a correlated subquery as a JOIN, then validated the execution plan again to confirm it was now using the index.

*Result:* Response time dropped from several seconds to well under a second for that partner's typical query. We applied the same index pattern to other tables with similar access patterns before they became problems.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you aligned multiple teams to ship a shared feature.

*Situation:* We needed to launch a real-time delivery-estimate widget that required coordinated work from the backend, data-science, and mobile teams at the same time.

*Task:* As the backend engineer owning the estimate API, I had to ensure all teams could build in parallel without blocking each other.

*Action:* I drafted a shared API contract early, circulated it for feedback across all teams, and built a mock endpoint so the mobile team could start integration before the actual model was ready. I ran a short regular sync specifically for this feature to catch blockers early.

*Result:* All teams shipped their parts in the same release window with no integration surprises at launch. The mock-first approach became our standard practice for cross-team features after that.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the most reliable structure. Keep the Situation brief (one or two sentences), spend most of your time on the Action (what you specifically did), and always close with an observable Result. Even if you cannot cite precise metrics, describe the before-and-after in concrete terms.

For system-design questions, candidates report that Shipsy interviewers appreciate a structured walk-through: clarify requirements and scale first, then define the data model, then describe the API layer, then discuss scalability and failure modes. Logistics systems deal with high write volumes and strict status ordering, so bring up idempotency and event ordering unprompted.

For algorithm questions, talk through your reasoning before writing code. Interviewers typically want to hear your thought process, not just a correct solution. If a logistics scenario is given (for example, finding the shortest delivery route), explicitly map it to a known graph or greedy problem before diving in.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Shipsy's real engineering challenges involve late packages, duplicate webhook events, carrier API failures, and multi-warehouse routing. Interviewers typically look for candidates who can connect computer-science fundamentals to these practical problems.

Domain curiosity. Candidates who ask about the actual logistics flow ('what triggers a status update?' or 'what happens when a carrier webhook fails?') tend to receive stronger feedback than those who treat questions as abstract puzzles.

Ownership. Expect follow-up questions like 'what would you do differently?' or 'who else did you loop in?' Interviewers want to see that you take responsibility for outcomes, not just assigned tasks.

Clear communication. Engineering at Shipsy involves regular collaboration with product and operations teams. Being able to explain a technical decision in plain language is valued across all seniority levels.

Solid backend basics. Database indexing, caching strategies, message-queue semantics, and REST API design come up consistently. Brush up on these regardless of your seniority level.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Phase 1: Core technical revision

Revise common data structures (trees, graphs, hash maps) and practise algorithm problems daily on a platform like LeetCode. Prioritise problems tagged with graphs and arrays, which candidates report seeing most frequently in Shipsy's coding rounds.

Phase 2: System design and logistics context

Read about how large e-commerce platforms handle order and shipment state machines. Practise designing systems end-to-end: one focused on real-time tracking (think WebSockets or polling, Kafka, and a status store) and one focused on a carrier integration layer (idempotency, retry logic, dead-letter queues).

Phase 3: Behavioural prep and mock interviews

Write out STAR stories from your own experience covering: a production issue you resolved, a performance improvement you drove, and a time you collaborated across teams. Practise saying them aloud so they sound natural rather than rehearsed.

On the day. Candidates report that Shipsy interviewers are collaborative rather than adversarial. If you are stuck, think out loud. Asking a clarifying question is viewed positively.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping to code without clarifying requirements. In system-design rounds especially, starting to draw architecture boxes before understanding scale or constraints wastes time and signals poor engineering judgment.

Ignoring failure modes. Logistics systems fail in specific ways: carrier timeouts, duplicate webhooks, partial shipments. Not addressing what happens when things go wrong is a gap that Shipsy interviewers consistently notice.

Vague STAR answers. Saying 'I improved performance' without describing what changed and what the outcome was is unconvincing. Describe the before-and-after in observable terms even if you cannot cite exact figures.

Over-engineering system design. Proposing an elaborate multi-region active-active architecture for a feature with modest traffic suggests you are not weighing cost and complexity tradeoffs appropriately.

Not asking questions. Candidates who engage with the interviewer, probe real constraints, and treat the session as a conversation tend to receive stronger feedback than those who treat it as a one-way exam.

How many rounds does the Shipsy Software Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates typically report a process spanning multiple rounds: an initial HR or recruiter screen, one or more technical rounds covering algorithms and system design, and a closing discussion with a hiring manager or senior engineer. The exact structure can vary by team and seniority level. Some candidates also mention a short coding exercise or pair-programming session embedded within the technical stage.

Is Shipsy's interview more focused on algorithms or system design?

Candidates report that both areas come up, but the emphasis shifts with experience level. For engineers early in their careers, algorithm and data-structure questions tend to dominate the technical rounds. For mid-level and senior candidates, system design (especially around logistics workflows, distributed consistency, and message queues) typically takes up more time.

Do I need logistics domain knowledge before interviewing at Shipsy?

You do not need deep logistics expertise going in. However, showing basic familiarity with concepts like shipment state machines, carrier integrations, and delivery SLAs will help you stand out. Candidates report that interviewers respond well when you ask about real business constraints rather than treating the questions as purely abstract engineering problems.

How long does the Shipsy hiring process take from application to offer?

Candidates report a typical timeline of a few weeks from first contact to offer, though this can vary based on team availability and how quickly rounds are scheduled. Following up politely after each round is reasonable if you have not heard back within a week.
