01 Overview

Overview

Skylark Drones is one of India's active drone technology companies, building autonomous aerial systems for enterprise, agriculture, and government sectors. As of July 2026, the company has 23 open roles, a signal that it is in an active growth phase. A Platform Engineer at Skylark Drones sits at the intersection of cloud infrastructure, edge computing, and real-time data pipelines, all in service of keeping drone operations reliable and scalable.

Candidates typically move through a recruiter screening call, a technical assessment (live or take-home), one or two in-depth technical rounds with engineers, and a final discussion with a senior team member or engineering manager. The process, as candidates report, is weighted more toward systems design and hands-on infrastructure experience than toward algorithmic puzzles.

Because drones operate at the edge of connectivity, interviewers pay close attention to how you reason about distributed systems, intermittent networks, and fault-tolerant design. The role demands both strong cloud-native skills and the ability to think through hardware-software integration challenges that are specific to a drone product company.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use these as templates. Fill in your own company name, tools, and outcomes.

Q: How would you design a real-time telemetry pipeline for a drone fleet?

*Situation:* At my previous company, IoT sensors on field equipment sent readings every few seconds, but the backend used a polling architecture that introduced significant delays before operational teams could see the data.

*Task:* I was asked to redesign the ingestion layer so the operations dashboard reflected near-live device state.

*Action:* I replaced the polling model with an MQTT broker at the edge, forwarding events into Apache Kafka topics partitioned by device ID. A stream processor enriched each message with device metadata and routed it to the right downstream consumers. I deployed all components on Kubernetes with horizontal pod autoscaling tied to consumer lag metrics, so the system could absorb sudden connection spikes without manual intervention.

*Result:* End-to-end latency dropped to a matter of seconds, and the operations team could act on field data in near real-time. The autoscaling configuration handled load spikes cleanly during peak mission hours.

---

Q: Walk us through building CI/CD for firmware updates to edge devices.

*Situation:* Our team managed edge gateways deployed across remote sites with unreliable internet connections. Firmware updates required engineers to visit each site in person, which was slow and costly.

*Task:* I was asked to build an over-the-air update system that worked reliably over low-bandwidth, intermittent links.

*Action:* I built a delta-update pipeline that computed binary diffs so only changed bytes were transmitted. I added a canary rollout stage that pushed updates to a small initial subset of devices, monitored health check endpoints for a defined observation window, and triggered automatic rollback if the error rate crossed a threshold. Update state was tracked centrally so the team always knew which version each device was running.

*Result:* Field visit frequency for routine software updates dropped substantially, and the team stopped treating OTA updates as high-risk operations requiring manual babysitting.

---

Q: Describe a time you improved observability for a production system.

*Situation:* Our on-call rotation was overwhelmed with alerts, most of which turned out to be transient and not actionable. Engineers had started ignoring pages, which created real risk for the platform.

*Task:* I was asked to reduce alert fatigue without letting genuine incidents go unnoticed.

*Action:* I introduced a layered observability stack: structured logs shipped to Loki, service-level metrics in Prometheus with Grafana dashboards, and distributed traces via Jaeger for diagnosing cross-service latency. I worked with each service owner to define SLOs, then rewrote alert rules to fire only when the error-budget burn rate sustained above a meaningful threshold for a set window, not on individual spikes.

*Result:* On-call pages dropped noticeably within the first few weeks. Engineers could pinpoint root causes in minutes using traces rather than hours of log digging, and the team regained confidence that an alert meant something real.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for experience questions

Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task brief, two or three sentences each. Spend most of your time on Action: the specific tools, decisions, and tradeoffs you made. Close with a concrete Result, even if it is qualitative rather than a precise number.

C-A-R for shorter follow-up questions

Context (one sentence on why the problem existed), Action (what you did), Result (what changed). Useful when the interviewer asks a quick follow-up and you need a tight answer without re-telling a full story.

A design structure for systems design rounds

A reliable order: clarify requirements and constraints first, then sketch the high-level components, then dive into the parts the interviewer finds most interesting. At Skylark Drones, always address connectivity gaps and fault tolerance explicitly, since drone operations depend on systems that degrade gracefully rather than fail hard.

For any design question, state your assumptions out loud. Interviewers typically want to see how you think, not just what you conclude. At a hardware-software company, naming the constraints (bandwidth, battery, latency, regulatory) before naming the tools shows maturity.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Hands-on infrastructure depth

Interviewers want evidence that you have actually operated Kubernetes clusters, written Terraform or Pulumi modules, and debugged production incidents, not just read documentation. Be specific about what you configured, what broke, and what you fixed.

Edge and distributed systems thinking

Skylarks platform runs partly on cloud servers and partly on devices in the field with no guaranteed network connection. Candidates who only think in cloud-native patterns without addressing intermittent connectivity, edge caching, or offline-first design typically score lower. Show that you understand what makes this role different from a standard DevOps or backend engineering position.

Safety and reliability awareness

Drone operations have real-world consequences if systems fail. Interviewers want to see that you treat rollback mechanisms, graceful degradation, and incident response as first-class design concerns rather than things to bolt on later.

Clear communication of tradeoffs

Strong candidates do not just describe what they built. They explain why they chose one approach over another and what they would do differently with more time or resources. Saying 'I chose MQTT over HTTP polling because it handles unreliable connections better and is natively supported on constrained devices' is more compelling than listing a stack of tool names.

STAR story preparation

Write out several STAR stories covering: a complex system you designed, a production incident you resolved, a time you improved reliability or observability, a time you automated a manual process, and a time you worked across teams on a platform change. Map each story to at least two of the question types listed above so you are not caught reaching for an example under pressure.

Mock interviews and review

Do at least two full mock technical interviews with a peer or using a practice platform. Time yourself on systems design questions and aim to stay structured without running too long. Review your answers against the frameworks above and tighten any responses that drift into tool-listing without explaining tradeoffs.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Generic cloud answers without drone context

Saying 'I would use Kubernetes to deploy the service' without addressing intermittent connectivity or edge deployment shows you have not thought about what makes this role different from a standard backend engineering position. Tie every design decision back to the constraints of a hardware-software product operating in the field.

Skipping failure modes in system design

Interviewers at hardware-software companies want to see that you think about what happens when things go wrong. If you design a telemetry pipeline without discussing what happens when the network drops or a queue fills up, you will likely be prompted. Unprompted is always better.

Overloading answers with tool names

Listing every tool in your stack without explaining why you chose each one reads as name-dropping. Interviewers respond better to a reasoned choice with a stated tradeoff than to a longer list with no rationale.

Not asking clarifying questions in design rounds

Jumping straight into a solution without clarifying scale, latency requirements, or use cases suggests you move to implementation before understanding the problem. Ask about expected device counts, data volumes, and uptime requirements before drawing your first architecture box.

Underselling results in STAR answers

Endings like 'the project went live' or 'the team was happy' leave interviewers without a sense of impact. Even without precise numbers, phrases like 'deployment time dropped from hours to minutes' or 'on-call pages fell noticeably in the first month' are far more persuasive.

How many rounds does the Skylark Drones Platform Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report a process that typically runs three to four rounds: a recruiter or HR screening call, a technical assessment (take-home or live coding), and one or two technical rounds with engineers. Some candidates also have a final conversation with an engineering manager or a senior leader. The exact structure can vary based on the seniority of the role and how the hiring team is organised at the time.

What technical skills matter most for a Platform Engineer at a drone company?

Strong Kubernetes and container orchestration experience is consistently relevant, alongside infrastructure as code tools like Terraform, CI/CD pipeline design, and cloud platforms such as AWS or GCP. For a drone company specifically, familiarity with edge computing, MQTT or similar IoT messaging protocols, and streaming systems like Kafka adds meaningful weight to your profile. Experience with over-the-air update systems or embedded Linux is a bonus that stands out from the typical applicant pool.

Is DSA (data structures and algorithms) a big part of the Platform Engineer interview at Skylark Drones?

Based on what candidates report for similar platform engineering roles, heavy DSA rounds are less common than systems design and infrastructure-focused questions. You may encounter a practical task such as writing a Terraform module, a Kubernetes manifest, or a Python automation script rather than a leetcode-style problem. Brushing up on scripting, automation, and shell skills is generally more relevant than grinding algorithm practice for this type of role.

How should I handle experience gaps, for example if I have never worked on drone-specific systems?
