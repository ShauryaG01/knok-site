02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly in Zscaler iOS interviews, based on candidate reports and the nature of their product:

  1. Explain how ARC (Automatic Reference Counting) works. Describe a retain cycle you personally debugged and the tools you used.
  2. Walk me through the NetworkExtension framework. How would you build a packet tunnel provider on iOS?
  3. How does iOS enforce App Transport Security, and when would adding an exception be justified?
  4. Describe how you would implement certificate pinning in a Swift app. What are the tradeoffs between URLSession delegation, a third-party library, and the native approach?
  5. How do you keep a network connection alive when an iOS app moves to the background? What are the OS-level constraints?
  6. You notice the Zscaler app is draining battery on a user's iPhone. How do you diagnose and fix that?
  7. How do you store sensitive tokens or certificates securely on device? Compare Keychain, Secure Enclave, and in-memory options.
  8. Explain the difference between NEPacketTunnelProvider and NEAppProxyProvider. When would you choose one over the other?
  9. Describe a time you worked with a backend or platform team to design a mobile API or SDK interface.
  10. How do you approach testing network-heavy iOS code without hitting production servers?
  11. What changes in recent iOS releases (2024 onwards) most affected enterprise or security-focused apps?
  12. How would you handle a situation where Apple's MDM restrictions on a managed device conflict with your app's expected behaviour?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Explain a retain cycle you debugged and how you fixed it.

*Situation:* In a previous role, our iOS app's memory footprint kept growing after users navigated between screens. Instruments showed a steady heap increase but no obvious leaks in simple objects.

*Task:* I needed to find the exact source of the leak and fix it without a large refactor, as we were close to a release.

*Action:* I used Instruments' Allocations and Leaks tools to trace which objects were not being deallocated. I found a closure inside a URLSession completion handler capturing 'self' strongly inside a view model, which itself held a reference back to the closure via a callback property. I replaced the strong capture with '[weak self]' and added a guard to safely unwrap it. I also audited two similar patterns in the same file.

*Result:* Memory usage dropped to a stable baseline. I added a note to our internal code review checklist to flag strong closure captures in network callbacks going forward.

---

Q: How do you keep a network connection alive when the app is in the background?

*Situation:* At a previous company, we had a feature that needed to poll a server frequently even when the app was backgrounded, to surface time-sensitive alerts to users.

*Task:* I had to implement reliable background execution within iOS's strict energy and process limits.

*Action:* I evaluated three options: Background App Refresh (too unpredictable for our interval), VoIP push (not applicable), and a combination of BGTaskScheduler plus silent push notifications from the server side. I chose the silent push path because it let the server control wake-up timing. I also used a URLSession background configuration so transfers could complete even if the process was suspended.

*Result:* Alert delivery latency dropped noticeably in testing. We shipped with a fallback to BGTaskScheduler for cases where push delivery was delayed.

---

Q: Describe a time you worked with a backend team to design a mobile API.

*Situation:* Our iOS team was building a new identity verification flow, but the backend team had designed the API around a web client model with large payloads and multiple round trips.

*Task:* I needed to push back on the API design before we were locked in, and get changes that would make the mobile experience fast on slow networks.

*Action:* I documented three specific pain points: payload size on first load, chattiness on poor connectivity, and missing error codes that forced us to parse error strings on the client. I proposed a mobile-optimised endpoint that batched the core data into one call and returned structured error enumerations. I presented this in a shared design review with a latency comparison on a network-throttled simulator.

*Result:* The backend team adopted the batched endpoint and added structured errors. First-load time dropped noticeably in internal testing, and client-side error handling became much cleaner.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For technical deep-dives (memory, networking, security): Start with the concept in one sentence, then move to how Apple implements it at the OS level, then describe where you have used it in production. Interviewers at security-focused companies want to see that you understand the 'why', not just the API surface.

For system design questions: Use this structure: constraints first (device limits, OS policies, battery), then components (client architecture, local storage, sync strategy), then tradeoffs. For Zscaler specifically, always mention security implications because the product is itself a security layer.

For behavioural questions: Use STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) and keep Situation and Task brief. Spend most of your time on Action, because that is what shows your thinking. End with a concrete Result, even if qualitative (for example, 'the team adopted the pattern going forward').

For 'what would you do' scenarios: Say what information you would gather first, name the iOS tools you would use (Instruments, Console, os_log, a network proxy), give a hypothesis, then describe how you would confirm it. This shows structured debugging, not just pattern matching.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Zscaler hires iOS Engineers who can work close to the OS, not just build screens. Based on the product and candidate reports, interviewers consistently look for a few things.

Deep iOS internals knowledge. You are expected to know how memory management, run loops, GCD, and URLSession work under the hood, not just how to call the APIs.

Security awareness by default. At a security company, mentioning certificate pinning, Keychain usage, or ATS compliance without being prompted signals that you think like a security-aware engineer.

Networking and background execution fluency. Because the Zscaler app maintains persistent tunnels and connections, candidates with experience in NetworkExtension, background sessions, or VoIP-style connections stand out.

Cross-functional communication. The iOS client connects to a complex backend. Interviewers want evidence that you can collaborate with platform, backend, and security teams, and push back on decisions that hurt the mobile experience.

Ownership mindset. Zscaler's engineering culture, based on candidate reports, rewards engineers who identify problems and drive solutions without waiting for direction. Bring examples where you spotted a problem and fixed it proactively.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: iOS fundamentals and Zscaler product context

Revise ARC, retain cycles, threading (GCD and Swift Concurrency), and URLSession internals. Separately, spend time actually using the Zscaler Client Connector app if you can access a trial or demo environment. Understanding what the product does makes your technical answers concrete rather than theoretical.

Week 2: NetworkExtension and security APIs

Read Apple's NetworkExtension documentation end to end. Build a simple packet tunnel provider in a test project. Review Keychain Services, certificate pinning with URLSession, and how iOS handles MDM-managed configurations. Also review what changed in recent iOS releases for enterprise and security-focused apps.

Week 3: System design and behavioural prep

Practice designing a mobile client for a VPN-like product: reconnection logic, offline state, token refresh, and audit logging. Prepare five to six STAR stories covering a hard debugging session, a cross-team collaboration, a performance improvement, a time you disagreed with a technical decision, and a time you learned something new quickly.

In the days before the interview: Review Zscaler's recent engineering blog posts and any public talks from their mobile team. Prepare two or three questions for the interviewer about the iOS team's current challenges and roadmap. 07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating it like a consumer app interview. Zscaler's iOS role is enterprise and network-heavy. Candidates who prepare only for UIKit layout questions or CoreData are often caught off-guard when asked about NetworkExtension or security APIs.

Vague answers on security topics. Saying 'I would use the Keychain for sensitive data' is not enough. Interviewers expect you to know which Keychain accessibility constants to use, why, and what the tradeoffs are on a shared enterprise device.

Not knowing Apple's background execution limits. Many candidates claim they can keep a connection alive in the background without knowing the actual OS constraints. Know the difference between what is possible with a VPN extension (which runs as a separate process) versus a regular app in the background.

Skipping the Result in STAR answers. Candidates often give rich Situation and Action descriptions but trail off without a result. Even a qualitative result is far better than leaving the story open-ended.

Not asking questions. With 363 open roles and multiple teams at Zscaler, asking thoughtful questions about which team you would join and what the iOS roadmap looks like shows genuine interest and helps you stand out from candidates who simply wait to be evaluated.

How many rounds does the Zscaler iOS Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates typically report three to five rounds. This usually includes a recruiter screen, one or two technical rounds covering Swift and iOS internals, a system design discussion, and a conversation with a hiring manager. The exact structure can vary by team, so it is worth asking the recruiter to outline the process before you begin.

Is the Zscaler iOS interview more about Swift syntax or system-level knowledge?

Based on candidate reports, the emphasis is clearly on system-level knowledge: memory management, networking APIs, background execution, and iOS security frameworks. Swift syntax fluency is assumed. Interviewers tend to spend more time on how the OS works and how you have applied lower-level APIs in a real production context.

Do I need prior experience with VPN or enterprise security apps?

Prior experience is helpful but not always required. What matters more is that you understand the iOS APIs relevant to networking and security, such as NetworkExtension, URLSession, Keychain, and App Transport Security. If you have not built a VPN app before, reading Apple's NetworkExtension documentation and building a small demo project before your interview is a practical way to fill that gap.
