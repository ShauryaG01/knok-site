01 Overview

Overview

GSK (GlaxoSmithKline) is a global pharmaceutical and healthcare company with a growing tech presence in India, primarily in Bangalore and Hyderabad. Software Engineers here build digital health platforms, data pipelines, enterprise applications, and tools that support clinical research, drug manufacturing, and patient outcomes. The work sits at the intersection of technology and a heavily regulated industry, so the interview reflects both strong engineering expectations and an emphasis on compliance-aware thinking.

As of July 2026, GSK has 64 open Software Engineer roles tracked on knok jobradar. The interview process typically runs three to four rounds, and candidates report it covers coding, system design, and behavioural questions, with interviewers paying particular attention to how you think about data integrity and cross-functional communication.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come from candidate reports and reflect GSK's focus on regulated environments, cross-functional collaboration, and production-grade engineering.

  1. Tell me about yourself and why you want to work at GSK specifically, rather than another technology company.
  2. How have you designed or contributed to a system that handled sensitive or regulated data? What constraints did it place on your architecture?
  3. Walk me through a production issue you debugged under pressure. What was your process from detection to resolution?
  4. How do you approach code reviews? How do you handle a situation where you strongly disagree with a reviewer's feedback?
  5. GSK operates in a compliance-heavy environment. How have you managed documentation, audit trails, or change management in previous roles?
  6. Describe a project where you worked closely with non-technical stakeholders such as scientists, analysts, or business teams. How did you keep communication clear and effective?
  7. What cloud platforms have you worked with in production, and can you walk through an architecture decision you made on cloud infrastructure?
  8. How do you design APIs that need to serve multiple teams with different use cases and varying levels of technical sophistication?
  9. Tell me about a time you had to learn a new technology quickly to meet a project deadline. How did you approach it?
  10. How do you balance delivery speed with code correctness, security, and maintainability when timelines are tight?
  11. Describe a situation where project requirements changed significantly after you had already started building. How did you adapt without losing momentum?
  12. What do you understand about data privacy regulations such as GDPR, and how have those considerations influenced your software design decisions?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time you worked with non-technical stakeholders on a technical project.

*Situation:* At my previous company, I was building a data reporting tool for the clinical operations team. The end users were research coordinators with no coding background who needed reliable daily reports on trial progress.

*Task:* My job was to deliver a dashboard they could use without training, while meeting strict data accuracy requirements that the compliance team had approved.

*Action:* I set up fortnightly demos instead of waiting for a final review, so the team could flag usability issues early. I replaced technical labels in the UI with the exact terminology the coordinators used in their day-to-day workflows. I also kept a shared feedback log so every input was tracked and addressed in the next sprint.

*Result:* The tool launched on schedule, adoption was immediate across the team, and the compliance team flagged zero data discrepancies in the first quarter of live use.

---

Q: Tell me about a production issue you debugged under pressure.

*Situation:* Our patient data ingestion pipeline stopped processing records on a Friday evening. No errors were surfaced, but data simply stopped flowing into downstream systems ahead of a Monday compliance audit.

*Task:* I was the on-call engineer and needed to identify the root cause and restore service before business hours on Monday.

*Action:* I started by reviewing the most recent deployment and found a dependency version bump from that afternoon. Tracing the logs revealed a subtle serialisation mismatch introduced by the new version. I rolled back the dependency, wrote a regression test to catch the pattern, and documented the incident with a full timeline for the audit trail.

*Result:* Service was restored within two hours. The regression test caught a similar mismatch six weeks later before it ever reached production.

---

Q: How do you handle changing requirements mid-project?

*Situation:* Midway through building an internal drug inventory tracking tool, the regulatory team introduced a new mandatory audit field that also needed to be backfilled into historical records.

*Task:* I had to add the field, safely backfill existing data, and ensure no existing integrations broke, all before a regulatory submission deadline.

*Action:* I split the work into three small pull requests: schema migration, backfill script, and API update. I tested the backfill against a staging snapshot first, then ran it in production during a low-traffic window. I communicated the rollout timeline to all dependent teams before each step so no one was surprised.

*Result:* The change was delivered one day ahead of the submission deadline with no downtime and no integration issues reported.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the most reliable structure for behavioural questions at GSK. Keep Situation and Task brief, spend most of your time on Action, and always close with a concrete Result. Do not invent numbers if you do not have them. A specific observation ('adoption was immediate', 'the audit found no issues') is more credible than a vague percentage you cannot support.

For system design questions, use a structured walkthrough: start with requirements and constraints, move to a high-level architecture, then drill into the components the interviewer shows interest in. At GSK, mention how your design handles data integrity, logging, or traceability even when not directly prompted. This signals that you understand the healthcare context without needing to be told.

For 'why GSK' questions, connect your motivation to the mission of improving patient outcomes through technology rather than offering generic praise. Reference a specific product area or digital health initiative that genuinely interests you. Candidates who have clearly read about GSK's technology strategy stand out from those who give a standard 'great company, great culture' answer.

For compliance or regulation questions, you do not need to be a regulatory expert. Show that you know how to ask the right questions, involve the right people early, and build systems with traceability in mind: structured logging, configuration version control, and clear change management steps.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Domain awareness. GSK is not a pure technology company. Interviewers want to see that you understand why reliability, auditability, and data integrity matter more in healthcare software than in a consumer application. You do not need prior pharma experience, but you need to appreciate the stakes.

Clean, readable code. Candidates report that GSK coding rounds focus on correctness and clarity over clever optimisation. Write code you would be comfortable reviewing with a colleague, handle edge cases explicitly, and add comments where the logic is not immediately obvious to a reader unfamiliar with the context.

Cross-functional communication. A consistent theme in interview feedback is the ability to explain technical decisions to non-engineers. Prepare at least one story where you translated a complex technical constraint into plain language for a business or science stakeholder and the outcome improved as a result.

Ownership and concrete results. GSK interviewers ask about outcomes, not effort. Every behavioural answer should close with something measurable or observable. 'The project went well' is not a result. 'The project shipped on time, the team adopted it immediately, and the first audit found no issues' is a result.

Mission alignment. GSK's stated purpose is to unite science, technology, and talent to beat disease. Candidates who can genuinely connect their engineering work to real-world health impact, even indirectly, tend to leave a stronger impression than those who focus purely on technical craft.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Technical Foundation

Review core data structures, algorithms, and system design concepts appropriate to your experience level. For mid and senior roles, candidates report that system design carries more weight than algorithm puzzles. Practise designing systems that prioritise reliability, data consistency, and observability. Be ready to discuss tradeoffs in the context of regulated data.

Week 2: Company and Domain Research

Read GSK's publicly available annual report and any recent press on their digital and data strategy. Understand what it means to build software in a regulated industry: audit requirements, change control, and data integrity standards. Prepare two or three specific, genuine reasons why the healthcare technology space appeals to you.

Week 3: Behavioural Preparation

Map your past work to GSK's key themes: cross-functional collaboration, handling sensitive data, debugging production systems, and adapting to changing requirements. Write out five to six STAR stories and practise each one until you can tell it clearly in under three minutes.

Week 4: Mock Interviews and Refinement

Do at least two full mock interviews, one technical and one behavioural. Record yourself if possible. Check whether your answers are concise, whether Results are concrete, and whether you naturally connect your work to business or health impact. Close any obvious gaps before your actual rounds.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Ignoring the healthcare context. Candidates who treat GSK like a standard tech interview and never acknowledge data integrity, compliance, or patient impact often miss the mark. Even a brief 'in a regulated environment I would also consider...' signals that you understand the business you are joining.

Over-engineering system design answers. Interviewers report that candidates sometimes propose unnecessarily complex architectures to appear impressive. Start with the simplest design that meets the requirements, justify added complexity only when a specific constraint demands it, and always walk through the tradeoffs aloud.

Vague Results in STAR answers. 'The project went well' or 'the team was happy' are not results. Tie your outcomes to a metric, a deadline met, a stakeholder response, or a measurable change in system behaviour. Even qualitative results ('the compliance team found no issues in the next audit') are stronger than nothing.

Underestimating behavioural rounds. Some candidates prepare intensively for coding and treat behavioural questions as an afterthought. At GSK, your ability to communicate, collaborate, and take ownership carries significant weight. Prepare your STAR stories as carefully as you would a system design problem.

Weak closing questions. Candidates report that interviewers notice whether you ask thoughtful questions at the end. Prepare two or three questions about the team's current technical challenges, their approach to compliance in the development lifecycle, or how they measure engineering quality.

Raising compensation too early. In Indian hiring, discussing salary before receiving a clear signal of intent can create an awkward dynamic. Let the recruiter raise compensation first, or wait until after you have received a positive signal from the final round.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the GSK Software Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates typically report three to four rounds in total. This usually includes an online coding assessment or take-home task, one or two technical rounds covering coding and system design, and a behavioural or hiring manager round. The exact structure varies by team and seniority level, so confirm the process with your recruiter after your application is acknowledged.

Does GSK ask about pharma or healthcare domain knowledge in tech interviews?

You are not expected to have prior pharma experience. However, candidates who demonstrate awareness of why regulated environments place extra demands on data integrity, audit trails, and change management tend to perform better. Showing that you can think about traceability and compliance as part of your engineering approach matters more than knowing specific industry regulations.

What coding languages and stacks does GSK look for?

Based on active job descriptions, GSK India tech roles commonly list Python, Java, and cloud platforms such as AWS or Azure. Some roles focus on data engineering, others on backend services or full-stack internal tooling. Read the specific job description carefully because the required stack varies by team. Strong fundamentals and the ability to learn quickly matter more than matching every listed technology.

How long does the GSK hiring process take from application to offer?

Candidates report the process from first interview to offer can range from two to six weeks depending on the team and interview slot availability. Following up politely with the recruiter after each round is standard practice and generally viewed positively. If you have a competing offer with a hard deadline, mention it to the recruiter early rather than waiting until the last moment.

What salary can I expect as a Software Engineer at GSK India?

Based on knok jobradar data, Software Engineer salaries in India broadly follow market bands: 6-12 LPA for entry-level roles (0-2 years), 15-25 LPA for mid-level (3-5 years), and 28-45 LPA for senior roles (6-9 years). Actual offers depend on the specific team, your experience, and negotiation. Glassdoor and levels.fyi carry additional self-reported compensation data points for GSK India specifically.

Is it worth applying to multiple GSK Software Engineer roles at the same time?

Yes, if you genuinely meet the requirements for more than one role. GSK currently has 64 open Software Engineer positions across different teams and experience levels. Applying to two or three well-matched roles increases your chances without signalling a lack of focus. Make sure each application addresses the specific requirements of that role rather than sending an identical resume to every opening.
