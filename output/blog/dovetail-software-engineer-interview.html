01 Overview

Overview

Dovetail builds research management software that helps product teams capture, organize, and share user insights. It is a product-led, design-conscious company where engineers work closely with designers and researchers every day.

As of mid-2026, Dovetail has 7 open Software Engineer roles. Candidates report a process that typically runs 3-4 stages: a recruiter or people-team screen, a technical assessment (take-home or live coding), a system design or architecture discussion, and a final round with engineering leadership or a hiring manager. The process is often async-friendly, with written communication carrying more weight than at many other companies.

Dovetail's product is built around empathy for users, and they expect that mindset from engineers too. Technical strength matters, but so does the ability to explain the 'why' behind decisions, write clearly, and collaborate across design and product.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up frequently in Dovetail Software Engineer interviews, based on candidate reports. Prepare a concrete story or answer for each.

  1. Walk us through a feature you built from scratch. How did you decide on the right solution?
  2. How do you approach performance in a React or TypeScript-heavy application?
  3. Dovetail's product handles qualitative, user-defined data. How do you think about data modeling for flexible or unstructured content?
  4. Describe a time you worked closely with a designer or product manager to shape a technical decision.
  5. How do you handle async collaboration with teammates across different time zones?
  6. Tell us about a complex state management problem you solved. What trade-offs did you consider?
  7. How do you approach testing: what do you test, what do you skip, and why?
  8. Describe your experience with GraphQL or API design. What makes a good API from an engineer's perspective?
  9. Tell us about a time you disagreed with a team decision. What did you do?
  10. How do you balance shipping fast with keeping the codebase maintainable?
  11. Describe a time you had to onboard quickly to an unfamiliar codebase or domain.
  12. How do you give feedback in code reviews without damaging team relationships?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through a feature you built from scratch.

*Situation:* Our product team noticed users were losing context when switching between multiple research projects. Session data showed many users leaving the app mid-session.

*Task:* I was responsible for designing and building a 'recently viewed' sidebar that would let users return to relevant content quickly.

*Action:* I started by talking to our designer and a few power users to understand what 'recently viewed' meant in this context. I chose to model the data client-side using a lightweight cache stored in localStorage, with a backend sync endpoint for cross-device consistency. I wrote the API contract first, reviewed it with the backend lead, then built the React component in TypeScript. I added integration tests for the sync logic and wrote a short internal doc on the trade-offs I made.

*Result:* The feature shipped in two sprints. The internal doc became a template for how we document new features going forward.

---

Q: Describe a time you disagreed with a team decision.

*Situation:* During a backend migration, the team proposed switching from REST to GraphQL for all internal APIs at once.

*Task:* I believed a gradual, endpoint-by-endpoint migration was safer given our release schedule and existing integrations.

*Action:* I wrote a short document comparing the big-bang approach with an incremental plan, covering risks and trade-offs honestly. I asked a senior engineer from another team to review my thinking before the planning meeting, then presented both options rather than just arguing for my preference.

*Result:* The team agreed to a two-phase approach. During phase one, we caught two integration issues that would have been much harder to debug in a full migration. The team later cited this as a good example of raising concerns constructively.

---

Q: How do you handle async collaboration across time zones?

*Situation:* I joined a team where frontend and backend engineers were split across two time zones with a significant gap between them.

*Task:* I needed to deliver a data export feature that required close coordination between both sides.

*Action:* I mapped dependencies early and wrote a shared spec document covering the API contract, expected data shapes, and edge cases. I posted structured daily updates in our project thread so nobody had to wait for a live sync. When a blocker came up on the backend side, I had prepared mock data the frontend could work against, keeping both sides unblocked.

*Result:* The feature shipped without a single cross-timezone meeting. Two other team members later adopted the spec document approach for their own work.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioral questions. Use Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep each part brief. Dovetail values clarity, so a focused 2-3 minute answer lands better than a long one.

Lead with 'why' for technical questions. Before explaining what you built, explain why you made the choices you did. Interviewers want to see your reasoning, not just the solution.

Clarify before designing. For system design questions, spend the first few minutes asking about requirements and constraints. Dovetail's product involves flexible, user-defined data structures, so thinking out loud about trade-offs in data modeling will resonate with interviewers.

Complete every trade-off. When you say 'it depends', always follow with 'and here is what it depends on.' Name the two options, state what you gain and give up with each, and say which context you would choose and why. An unfinished trade-off is one of the most common reasons candidates do not move forward.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Product empathy. Dovetail's product is built for people who study users, so they want engineers who think about the person using a feature, not just the code powering it. Be ready to talk about decisions you made because of user feedback or usage data.

Clear written communication. Dovetail is async-first and distributed. Interviewers often evaluate how well you explain technical decisions in writing, not only in live conversation. A well-structured PR description or trade-off doc matters as much as the code itself.

Ownership without ego. Candidates who take responsibility for their work but receive feedback without getting defensive tend to do well. Code review culture is strong here, and they expect engineers to engage with it constructively.

Comfort with ambiguity. Early-stage features rarely have fully defined requirements. Candidates who ask good clarifying questions and propose small experiments over large upfront designs signal the right mindset.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Understand the product first. Spend a few hours using Dovetail's product before any interview round. Note what feels intuitive, what feels clunky, and what engineering challenges you imagine behind the scenes. This gives you genuine material for product-empathy questions and signals real interest.

Refresh core technical skills. Practice TypeScript, React patterns, and API design across both REST and GraphQL. Review system design fundamentals, especially around modeling flexible or user-defined data structures, as this is directly relevant to Dovetail's domain.

Prepare 5-6 STAR stories. Cover: a feature you built end-to-end, a conflict you navigated, a technical trade-off you made, a time you improved a process, and a time you worked closely with a designer or product manager.

Read about Dovetail's engineering culture. Look for any public writing from their team. Review the job description line by line and prepare one concrete example for each key requirement listed.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

  1. Treating it like a pure algorithm interview. Dovetail is not known for heavy LeetCode-style rounds. Candidates who over-prepare on competitive programming and under-prepare on system design and behavioral questions are often caught off-guard.
  1. Not using the product before the interview. Walking in without having used Dovetail reads as low interest. A few hours of hands-on experience gives you material for multiple questions and shows you take the role seriously.
  1. Vague trade-off answers. Saying 'it depends' without completing the thought signals shallow thinking. Always name the options, state what you gain or lose with each, and explain your choice.
  1. Skipping the 'why' in technical answers. Jumping straight to a solution without explaining your reasoning misses what Dovetail interviewers are actually evaluating. Briefly set context before diving in.
  1. Underestimating async communication skills. Many candidates focus only on coding rounds and come unprepared for questions about distributed, async collaboration. Prepare at least one clear, specific example.

How many rounds does the Dovetail Software Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates typically report 3-4 rounds. This usually includes a recruiter or people-team screen, a technical assessment (take-home or live coding), a system design or technical deep-dive, and a final round with a hiring manager or senior engineer. The exact structure varies by team and role level, so confirm the format with your recruiter at the start of the process.

What tech stack does Dovetail use?

Candidates report that Dovetail's frontend is primarily React and TypeScript, with GraphQL for API communication and a Node.js backend. The exact stack can vary by team, so ask your recruiter which area the role focuses on. Preparing on TypeScript, React, and GraphQL covers a strong base for most Software Engineer roles at Dovetail.

Does Dovetail give a take-home assignment?

Many candidates report receiving a take-home coding task as part of the process, though this varies by role and level. The task is typically scoped to a few hours and involves building a small feature or solving a design problem. Treat the take-home as a chance to show your writing and documentation skills alongside code quality, not just a speed exercise.
