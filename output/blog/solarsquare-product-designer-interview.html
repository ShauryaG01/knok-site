01 Overview

Overview

SolarSquare is one of India's leading rooftop solar companies, helping homeowners and businesses shift to clean energy. A Product Designer here works on flows that guide users through a significant purchase decision: understanding solar savings, requesting a quote, tracking installation, and managing their system post-install. The work combines consumer UX with real complexity around technical data, financing options, and post-sale service.

The interview process typically runs 2-4 rounds. Candidates report a portfolio walkthrough in an early round, followed by a design challenge or live case study, and later rounds with PMs or engineers. Interviewers tend to probe how you handle constraints specific to this market: low digital literacy, first-time-buyer anxiety, and users spread across Tier 2 and Tier 3 cities.

Salary bands for Product Designers, based on knok jobradar data as of July 2026, are 6-12 LPA at entry level (0-2 years), 14-24 LPA at mid level (3-5 years), 26-40 LPA at senior level (6-9 years), and 36-55+ LPA at Lead or Principal level. Of 393 Product Designer openings tracked across India, Bangalore leads with 62 roles, followed by Delhi (33), Mumbai (13), Chennai (4), Pune (4), and Hyderabad (3).

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

The following questions are based on what candidates report being asked in SolarSquare Product Designer interviews:

  1. Walk us through a project from your portfolio where you solved a real user problem. What was the problem, how did you approach the solution, and how did you know it worked?
  1. How would you design the onboarding experience for a homeowner considering rooftop solar for the first time? What steps would you include and why?
  1. SolarSquare's users span metros and Tier 2 cities, and many may not be very app-savvy. How do you approach designing for users with low digital literacy?
  1. Tell me about a time when user research changed the direction of a design you were already working on.
  1. How do you balance a business goal, like pushing users to complete a quote request quickly, against user comfort, like giving them time to understand the product?
  1. Describe a time you presented a design to stakeholders who disagreed with your approach. What happened and how did you resolve it?
  1. How would you design a dashboard for a solar system owner to track their energy production and monthly savings?
  1. Tell me about a complex technical concept you had to turn into a simple, clear UI. What choices did you make?
  1. How do you work with engineers to make sure your designs are implemented correctly? Give a specific example where this was difficult.
  1. You shipped a feature and the numbers are not looking good. How do you investigate what went wrong?
  1. How do you prioritize when you have several features to design but bandwidth for only one?
  1. What does 'good design' mean in the context of a sustainability-focused product like solar energy?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a time when user research changed the direction of a design.

*Situation:* I was designing a lead-capture form for a solar installation inquiry flow. My initial version asked for detailed roof size and electricity bill data upfront, because the sales team said they needed it to qualify leads.

*Task:* My job was to create a form that captured useful data and actually got users to complete and submit it.

*Action:* I ran several quick user interviews with homeowners in our target segment. Almost all of them said the technical questions upfront made them feel unqualified or suspicious. I redesigned the form to ask only for name, city, and average monthly electricity bill on step one, moving the detailed questions to a follow-up call with the sales rep. I also added a short line below each field explaining why the information was needed.

*Result:* In a moderated usability test, task completion improved and users reported feeling more at ease. The sales team confirmed they could gather the technical details during the follow-up call without losing qualified leads. The redesign was shipped within the same sprint.

---

Q: Describe a time you had to turn a complex technical concept into a simple, clear UI.

*Situation:* I was working on a post-installation dashboard where users could see how much electricity their solar panels were generating versus what they were drawing from the grid.

*Task:* The underlying data was complex: real-time generation, net metering credits, units saved, and projected annual savings. I needed to make this instantly understandable for a homeowner checking the app on their phone in the morning.

*Action:* I listed every data point the engineering team wanted to show, then interviewed several recent solar customers and asked: 'What one thing do you want to know when you open this app in the morning?' Almost everyone said something like: 'Am I saving money today?' I reorganized the entire dashboard around that question, leading with a large 'units saved today' figure and moving the detailed breakdown behind a 'See full report' tap. I also replaced abbreviations like kWh with the plain word 'units' throughout.

*Result:* In our internal review, stakeholders approved the design in the first round without revision requests. The engineering team said it was the clearest spec handoff they had received for a data-heavy screen.

---

Q: How do you handle a situation where business goals and user needs are in conflict?

*Situation:* The growth team wanted a hard paywall pop-up after a user viewed their solar savings estimate, requiring them to share contact details before they could see the results. User interviews showed this created distrust at the most critical moment in the funnel.

*Task:* I needed to find a design that served the business goal of lead capture without breaking user trust at a moment when they were most likely to convert.

*Action:* I proposed a middle path: show the savings estimate in full, then place a soft contextual prompt directly below it saying 'Want us to send this estimate to your email?' with a single email field. I presented the business team with a side-by-side flow comparison and the user research behind my recommendation. I also referenced publicly reported industry patterns on forced-gate conversion rates to support my case.

*Result:* The business team agreed to test my version. The softer prompt maintained trust at that critical point in the flow while still capturing contact details from users who were genuinely interested. The product lead cited it as a good example of designing for both the user and the business at the same time.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For portfolio walkthroughs, use a 'Problem, Process, Decision, Outcome' spine. Spend the most time on the decisions you made and why, not on showing every screen you produced. Interviewers want to see how you think, not just what you shipped.

For design challenge rounds, candidates report that SolarSquare typically gives a scenario tied to their actual product. Start by asking clarifying questions about the user, platform, and constraints before sketching anything. Structure your answer as: define the user and the problem, outline 2-3 possible approaches, pick one and justify it, sketch the key screens, then state what you would measure to know if it worked.

For 'tell me about a time' questions, use the STAR format: Situation (brief context), Task (what you were responsible for), Action (specifically what you did), Result (what happened and what you learned). Keep Situation and Task short. Action and Result are where you earn the most credit.

For metrics questions, anchor your answer to specific user behaviors (form completion rate, drop-off point, time on task) and business outcomes (lead conversion, repeat sessions, support tickets raised). Avoid vague claims like 'user satisfaction improved' without any supporting detail.

For 'how would you design X' questions, always start with the user: who they are, what they already know, and what they are anxious about. Talk through the core flow before jumping into visual details. Interviewers want to see that you design from insight, not from habit.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Clarity of thinking over visual polish. SolarSquare interviewers typically care more about how you explain a decision than how refined your Figma screens look. They want to hear you articulate why you made a choice, including what you considered and ruled out.

Comfort with a complex user base. Solar customers include retirees in Tier 2 cities, young apartment owners, and small business owners. Candidates who show genuine awareness of this range, and who design with it actively in mind, tend to stand out.

Ability to work within real constraints. Solar products involve technical, regulatory, and trust-related limits. Interviewers want to see that you can design creatively inside those constraints rather than ignoring them or treating them as someone else's problem.

Cross-functional fluency. Product Designers at SolarSquare work closely with PMs, engineers, and the sales team. Candidates who can show examples of navigating disagreement or building shared understanding across functions tend to do well in later rounds.

Ownership and follow-through. Interviewers look for examples where you stayed involved after the design was handed off: watching metrics, flagging implementation gaps, and iterating based on real-world data, not just what the spec said.

Mission awareness. SolarSquare is building something they genuinely believe in. Candidates who can connect their design work to the goal of making solar accessible to Indian homeowners, without being generic about it, often leave a stronger impression than those who treat it as a purely technical exercise.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know the product. Download and use the SolarSquare app as a first-time visitor would. Map out the main flows: getting a quote, understanding savings, post-installation tracking. Note where the experience feels smooth and where it feels rough. This gives you specific, genuine material when interviewers ask 'how would you improve our product?'

Week 1: Sharpen your portfolio. Pick 2-3 projects to discuss in depth. For each, prepare a crisp walkthrough covering the user problem, the constraints you worked within, the key design decision you made, and what you measured or learned. Cut anything that does not serve that story.

Week 2: Practice design challenges. Work through at least two timed design challenges using prompts related to consumer products aimed at first-time buyers. Force yourself to articulate your decisions out loud as you sketch. Record yourself once to hear how clearly you explain your thinking under time pressure.

Week 2: Prepare STAR stories. Write out answers for five common themes: user research changing your direction, conflict with stakeholders, designing under constraints, working with engineers, and measuring design outcomes. You do not need to memorize these word for word, but having the structure ready stops you from rambling under interview pressure.

Before the interview. Research SolarSquare's business model: how they acquire customers, who their typical buyer is, and what publicly reported milestones or expansions they have announced. Candidates who demonstrate business context alongside design skill tend to progress further in the process. 07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Spending too long on context. Many candidates spend most of their portfolio walkthrough describing the company background and product before getting to their own work. Interviewers already have context. Get to your decisions and trade-offs quickly.

Designing for a metro urban power user. When given an open-ended design challenge, candidates often default to a feature-rich interface suited to a tech-savvy city dweller. For SolarSquare, always ask about the user profile first and consider users who may be less digitally confident.

Jumping to solutions before defining the problem. In live design rounds, rushing into wireframes before clarifying the user, platform, and constraints is a common signal of inexperience. Take a moment to frame the problem before you sketch. Interviewers notice when you skip this step.

Vague impact claims. Saying 'the design improved the user experience' without any specific outcome, metric, or user feedback weakens your answer. Even qualitative results carry more weight than a general statement.

Not asking clarifying questions. Candidates who accept every brief at face value and never ask about constraints, user segments, or success metrics miss a key opportunity to show critical thinking. Good designers ask before they sketch.

Ignoring the mission context. SolarSquare is not a generic consumer app company. Candidates who treat the interview as a standard product design round, without acknowledging the trust and education challenge involved in selling solar to Indian homeowners, can come across as underprepared or uninterested in the company specifically.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the SolarSquare Product Designer interview typically have?

Candidates report a process that typically runs 2-4 rounds. This usually includes a portfolio review, a design challenge or take-home assignment, and one or more cross-functional discussions with PMs, engineers, or senior design leadership. The exact number can vary by team and seniority level, so it is worth asking your recruiter at the start of the process.

Is there a design assignment or take-home task?

Candidates commonly report a design challenge as part of the process, either as a take-home or a live exercise during the interview. These tend to be grounded in real product scenarios rather than abstract puzzles. You are typically expected to show your thinking process, not just a finished design, so documenting your decisions and trade-offs clearly matters more than visual polish.

What salary can a Product Designer expect at SolarSquare?

Based on knok jobradar data, Product Designer salary bands in India are 6-12 LPA at entry level (0-2 years), 14-24 LPA at mid level (3-5 years), 26-40 LPA at senior level (6-9 years), and 36-55+ LPA at Lead or Principal level. Actual offers depend on your portfolio strength, interview performance, and the specific scope of the role. It is worth confirming the range with your recruiter early in the process so there are no surprises at the offer stage.

Does SolarSquare hire Product Designers in specific cities, or is the role remote-friendly?

Based on knok jobradar data, Product Designer openings at SolarSquare are primarily in Bangalore, Delhi, Mumbai, Chennai, Pune, and Hyderabad. Remote and hybrid arrangements vary by role and team. It is worth clarifying the work model with your recruiter when you first connect, as this can differ between product teams within the same company.

What tools and skills should I highlight in my SolarSquare interview?

Proficiency in Figma is typically expected for Product Designer roles today. Beyond tools, SolarSquare interviewers tend to care about your research skills, your ability to simplify complex information for non-expert users, and your comfort working across functions with PMs and engineers. Highlighting experience with consumer-facing products, onboarding flows, or data dashboards is particularly relevant to SolarSquare's product surface.

How should I talk about metrics if I don't have exact numbers from past projects?

You do not always need precise numbers to give a strong answer. Qualitative data counts: explaining that most users in a usability test completed a task without assistance is a valid and credible result. If you genuinely do not have metrics, explain what you would have measured and what you wish you had tracked. Honesty about data gaps, paired with clear thinking about what measurement would look like, tends to land better than invented figures.
