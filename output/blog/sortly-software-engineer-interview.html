01 Overview

Overview

Sortly builds inventory and asset-tracking software used by small businesses, field crews, and growing enterprises. With 2 open Software Engineer roles as of mid-2026, the company keeps a lean but focused engineering team. Candidates typically report a process spanning a coding screen, one or two technical rounds, and a final conversation with a hiring manager or team lead. The interviews lean toward practical, product-aware engineering: expect questions on API design, query optimisation, and how you collaborate cross-functionally, rather than algorithmic puzzles for their own sake.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time you improved the performance of a slow database query.

*Situation:* Our product catalogue page was timing out for merchants with large inventories.

*Task:* I was asked to diagnose and fix the slowdown without changing the API contract the frontend relied on.

*Action:* I enabled query logging and found a classic N+1 pattern: the code fetched each item's category in a separate database round trip. I rewrote it to use a single JOIN, added a composite index on the relevant columns, and introduced short-lived caching for category metadata that rarely changed.

*Result:* Page load times dropped noticeably (tracked internally), the timeout tickets stopped, and the fix shipped within the same sprint with no API changes needed.

---

Q: Tell me about a feature you built from spec to deployment.

*Situation:* Our team needed a bulk-import feature so users could upload a CSV and populate their inventory without manual entry.

*Task:* I owned the feature end-to-end: backend parser, validation layer, async processing queue, and a status-polling endpoint for the frontend.

*Action:* I started by mapping edge cases with the product manager: duplicate SKUs, missing required fields, encoding issues. I built the parser to return row-level errors rather than failing the whole file, used a background job for large uploads, and wrote integration tests against real CSV samples the support team provided.

*Result:* The feature launched on schedule, support tickets about manual data entry dropped sharply per the team's internal tracker, and enterprise prospects cited it in their onboarding calls.

---

Q: Describe a time a release you owned caused an incident.

*Situation:* I deployed a migration that altered a heavily used index, which caused query plans to change under production load.

*Task:* I was on call and needed to restore service quickly while protecting data integrity.

*Action:* I rolled back the migration within minutes of the alert, wrote a post-mortem, and scheduled a follow-up deploy in a maintenance window with a dry-run on a production replica first.

*Result:* Downtime was limited to a short window. The revised process of running replica dry-runs before schema changes was adopted by the wider team.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions: Structure every story as Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep the Situation brief (one or two sentences), spend most of your time on Action, and quantify the Result if you can, even with relative terms ('halved', 'reduced significantly', 'eliminated') when exact figures are not publicly reported.

For system-design questions: Candidates report that Sortly interviewers typically want you to clarify requirements before jumping to diagrams. Walk through: (1) clarify scope and scale, (2) define the API contract, (3) pick a data model and justify it, (4) discuss tradeoffs (consistency vs availability, SQL vs NoSQL, sync vs async). Thinking out loud earns more credit than arriving at a 'correct' answer silently.

For debugging or optimisation questions: Follow a structured approach: reproduce the problem, isolate the layer (network, query, application logic, cache), form a hypothesis, test it, fix, verify. Interviewers want to see methodical thinking, not trial and error.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Candidates who have interviewed at Sortly commonly describe interviewers as product-aware: they care whether you understand why a feature matters to a user, not just whether you can code it. Three traits come up repeatedly in candidate reports.

Product curiosity. Engineers at Sortly typically own features end-to-end, so interviewers look for people who ask 'what problem does this solve for the user?' before writing code.

Practical engineering. Expect questions grounded in real inventory-management scenarios (multi-user edits, large data sets, CSV imports). Interviewers want clean, maintainable solutions over over-engineered ones.

Clear communication. Because the team is lean, every engineer collaborates closely with product and design. Candidates who think out loud, ask clarifying questions, and flag tradeoffs explicitly tend to do well.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Understand the product.
Sign up for Sortly's free trial and use it as a real user would. Note the core workflows: adding items, moving stock, generating reports, sharing access. This gives you vocabulary and context for design questions, and interviewers notice when you reference actual product behaviour.

Sharpen your coding fundamentals.
Practise array manipulation, hash maps, trees, and sorting on a platform like LeetCode or HackerRank. Focus on medium-difficulty problems. Sortly candidates report the coding screen is practical rather than competitive-programming-hard.

System design practice.
Work through two or three inventory-relevant design problems: a check-in/check-out API, a bulk-upload pipeline, a multi-tenant permission system. Use a clear structure (requirements, API, data model, tradeoffs) and set a firm time limit for each exercise so you practise pacing.

Prepare behavioural stories.
Write out STAR stories for: a performance fix, a feature you owned end-to-end, a disagreement with a colleague or product manager, and an incident you handled. Practise saying them aloud, not just writing them down.

Before the interview.
Research Sortly's recent product updates, read the job description carefully, and prepare two or three specific questions about the team's technical stack or current engineering challenges.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping to code without clarifying requirements. Candidates who start typing before asking scope questions lose marks quickly. Always clarify scale, edge cases, and constraints first.

Ignoring the product context. Generic answers ('I would use a queue') land poorly when the interviewer is framing the problem around Sortly's actual use case (inventory sync, multi-user access). Reference the product.

Over-engineering the design. Proposing a microservices mesh for a feature a small SaaS team maintains signals poor judgement. Match complexity to scale.

Vague behavioural answers. Saying 'I improved performance' without a concrete action and outcome wastes your chance. Specifics win, even if the numbers are relative ('query time dropped by half').

Not asking questions at the end. Sortly interviewers typically expect candidates to be curious about the team, the product roadmap, and the engineering culture. Arriving with no questions signals low interest.

Candidates report a process of roughly three to four conversations. Sortly's job listings as of 2026 commonly mention full-stack or backend roles. Some candidates report receiving a short take-home coding task before or instead of a live coding screen. Others go straight to a live round. The format has varied across roles, so ask your recruiter early so you can prepare accordingly and manage your time.
