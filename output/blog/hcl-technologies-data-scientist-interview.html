02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions are drawn from what candidates report across HCL Data Scientist interview rounds, covering both technical and behavioural areas.

  1. Walk me through a data science project you completed end-to-end, from problem statement to deployment.
  2. What is the difference between bagging and boosting? When would you choose one over the other?
  3. How do you handle missing data in a large dataset? What are the trade-offs between different imputation approaches?
  4. How do you deal with class imbalance in a classification problem?
  5. Explain the bias-variance trade-off in simple terms. How does it guide your model selection?
  6. HCL deploys solutions in enterprise and legacy environments. How would you approach putting a machine learning model into production for a client with older IT infrastructure?
  7. A model you built performed well in testing but is underperforming in production. How do you diagnose and fix it?
  8. How would you build a customer churn prediction model for a telecom or banking client? Walk through your approach.
  9. What metrics would you use to evaluate a recommendation system, and how do you decide which matters most?
  10. Describe a situation where you had to explain a complex model output to a non-technical business stakeholder. How did you handle it?
  11. HCL values reusable, maintainable solutions. How do you make sure your data science code and pipelines can be picked up by another team member?
  12. How do you keep your skills current given how fast the data science field is changing?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format (Situation, Task, Action, Result) for behavioural and scenario-based questions. Here are three examples you can adapt with your own project details.

---

Q: Walk me through a data science project you completed end-to-end.

*Situation:* My previous team was losing clients due to poor after-sales service, but no one knew which customers were at risk until they had already decided to leave.

*Task:* I was asked to build a system that could flag high-risk customers well before their contract renewal so the account team could intervene proactively.

*Action:* I pulled over a year of CRM and support ticket data, cleaned it, and engineered features around ticket frequency, resolution time, and product usage patterns. I tested logistic regression, random forest, and XGBoost, then selected XGBoost after cross-validation. I also built a simple dashboard so the account management team could view scores without querying a database directly.

*Result:* The model flagged at-risk customers at a precision the account team found actionable for prioritisation. They used it for several months and reported a noticeable improvement in proactive outreach. I handed it off with full documentation so the next data scientist could maintain it without needing to ask me questions.

---

Q: A model you built performed well in testing but underperformed in production. How did you diagnose and fix it?

*Situation:* A fraud detection model I built for an internal project was hitting strong offline accuracy but generating too many false positives once it went live.

*Task:* I needed to identify the root cause quickly because the operations team was spending too much time on manual reviews.

*Action:* I compared feature distributions in training data versus live data and found that one key feature (transaction amount bins) had shifted significantly. This was a classic case of data drift. I retrained the model on a rolling window of recent data, added a monitoring pipeline to detect future drift early, and adjusted the decision threshold to reduce false positives without hurting recall much.

*Result:* False positives dropped substantially within two weeks of the fix. The monitoring pipeline then caught further drift events in the months that followed before they could affect performance again.

---

Q: Describe a time you explained a complex model to a non-technical stakeholder.

*Situation:* I built a demand forecasting model for a retail client engagement. The client's business head questioned why the model was predicting lower demand for one product category, which went against his intuition.

*Task:* I needed to explain the model's reasoning clearly enough that he could either trust it or flag a genuine data problem.

*Action:* Instead of showing accuracy metrics, I pulled the top features driving that prediction, specifically a steady multi-month decline in repeat purchase rate for that category, and presented it as a simple line chart. I used an analogy: 'Think of the model as reading your past customers' buying habits, and right now those habits are showing they purchase this category less often.'

*Result:* He accepted the forecast and asked his category team to investigate. They found a competitor had launched a cheaper alternative. The model was correct, and he became a strong advocate for data-led forecasts on the account.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For technical concept questions (for example, bias-variance trade-off or bagging vs boosting):
Use a three-part structure: define the concept in one sentence, give a concrete example from your own work, then state the trade-off or limitation. HCL interviewers typically want to see that you know when to use something, not just what it is.

For case study or 'build a model' questions:
Walk through five steps: (1) understand the business problem and success metric, (2) describe what data you need and where it might come from, (3) explain your feature engineering thinking, (4) describe model selection and evaluation, (5) mention deployment and monitoring. Candidates who skip step 1 or step 5 often score lower, candidates report.

For behavioural questions:
Use STAR: Situation (context in 1-2 sentences), Task (what you were specifically responsible for), Action (what you did, not 'we'), Result (a concrete outcome, even if qualitative). Keep the full answer under 3 minutes.

For coding or SQL questions:
Think out loud before you write any code. State your approach, mention edge cases you are considering, and narrate as you work. HCL interviewers typically value clear thinking over a perfect first attempt.

For questions about HCL specifically:
Scan HCL's publicly reported focus areas (AI, cloud, digital engineering) before your interview. Connecting your experience to a domain they are visibly investing in signals genuine interest rather than a generic application.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Strong fundamentals, not just tool familiarity. HCL interviewers commonly look for candidates who can explain why they chose a model or technique, not just name it. Saying 'I used XGBoost because it handles missing values well and tends to perform on tabular data with these characteristics' is stronger than 'I used XGBoost because it gave the best accuracy.'

Client-facing communication skills. HCL is an IT services company, so many Data Scientists work directly with external clients. Interviewers pay attention to how clearly you explain things and whether you can translate technical results into business language.

End-to-end ownership. Candidates who can speak to the full lifecycle, from raw data to a model running in production, stand out. If you have only done analysis or only built models without deploying them, be honest but explain what you understand about the steps you have not personally handled.

Practical problem-solving over textbook answers. HCL projects often involve messy real-world data, legacy systems, and client timelines. Showing that you can adapt to constraints is more useful than describing the academically ideal approach.

Team habits and documentation. Candidates report that HCL places real weight on whether you write maintainable code and document your work. Teams frequently hand off models to other engineers or to client-side teams, so this matters well beyond the initial build.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Strengthen your core fundamentals
Revise statistics topics you find shaky: probability, distributions, hypothesis testing, and A/B testing basics. Review the machine learning algorithms you have used and be ready to explain the intuition behind each one without a whiteboard. Practise talking through concepts out loud, as if explaining to a junior colleague.

Week 2: Coding and SQL practice
Solve data manipulation problems in Python (pandas, NumPy) and write SQL queries covering joins, window functions, and aggregations. Technical rounds at HCL typically include at least one hands-on data task. You do not need to be a competitive programmer. Comfort with data wrangling under mild time pressure is what matters most.

Week 3: Build your STAR stories
Write out 4-5 work stories covering: an end-to-end project, a time you handled messy or incomplete data, a model that failed or underperformed, a time you explained something technical to a non-technical person, and a collaboration or cross-team story. Practise delivering each in under 3 minutes.

Week 4: HCL-specific preparation
Look at HCL's publicly reported priorities in AI and digital engineering. Think about which of your past projects maps naturally to an enterprise services context. Prepare 2-3 thoughtful questions to ask your interviewer about the team and the kind of client work involved. This signals genuine interest.

On the day: Candidates report that HCL interviewers respond well to structured, calm answers. If you do not know something, say 'I have not worked with that directly, but my approach would be...' rather than guessing or going silent.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Skipping the business context. Many candidates jump straight into model details without explaining what problem they were solving and why it mattered. At a services company like HCL, the business outcome carries as much weight as the technical approach.

Using 'we' when describing your own work. Interviewers want to know what you did. Replace 'we built a model' with 'I was responsible for feature engineering and model training, while my colleague handled the data pipeline.'

Defaulting to the most complex solution you know. If a simpler model would have solved the problem adequately, explain why you chose it. HCL projects often have practical constraints that make simplicity a virtue, not a weakness.

Not knowing your own resume numbers. If your CV claims your model 'improved accuracy,' be ready to explain the baseline, the metric used, and why that improvement mattered to the business. Vague claims that fall apart under questioning leave a poor impression.

Stopping your story at model training. Ending with 'the model achieved good accuracy' without mentioning how it was used, maintained, or monitored suggests limited production experience. Even if you have not personally deployed a model, show you understand what comes after training.

Asking no questions at the end. Candidates who ask nothing signal low interest. Prepare at least two genuine questions about team structure, the type of client problems you would work on, or what success looks like in the first few months.
