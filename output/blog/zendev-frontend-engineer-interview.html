02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions are based on what Frontend Engineer candidates at Zendev and similar product companies commonly report facing. Use them as a focused prep list.

  1. Walk me through how React's reconciliation algorithm works and why it matters for performance.
  2. How do you handle state management in a large React application? What tradeoffs do you weigh between Context, Redux, Zustand, or other options?
  3. Explain the difference between SSR, SSG, and CSR. When would you choose each for a product?
  4. Describe a time you improved the performance of a slow frontend page or component. What tools did you use and what changed?
  5. How have you handled a complex UI bug in production? Walk through your debugging process step by step.
  6. How do you structure a component library to be reusable and maintainable across multiple teams or products?
  7. What is your approach to web accessibility (a11y)? Give a concrete example of how you have applied it.
  8. How do you manage API error states, loading states, and empty states in a way that gives users a smooth experience?
  9. Tell me about a project where you worked closely with a designer or backend engineer. How did you resolve any disagreements?
  10. How do you keep CSS maintainable in a large codebase? What methodologies or tooling do you rely on?
  11. Describe a situation where you had to learn an unfamiliar technology or framework quickly. What was your approach?
  12. What is your testing strategy for frontend code? How do you decide what to unit-test versus what to integration-test?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format for behavioural questions: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Here are three examples tailored to questions commonly asked at Zendev.

Q: Describe a time you improved the performance of a slow frontend page.

*Situation:* At my previous company, our main dashboard took a long time to load and users were dropping off before data appeared.

*Task:* I was asked to investigate and reduce the load time without changing the core feature set.

*Action:* I used Chrome DevTools and Lighthouse to profile the page. I found three issues: an oversized bundle due to an un-tree-shaken charting library, a waterfall of sequential API calls, and missing memoisation on a list that re-rendered on every keystroke. I replaced the charting library with a lighter alternative, parallelised the API calls using Promise.all, and wrapped the list component with React.memo.

*Result:* Load time dropped noticeably on standard connections. The team adopted the same profiling checklist for new features going forward.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you resolved a disagreement with a designer.

*Situation:* A designer wanted a complex animation sequence on our onboarding flow. I felt it would hurt performance on mid-range Android devices that a large portion of our users were on.

*Task:* I needed to advocate for a performance-conscious approach without dismissing the designer's vision entirely.

*Action:* Instead of just saying no, I built a quick prototype showing the animation on a throttled device. I then proposed an alternative: a simpler CSS transition that achieved the same feel. I shared both with the designer and product manager together so the decision was data-driven rather than based on opinion alone.

*Result:* The team agreed to the simpler animation. It shipped on time, performed well on low-end devices, and the designer appreciated being included in the decision rather than overruled.

---

Q: Describe a situation where you had to learn an unfamiliar technology quickly.

*Situation:* My team decided mid-sprint to migrate our data-fetching layer from plain fetch to React Query. I had no prior experience with it.

*Task:* I had to get productive within two days so I could contribute to the migration alongside the rest of the team.

*Action:* I spent the first day going through the official docs and building a small side project to understand caching, invalidation, and query keys. On the second day I paired with a senior engineer on our first real migration task, asking questions as I went. I also wrote a short internal note documenting the patterns we settled on for our codebase.

*Result:* I was able to migrate two feature modules independently by end of sprint. The internal note became the reference guide the team used for the rest of the migration.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For technical 'how does X work' questions, use a three-part structure: define the concept in one sentence, explain the mechanism in plain terms, then give a concrete example from your own work or a well-known use case. Avoid reciting documentation. Show that you understand why the feature exists, not just what it does.

For behavioural questions, STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is your best structure. Keep Situation and Task brief, maybe two sentences each. Spend most of your time on Action, specifically what you did rather than what 'we' did, and make the Result concrete. Candidates who say 'it went well' without specifics miss the chance to stand out.

For system design questions (frontend), candidates report that Zendev interviewers typically want to see you think through component hierarchy, data flow, state placement, and performance considerations in that order. Start by clarifying requirements, sketch the architecture before diving into code details, and flag tradeoffs as you go.

For opinion or tradeoff questions (such as 'which state manager do you prefer?'), avoid picking a side without reasoning. Acknowledge the tradeoffs, state your preference with context ('for a team of this size, I would lean toward X because...'), and show that you can adapt based on constraints.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates report, Zendev Frontend interviews tend to reward a few specific qualities.

Deep React knowledge, not just surface familiarity. Knowing that useEffect runs after render is not enough. Interviewers typically probe whether you understand why, and what that means for race conditions, cleanup functions, and dependency arrays.

Product thinking alongside technical skill. Zendev is a product company. Interviewers often ask not just 'can you build this?' but 'should we build it this way?' Candidates who connect technical decisions to user experience or business impact consistently score well.

Clear communication under pressure. Frontend roles at Zendev involve working across teams. Interviewers watch for candidates who think out loud during coding rounds, ask clarifying questions before diving in, and explain tradeoffs rather than just writing code in silence.

Ownership and initiative. Stories where you spotted a problem nobody assigned you and fixed it, or where you improved an underperforming feature without being told to, resonate well in product engineering cultures.

Attention to craft. Accessibility, performance, testability, and clean component APIs all signal that you treat frontend engineering as a discipline, not just a way to put pixels on screen.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused two-week plan. Adjust to your experience level and the specific rounds Zendev has lined up for you.

Week 1: Build your foundation (Days 1-7)

Days 1-3: Go deep on JavaScript internals. Focus on the event loop, closures, prototypes, and async patterns (Promises, async/await, error handling). MDN docs and javascript.info are solid references.

Days 4-5: React internals. Study reconciliation, the virtual DOM, hooks rules and why they exist, and rendering behaviour. Ask yourself: when does a component re-render, and how do you prevent unnecessary ones? Write small examples to test your understanding.

Days 6-7: Frontend system design. Practice talking through how you would architect a component library, a data table with sorting and filtering, or a real-time notification feed. Focus on component hierarchy, state placement, and performance considerations.

Week 2: Practice and polish (Days 8-12)

Days 8-9: Coding practice. Solve array, string, and tree problems at a medium difficulty level. Frontend interviews sometimes include these as warm-ups. Also practice live CSS challenges such as flexbox, grid, and responsive layouts.

Days 10-11: Mock interviews. Do at least two full mock rounds with a peer or on a practice platform. Practise thinking out loud and recovering gracefully when stuck. Recording yourself and watching it back is surprisingly useful.

Day 12: Review the weak areas that came up in your mock rounds. Revisit any topic that felt shaky.

Final stretch (the last two days before your interview): Rest, do light revision of your own notes, and prepare two or three thoughtful questions to ask the interviewers. Good questions signal genuine interest and give you useful information about the role and team.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Memorising answers instead of understanding concepts. Zendev interviewers typically ask follow-up questions that go one level deeper. If you have only memorised a definition, you will struggle when they ask 'why does it work that way?' or 'what would happen if you changed X?'

Staying silent during coding rounds. Interviewers want to see how you think, not just whether you reach the right answer. Narrate your approach as you go. If you are stuck, say so and talk through what you have ruled out.

Ignoring edge cases and error states. Candidates who code only the happy path miss marks. Always ask 'what if the API fails?' or 'what if the list is empty?' and handle those cases in your code, or at least call them out explicitly.

Vague behavioural answers. Saying 'I improved the performance of a page' is weak. Describing what you profiled, what you changed, and what measurably improved is strong. Be specific about your actions and the outcome.

Not asking clarifying questions before coding. Jumping straight into code without understanding requirements is a red flag in system design and live coding rounds. Spend a couple of minutes asking questions first.

Underselling your own contribution. Indian professionals sometimes default to saying 'we did X' when the interviewer needs to hear 'I did X as part of a team.' Be specific about your personal role in any outcome you describe.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many interview rounds does Zendev typically have for Frontend Engineer roles?

Candidates report a process that typically runs 3-4 rounds, though this can vary by seniority and which team you are joining. You can expect at least one JavaScript and React technical round, a coding challenge (take-home or live), and a final round that may include system design or a culture discussion. With 18 open Frontend Engineer roles at Zendev right now, the exact structure may differ between teams.

Is there a take-home assignment at Zendev?

Several candidates report receiving a take-home coding task, though this is not universal across all teams. The tasks typically involve building a small React component or feature, with an emphasis on code quality, accessibility, and handling edge cases rather than just getting something to render. Treat any take-home as a chance to show your best practices: clean component structure, meaningful naming, and thoughtful error handling.

What salary can I expect at Zendev as a Frontend Engineer?

Zendev does not publicly publish salary ranges, so going in with market benchmarks from Glassdoor or levels.fyi is a smart move. Based on knok jobradar data across 405 Frontend Engineer roles, mid-level roles (3-5 years) in the market commonly range 12-22 LPA and senior roles (6-9 years) 24-40 LPA. Your actual offer will depend on your experience level and how you negotiate.

Should I prepare for DSA (data structures and algorithms) questions?

Frontend interviews at product companies like Zendev are generally more focused on JavaScript, React, and UI problem-solving than on heavy DSA. That said, candidates do report encountering medium-difficulty array and string problems as warm-ups. Spend the majority of your prep time on frontend-specific topics and a smaller portion on DSA basics so you are not caught off guard.

How should I handle a question I do not know the answer to?

Do not pretend to know something you do not. Interviewers at product companies value intellectual honesty over bluffing. A good response is something like: 'I have not worked with that specific API, but here is how I would approach learning it,' followed by your reasoning process. This shows problem-solving ability and self-awareness, both of which matter when working in a real team.
