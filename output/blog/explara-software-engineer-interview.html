02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions are compiled from candidate reports and reflect the kind of problems Explara engineers solve day to day. Technical rounds typically blend coding with product-adjacent design questions.

  1. Walk me through how you would design a ticket booking system that handles a large number of concurrent users during a flash sale.
  2. How have you handled database performance issues in a production system? What was your debugging approach?
  3. How would you implement idempotency in a payment or notification service to prevent duplicate processing?
  4. Describe a time you tracked down a hard-to-reproduce bug in a live system.
  5. How do you design a REST API for an event registration flow? What are the key trade-offs?
  6. Tell me about a time you refactored legacy code without breaking existing functionality.
  7. How would you design a notification system that sends emails, SMS, and push notifications reliably at scale?
  8. Walk me through how you would implement pagination for a large dataset of events and attendees.
  9. Describe your experience with message queues or async processing. When would you choose them over synchronous calls?
  10. How do you ensure your code is maintainable and testable? Give a specific example from your work.
  11. Tell me about a feature you owned end-to-end. What trade-offs did you make during design?
  12. How would you handle a situation where a third-party integration (such as a payment gateway) is intermittently unreliable?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use these as templates. Swap in your own projects and keep answers to roughly two to three minutes when spoken aloud.

Q: Walk me through how you would design a ticket booking system that handles a large number of concurrent users during a flash sale.

*Situation:* At my previous company, we ran a limited-availability product launch where many users tried to complete purchases at the same time.

*Task:* I was responsible for ensuring the checkout flow would not oversell inventory or create race conditions.

*Action:* I implemented optimistic locking on inventory records, added a Redis-based reservation layer to hold seats for a short window during checkout, and used a message queue to process payment confirmations asynchronously. I also wrapped the payment gateway calls in a circuit breaker to handle downstream failures gracefully.

*Result:* The launch completed with no overselling incidents and no major downtime. The team could monitor queue depth in real time, and the system scaled cleanly under peak load without manual intervention.

---

Q: Describe a time you tracked down a hard-to-reproduce bug in a live system.

*Situation:* Our event registration service was occasionally sending duplicate confirmation emails to attendees, but only during high-traffic windows.

*Task:* I needed to find the root cause and fix it without disrupting registrations already in progress.

*Action:* I added structured logging around the email dispatch path, which let me trace the issue to a missing idempotency key on retry attempts. I then introduced a deduplication check using a Redis key with a short TTL and wrote integration tests covering the retry scenario before deploying the fix.

*Result:* Duplicate emails stopped appearing after the fix was deployed, and the customer support queue for this complaint cleared up within a business day. The logging we added also became useful for monitoring other parts of the notification pipeline.

---

Q: Tell me about a feature you owned end-to-end.

*Situation:* Our platform needed a self-service check-in feature so event organizers could scan QR codes at venue entry points from a mobile device, without needing a desktop.

*Task:* I was the sole engineer responsible for designing and delivering the check-in API and the front-end integration.

*Action:* I wrote a design doc covering the ticket validation state machine, the API contract, and an offline-first sync strategy for venues with poor connectivity. I reviewed it with the product and QA teams before writing any code, then built the feature in incremental pull requests with tests at each step.

*Result:* The feature shipped on schedule. Organizers used it successfully at several events, and post-launch feedback led to one minor UX tweak. The core architecture held up with no production bugs reported.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) is the most reliable structure for behavioral questions. Keep Situation and Task brief (one to two sentences each), spend most of your time on Action (what you personally did, not what the team did), and always close with a concrete Result even if it is qualitative.

For system design questions, candidates report that Explara interviewers respond well to a structured approach: clarify requirements first, sketch the overall components, then drill into the trickiest part. For an event platform, the trickiest parts are usually concurrent seat reservation, idempotent payment handling, and reliable notification delivery. Lead with those.

For coding questions, talk through your approach before writing any code. Name the data structure or algorithm you plan to use and explain why. After writing a working solution, proactively discuss its time and space complexity and mention any edge cases you considered. Interviewers at product companies often care more about your reasoning than the final answer.

For debugging questions, use a 'hypothesis first' structure: state what you observed, list possible causes in order of likelihood, describe how you would isolate each one, and explain what you found. This mirrors how experienced engineers actually debug and is more convincing than a story that jumps straight to the fix.

The 'Why Explara' question deserves a genuine answer. Mentioning the product specifically (event management, ticketing, attendee workflows) is more credible than generic startup enthusiasm. Think about a real problem in event management that you find technically interesting.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Product-aware engineering judgment. Explara is a product company, not a services firm. Interviewers typically want to see that you think about user impact alongside technical correctness, not just whether the code compiles.

Comfort with concurrency and reliability problems. Event ticketing is full of classic distributed systems challenges: overselling seats, duplicate payments, delayed notifications. Candidates who can speak fluently about idempotency, locking strategies, and retry semantics tend to stand out.

Ownership and follow-through. In a focused team, engineers are expected to carry features from design to deployment to post-launch monitoring. Stories that show end-to-end ownership, including what you observed after shipping, resonate more than stories about writing a PR and moving on.

Pragmatic trade-off thinking. Candidates report that Explara interviewers probe trade-offs: why did you pick this approach, what did you leave out, what would you do differently? Admitting a reasonable trade-off is a strength, not a weakness.

Clear communication. With a small engineering team, the ability to write a clear design doc or explain a technical decision to a non-technical stakeholder matters. Practice explaining your work without jargon.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Core coding and platform context

Review data structures and algorithms with a focus on problems relevant to a ticketing platform: queues, trees, hash maps, and sorting. Spend time on concurrency patterns (optimistic vs pessimistic locking, idempotency keys) since these come up in Explara interviews, per candidate reports. Use Explara's own product as a study guide: book a free event, trace the registration flow, and think about how you would build each part.

Week 2: System design and behavioral prep

Practice designing two or three systems relevant to Explara's domain: a seat reservation system, a notification fanout service, and a webhook delivery system. For each one, work through the concurrency challenge explicitly. In parallel, write out two or three STAR stories from your own work covering a bug you fixed, a feature you owned end-to-end, and a time you handled a production incident.

Week 3: Mock interviews and company research

Do at least two timed mock interviews with a peer who can give honest feedback on your communication clarity. Research Explara's recent product updates and job descriptions to understand what the team is currently building. Prepare two or three questions to ask your interviewers about the team's current technical challenges, the on-call culture, or how product decisions get made.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Starting to code before clarifying requirements. In a product company interview, jumping straight to code without asking about scale, edge cases, or constraints signals poor habits. Spend a minute or two aligning on scope before you write anything.

Describing 'we' instead of 'I' in behavioral answers. Interviewers need to evaluate your individual contribution. Replace 'we built' with 'I designed' or 'I was responsible for.' You can credit the team at the end, but lead with your own role.

Ignoring failure modes in system design. Candidates who design only the happy path score lower. For any Explara-style design question, explicitly address what happens when a component fails: what retries, what fallbacks, what alerts.

Treating salary discussion as a formality. With 4 open roles at Explara and a market of 5,395 Software Engineer openings across India, you have real leverage. Know the salary bands (see the table in the overview section) and be ready to anchor with a specific number based on your experience band.

Under-preparing for 'why this company' questions. Generic answers about growth or learning are forgettable. Mention something specific about the event management domain or a technical challenge that Explara's product actually faces.

Skipping post-result analysis in STAR answers. Ending a story with 'the feature shipped' is weaker than adding what you observed afterward, what you would change, or what the team learned.
