01 Overview

Overview

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates report that Dalberg Advisors interviewers focus on three broad themes: your ability to manage complex, ambiguous programs; your understanding of the development sector; and your skill in aligning diverse, often non-technical stakeholders. These are the questions most commonly cited by candidates who have gone through this process.

  1. Walk us through a program you managed end to end. How did you define success for a mission-driven stakeholder?
  2. Dalberg works across health, agriculture, and climate. How do you quickly build domain knowledge when you join a new project?
  3. Describe a time you managed a technically complex program with limited resources. What trade-offs did you make, and why?
  4. How do you handle scope creep in a consulting engagement where client priorities shift frequently?
  5. Tell us about a time you had to align stakeholders from very different backgrounds (for example, a government ministry, an NGO, and a tech team) toward a single delivery timeline.
  6. How do you build a program roadmap when requirements are ambiguous or come from multiple clients at once?
  7. Describe a situation where a key technology dependency was delayed. How did you protect the program and manage the client relationship?
  8. How do you measure impact for a program that has both technical deliverables and social outcomes?
  9. Give an example of when you escalated a risk to senior leadership. What triggered the escalation, and what was the outcome?
  10. Dalberg values analytical rigour. Walk us through how you use data to monitor program health and catch problems early.
  11. How do you translate technical constraints into language that a non-technical government or foundation client can act on?
  12. What draws you specifically to development-sector work, and how does your program management experience connect to that interest?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format for every behavioural question: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep each answer to roughly two minutes when spoken aloud. These examples show the level of specificity Dalberg interviewers typically look for.

Q: Describe a time you managed a technically complex program with limited resources.

*Situation:* I was leading a data platform rollout for a public health analytics project. Mid-program, the engineering team lost a senior developer to an unexpected departure.

*Task:* I needed to protect the delivery timeline without compromising data quality, because a government partner was depending on the outputs for an upcoming budget planning cycle.

*Action:* I ran a quick scope triage with the remaining team and renegotiated one module to a later phase, prioritising the pipeline components the government needed first. I brought in a contract data engineer for a short sprint and set up daily standups to catch blockers early. I also proactively communicated the revised plan to the client before they heard about the team change from anyone else.

*Result:* We delivered the core pipeline on the original date. The deferred module launched on a revised schedule with no impact on the partner's budget cycle, and the client cited our transparency as a reason they extended the engagement.

---

Q: How do you align stakeholders from very different backgrounds toward a common timeline?

*Situation:* On a rural finance program, I had to coordinate between a technology vendor, a microfinance NGO, and a state government department, each with different reporting lines and conflicting expectations.

*Task:* We needed sign-off on a unified data-sharing protocol within three weeks or the pilot launch would slip.

*Action:* I mapped each stakeholder's primary concern: the vendor wanted clear API specs, the NGO wanted data privacy guarantees, and the government team wanted a paper trail for audit purposes. I rewrote the protocol document so each concern was addressed explicitly on the first page rather than buried in technical appendices. I then ran a single joint review session instead of three separate calls, which forced a shared conversation and cut back-and-forth considerably.

*Result:* We got sign-off well within the deadline. The NGO later said it was the smoothest cross-stakeholder alignment they had seen on a government-linked program.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you escalated a risk. What triggered it?

*Situation:* I was managing a capacity-building program where a third-party training vendor was repeatedly missing content milestones.

*Task:* The delays were not yet visible to the foundation funder, but I could see they would cause a significant slip if not addressed within the week.

*Action:* I documented the delay pattern with dates and original commitments, prepared two remediation options with honest trade-offs, and escalated to my engagement lead with a clear recommendation rather than just a problem statement. I also had a direct conversation with the vendor beforehand to understand their root-cause blockers so I could speak to them accurately in the escalation meeting.

*Result:* The engagement lead chose the option that revised the vendor contract with milestone accountability clauses. The vendor accelerated delivery, and the program launched only a few weeks behind the original date rather than months behind.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions. Every behavioural question at Dalberg expects a concrete story. Lead with the Situation (one or two sentences of context), the Task (your specific responsibility), the Action (what you personally did, not what the team did), and the Result (a measurable or clearly observable outcome). Candidates who stay abstract or use 'we' throughout rarely progress past the first round.

MECE thinking for problem-solving questions. If an interviewer gives you an ambiguous program scenario, structure your answer so your categories are mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive. For example, when asked how you would monitor program health, break it into people risks, delivery risks, and stakeholder risks rather than listing random items in no particular order.

Stakeholder mapping for alignment questions. When a question involves multiple parties (government, NGO, tech team, funder), briefly name each stakeholder's primary incentive before describing what you did. This signals consulting-level thinking and shows you did not treat all stakeholders as a single group with the same interests.

The 'so what' close. End every answer with a sentence that ties the delivery back to the actual mission or business outcome. 'The module launched on time' is weaker than 'The module launched on time, which meant the district health officer had the data she needed before the state budget window closed.' Dalberg interviewers are consistently looking for people who connect technical delivery to real-world impact.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Mission alignment grounded in experience, not just interest. Saying you care about development is table stakes. Interviewers want to see that you have thought through why technology programs in the development sector are harder than corporate ones (procurement constraints, government approval timelines, beneficiary data sensitivity) and that you have either navigated those challenges or are genuinely curious about them in a specific, informed way.

Analytical rigour with a light touch. Dalberg's consulting DNA means interviewers notice whether you think in frameworks and back claims with evidence. However, they also work with non-technical clients daily, so candidates who lead with jargon or over-engineer their answers can struggle. The sweet spot is structured thinking explained in plain language.

Calm under ambiguity. Many Dalberg programs start with unclear requirements and evolving client priorities. Interviewers probe for how you respond when a brief changes mid-engagement or a key assumption turns out to be wrong. Stories that show composure, rapid reorientation, and honest communication under pressure are consistently what stands out.

Cross-functional credibility. TPMs at Dalberg typically sit between engineers, data scientists, and non-technical client teams. Interviewers want evidence that both sides trust you: that engineers see you as someone who understands technical constraints, and that clients see you as a clear, reliable communicator who will not overpromise on their behalf.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Understand Dalberg's work in depth. Read their public reports and case studies in the sectors most relevant to the role you applied for. Candidates who can reference a specific Dalberg initiative or publication make a much stronger impression than those who say only 'I know you work in health and agriculture.'

Step 2: Map your own stories to their themes. Before the interview, prepare at least one STAR story for each of these themes: managing ambiguity, aligning diverse stakeholders, handling a delayed dependency, and measuring non-commercial impact. Four strong stories cover the majority of questions that candidates report being asked.

Step 3: Practise out loud, not just on paper. Dalberg interviews are typically conversational and evaluative of how you communicate. Reading your answers in your head is not the same as saying them clearly and calmly in two minutes. Record yourself or run a mock with a friend.

Step 4: Prepare thoughtful questions for your interviewers. Ask how the TPM role interfaces with Dalberg's consulting teams, or how impact is defined on a typical technology engagement. Avoid questions whose answers are already on the public website.

Step 5: Review your program management fundamentals. Be ready to discuss how you manage a program backlog, communicate risks upward, and run retrospectives. You do not need to follow a specific methodology, but you should be able to explain your approach clearly and defend the choices you make.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Giving generic PM answers with no sector context. Many candidates describe textbook Agile ceremonies without connecting them to development-sector realities. Dalberg interviewers can quickly tell the difference between someone who has managed a SaaS product roadmap and someone who has dealt with government procurement timelines, donor reporting cycles, or beneficiary data constraints.

Overusing 'we' instead of 'I'. In a collaborative consulting culture, candidates often default to 'we managed' or 'our team delivered.' Interviewers need to understand your specific contribution. Be comfortable saying what you personally decided, communicated, or escalated, even when the outcome was a team effort.

Not knowing Dalberg's actual work. Candidates report that interviewers can tell within the first few minutes whether someone has done real research or just skimmed the homepage. Specific knowledge of a Dalberg program, publication, or sector initiative saves you from generic 'I want to make an impact' answers that do not differentiate you.

Treating mission interest as sufficient on its own. On the other end, some candidates lean so heavily on their passion for impact that they underplay delivery discipline. Dalberg is a professional advisory firm with rigorous standards. Your motivation for the mission should always be paired with concrete evidence of how you deliver.

Closing answers at the technical level. Results that stop at 'the platform went live on time' without connecting to who benefited and how miss what Dalberg values most. Always close with the downstream outcome: what the client was able to do because you delivered.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Dalberg Advisors TPM interview typically have?

Candidates report that the process typically involves two to four conversations, often starting with a recruiter or HR screen followed by one or two interviews with the program or technical team and sometimes a final conversation with a senior leader. The exact structure varies by team and location. It is worth asking your recruiter for the expected format when they first reach out so you can plan your preparation accordingly.

Does Dalberg give a technical assessment or case study for TPM roles?

Candidates report that Dalberg sometimes uses consulting-style case discussions rather than a traditional coding assessment for TPM roles. The case may involve scoping a technology program for a development-sector client or prioritising features under resource constraints. Practising structured problem-solving out loud is more useful than preparing for a whiteboard coding exercise, though the format can vary by team.

What salary can I expect for a TPM role at Dalberg Advisors in India?

Dalberg does not publicly publish salary bands for India-based TPM roles, and the knok jobradar data for this role does not include verified compensation figures. Publicly reported ranges on Glassdoor and similar platforms vary widely by seniority and city. Your best move is to ask the recruiter directly for the band during your first call so you do not spend time in a process that is misaligned on compensation.

Is Dalberg a good fit if my background is entirely in corporate tech rather than the development sector?

Yes, candidates from corporate tech backgrounds do join Dalberg, but you will need to demonstrate genuine curiosity about the development sector and show that you have thought through how your skills translate. The biggest gap is usually not technical but contextual: government procurement cycles, non-commercial KPIs, and multi-stakeholder coordination patterns differ significantly from a product company environment. Prepare at least one story that shows you have navigated ambiguous or mission-adjacent work, even if informally.

How important is domain knowledge in health, agriculture, or climate for the TPM interview?

Interviewers do not expect you to arrive as a domain expert, but they do expect you to show that you can learn a domain quickly and ask the right questions. Reading one or two Dalberg public reports in the sector most relevant to the role you applied for is usually enough to show genuine effort. Candidates who demonstrate intellectual curiosity about the sector consistently do better than those who approach the role as a generic PM position.

How competitive is the Dalberg TPM hiring process right now?

Dalberg Advisors had 16 open roles in India as tracked by knok jobradar in July 2026, which indicates active hiring. However, TPM roles at mission-driven advisory firms tend to attract candidates from both consulting and tech backgrounds, making the pool genuinely competitive. Strong preparation on the development-sector context and clear STAR stories for ambiguity and stakeholder alignment are the most reliable ways to differentiate yourself from candidates who treat it like a standard tech interview.
