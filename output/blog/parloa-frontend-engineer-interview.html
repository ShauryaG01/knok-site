01 Overview

Overview

Parloa is a conversational AI company that builds voice and chat automation for enterprise customer service teams. As of mid-2026, it has 61 open roles, a sign of rapid scaling. Frontend Engineers here work on a complex SaaS platform where enterprise users configure AI agents, review conversation analytics, and manage integrations with telephony and messaging providers.

Candidates typically report a process of three to five rounds: an initial recruiter call, a technical screen on JavaScript and React, a coding task (take-home or live), a system design round, and a final conversation with a hiring manager or senior team member. Round names and order can vary, so treat this as a guide. Confirm the exact structure with your recruiter.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions appear frequently in Parloa Frontend Engineer interviews, based on candidate reports and the nature of the product:

  1. Walk us through the most complex React component you have built. How did you manage state across it?
  2. How do you use TypeScript in a large codebase? Which patterns have saved you from the most bugs?
  3. Parloa processes real-time data from AI agents. How would you architect a UI that shows live conversation streams?
  4. What is your experience with WebSockets or Server-Sent Events? When would you choose one over the other?
  5. How do you approach accessibility (a11y) when building a dashboard for enterprise users who may not be technical?
  6. Describe a time you meaningfully improved frontend performance. What did you measure, and what changed?
  7. How would you build a reusable component library for a SaaS product where branding consistency matters to enterprise clients?
  8. Parloa connects to telephony and messaging channels. How would you design a configurable 'channel settings' UI that works for multiple integration types?
  9. How do you handle error boundaries and loading states so that one failing widget does not crash the whole dashboard?
  10. Walk us through how you collaborated with designers, backend engineers, and a product manager to ship a feature from spec to production.
  11. What is your testing strategy for frontend code? How do you balance unit tests, integration tests, and end-to-end tests?
  12. What excites you about building UI for AI-powered, conversational products?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format for behavioural questions. Here are three worked examples.

Q: Describe a time you improved frontend performance.

*Situation:* Our analytics dashboard loaded a full table on mount, pulling a large volume of historical call data. Users on slower connections were seeing a blank screen for several seconds.

*Task:* I was asked to cut the initial load time without hiding any data from users.

*Action:* I profiled the network waterfall and found we were fetching all rows before rendering anything. I introduced virtual scrolling using 'react-virtual', added server-side pagination, and lazy-loaded the charting library since it was only needed when a user drilled into a report.

*Result:* Initial render dropped noticeably in staging tests. The PM reported that users stopped filing 'dashboard is broken' support tickets after the release.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you worked closely with a backend team to agree on an API contract.

*Situation:* We were building a 'live agent handoff' screen so a human agent could see an AI conversation transcript before taking over a call.

*Task:* The backend team and I needed to agree on a data shape before either side could start building.

*Action:* I drafted a mock JSON response covering every UI state (loading, partial transcript, handoff complete, error), shared it in a collaborative doc, and ran a short review with backend and design before any real code was written. We caught two missing fields early.

*Result:* Integration went smoothly with no blocking issues at the end. The feature shipped on time and the handoff screen received strong positive feedback from early customers.

---

Q: Give an example of making a UI accessible for users with disabilities.

*Situation:* A quarterly audit flagged our dialog modals as failing WCAG 2.1 AA, specifically around focus trapping and missing ARIA roles.

*Task:* I owned the fix across the component library, which was used in many places across the product.

*Action:* I replaced our custom modal with a 'Radix UI Dialog' primitive, added 'aria-labelledby' and 'aria-describedby' bindings, and wrote a clear checklist in the component docs so future contributors would not regress the work. I also added a jest-axe test to catch violations in CI.

*Result:* The next audit returned no modal violations. The pattern became the team standard and was adopted across other components over the following quarter.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For technical depth questions (state management, TypeScript, architecture): open with the problem you were solving, explain the tradeoffs you weighed, then land on what you chose and why. Interviewers at product companies like Parloa value clear reasoning over textbook recitation.

For system design questions (live data UI, component libraries): use a think-aloud structure. State your constraints first (scale, team size, update frequency), sketch the data flow, then go deep on one area. Knowing what to simplify is as impressive as knowing what to build.

For behavioural questions: follow STAR but do not skip the Result. Many candidates describe what they did in detail and then trail off. The result tells the interviewer whether the approach worked. If the outcome was mixed, say so and explain what you learned.

For 'why Parloa' questions: be specific about the product. Phrases like 'I want to work on AI' apply to hundreds of companies. Tie your answer to something concrete: the challenge of building UI for non-deterministic AI outputs, or the problem of making complex enterprise configuration feel simple to non-technical users.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Parloa interviewers are typically looking for four things in a Frontend Engineer.

Strong React and TypeScript fundamentals. This is a product that handles complex, real-time state. Candidates who can discuss rendering behaviour, memoisation, and type safety at a practical level stand out.

Product thinking. Parloa sells to enterprise clients who care about reliability and usability. Engineers who have opinions about user experience, and who can connect their technical decisions to user outcomes, tend to progress further.

Comfort in cross-functional teams. The product involves AI, telephony, and cloud integrations, so frontend work is always adjacent to complex backend systems. Interviewers look for candidates who have navigated unclear requirements without blocking themselves or their team.

Genuine interest in the problem space. Conversational AI is a specific domain. Candidates who have thought about the UX challenges of displaying AI-generated content (confidence scores, fallback states, human handoff flows) tend to connect better with the team.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: React and TypeScript foundations
Review hooks deeply, especially 'useReducer', 'useContext', and custom hooks for data fetching. Practise writing TypeScript generics and utility types. If you have not used 'react-query' or 'SWR', spend a day on one, since real-time data management is central to Parloa's product.

Week 2: System design and product familiarity
Explore Parloa's public website and any available demos so you understand what the platform actually does. Prepare a design for a 'live conversation dashboard' that handles websocket updates, error states, and pagination. This is the kind of problem that tends to come up in design rounds.

Week 3: Behavioural prep and mock interviews
Write STAR stories for performance improvements, cross-team collaboration, and accessibility work. Run at least two mock interviews with a peer or using a practice platform. Polish your 'why Parloa' answer so it references the product specifically, not just the AI space in general.

Keep your job search parallel

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating it like a pure algorithms interview. Parloa is a product company. Competitive-programming-style problems are unlikely to dominate. Candidates who over-prepare for DSA and under-prepare for component design and real-world React scenarios often find themselves caught off guard.

Vague answers about TypeScript. Saying 'I use TypeScript for type safety' is not enough. Be ready to discuss specific patterns: discriminated unions for state machines, generic API wrappers, or how you have handled third-party libraries with poor type definitions.

Skipping the accessibility angle. Enterprise SaaS products face real accessibility requirements from clients. Candidates who have never thought about ARIA, keyboard navigation, or WCAG compliance signal a gap that matters in this market.

Not asking questions at the end. The final round typically includes time for your questions. Candidates who ask nothing, or who ask only about salary, leave a weak impression. Ask about the team's approach to design systems, how they handle breaking API changes from backend, or what the biggest current frontend challenge is.

Generic 'why this company' answers. 'I want to work on AI' applies to hundreds of companies. Specificity about Parloa's product, its enterprise customer base, or a technical challenge you noticed in the job description always lands better.
