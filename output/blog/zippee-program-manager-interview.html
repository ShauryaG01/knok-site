01 Overview

Overview

Zippee is a quick-commerce and last-mile logistics startup with active hiring across India, currently listing 51 open roles. The Technical Program Manager position sits at the intersection of product, engineering, and operations, where you own complex, multi-team programs end-to-end rather than just coordinating meetings or updating trackers.

Across India, knok jobradar tracked 313 TPM openings as of July 2026. Bangalore leads with 41 openings, followed by Delhi (14), Pune (13), Hyderabad (12), Chennai (5), and Mumbai (1). Zippee's interview process typically includes a recruiter screen, one or two rounds with engineering or product leaders, and a final discussion with senior leadership. Candidates report the process moves quickly, which itself reflects what Zippee values in a TPM.

The logistics domain adds specific demands: delivery windows are measured in minutes, real-time system reliability matters directly to revenue, and cross-functional complexity spans engineering, operations, and city-level teams. Interviewers want to see that you can create clarity and momentum in that kind of environment.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up repeatedly in Zippee TPM interviews, based on what candidates in similar roles and companies typically report.

  1. Walk us through a complex program you owned end-to-end. How did you define and measure success?
  2. Zippee moves very fast. Tell us about a time you had to make a consequential decision with incomplete or conflicting information.
  3. How do you manage hard dependencies across multiple engineering teams when everyone's timeline is already stretched?
  4. Describe how you handle scope creep on a program that already has a committed delivery date.
  5. How do you build credibility with senior engineers or architects who know the technical domain better than you?
  6. Tell us about a time you pushed back on a leadership ask, whether on scope, timeline, or resources.
  7. How do you communicate program status to stakeholders who have very different levels of technical depth?
  8. Describe a situation where a third-party vendor or external dependency caused a major delay. How did you respond?
  9. How do you prioritize when multiple high-priority programs are competing for the same engineering resources?
  10. How would you set up a brand-new program from scratch at a company like Zippee, where the domain and requirements change quickly?
  11. Have you worked with real-time systems, data pipelines, or high-throughput platforms? How does that shape your approach?
  12. Tell us about a program that missed a key milestone or failed. What went wrong and what did you change afterward?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through a complex program you owned end-to-end.

*Situation:* At my previous company, we were migrating a core order-management service from a monolith to microservices while the platform continued to serve live production traffic.

*Task:* I owned the program end-to-end, coordinating backend, mobile, QA, and DevOps teams, while keeping product and business stakeholders informed throughout.

*Action:* I started by building a dependency map so every team could see how their work connected to others. I set up a shared RACI document so ownership of every key decision was explicit, not assumed. For stakeholder visibility, I sent a concise written RAG (red-amber-green) status update to leadership each week, short enough that it actually got read rather than skimmed.

*Result:* We completed the migration with no customer-visible downtime, delivered within the planned quarter, and the new architecture allowed the engineering team to ship feature updates significantly faster in subsequent sprints.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you made a consequential decision with incomplete information.

*Situation:* During a new delivery-slot feature rollout at my last company, we detected inconsistent behaviour in a downstream notification service midway through staging, with the launch date approaching fast.

*Task:* I had to decide whether to delay the launch or proceed with a mitigation plan, with no definitive root-cause analysis yet and real business pressure to go live.

*Action:* I called a quick alignment session with the tech lead and QA lead to assess the blast radius of the known risk. We agreed to do a partial rollout to a small initial cohort, with rollback criteria defined upfront, so we could gather real data without full customer exposure. I documented the risk and the mitigation in writing and got explicit sign-off from the product owner before proceeding.

*Result:* The partial rollout confirmed the issue was isolated to a specific edge case. We patched it before expanding the rollout, and the feature went fully live without incident. The written sign-off meant the whole team was aligned on the decision throughout.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you pushed back on a leadership ask.

*Situation:* Leadership wanted to compress a multi-sprint program into a single sprint to align with an upcoming marketing campaign date.

*Task:* My job was to give an honest, evidence-based assessment of what was actually feasible without shipping a half-built product or burning out the team.

*Action:* I prepared a short trade-off brief with three clearly labelled options: (1) deliver the full scope on the original timeline, (2) deliver a reduced MVP by the marketing date with documented gaps communicated to the sales team upfront, or (3) shift the marketing date and deliver the full scope intact. I walked the VP through this in a brief meeting, keeping the tone factual and focused on business impact rather than effort.

*Result:* Leadership chose the reduced MVP path. The product launched on the marketing date, the sales team knew the gaps upfront so there were no surprises, and the full feature shipped in the following sprint. The VP later said the trade-off brief made the decision straightforward.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions is the standard foundation: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task brief and spend most of your time on Action and Result. Zippee interviewers want to understand what you specifically decided and did, not just what the team achieved collectively.

The trade-off brief format works well for questions about prioritization, scope decisions, or any tension with leadership or stakeholders. Structure your answer as: here is what was being asked, here are the realistic options (typically two or three), here is the reasoning I used to evaluate them, and here is what happened. This signals that you think in options and trade-offs, not just in yes-or-no binaries.

Dependency mapping is a strong framing for cross-functional program questions. Explain how you identified dependencies at the start, which ones sat on the critical path, and what you did proactively to de-risk those. This shows TPM maturity well beyond basic project tracking.

Communication tiering handles stakeholder questions well. Describe how you segment your audience: engineering teams get detailed async updates with specifics, product and business stakeholders get a RAG summary with decisions needed, and senior leadership gets a brief with business impact front and centre. Adapting format by audience demonstrates real cross-functional experience rather than a one-size-fits-all approach.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Zippee TPM interviewers are typically looking for a few clear signals, based on what candidates at similar high-velocity logistics and quick-commerce companies report.

Technical credibility without being the coder. You need to hold your own in discussions about system architecture, API dependencies, and infrastructure constraints. You are not expected to write the code, but you must ask the right questions and know when an engineering estimate is credible or optimistic.

Ownership over coordination. 'Program manager' sometimes implies someone who runs standups and updates a tracker. Zippee wants a TPM who takes accountability for outcomes, removes blockers before they escalate, and makes decisions rather than routing every question upward.

Comfort with speed and ambiguity. Quick-commerce is a domain where requirements shift fast and incomplete information is normal. Interviewers probe whether you have a method for making good-enough decisions quickly or whether you wait for perfect clarity that never arrives.

Stakeholder influence without formal authority. You will not have direct authority over the engineering teams you coordinate. Interviewers want to see how you earn trust and drive alignment through credibility and communication, not through hierarchy or pressure.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Understand Zippee's business model before anything else. Read publicly available news coverage of Zippee's operations, their position in India's quick-commerce market, and the logistics challenges they face. Interviewers notice when a candidate understands that a TPM at a logistics company deals with real-time constraints that a SaaS TPM does not.

Prepare five or six strong STAR stories that you can adapt across different question types. Cover at minimum: a complex multi-team program, a decision made under uncertainty, a scope or timeline conflict, a stakeholder communication challenge, and a program that did not go as planned.

Practise trade-off thinking out loud. A common TPM interview failure is giving answers that sound too certain too quickly. Practise framing your thinking as 'here were my options and here is why I chose this one' rather than 'I just did X.'

Know the technical domain at a high level. If you are coming from a non-logistics background, read up on how real-time tracking, route optimisation, and order-management systems typically work at a broad architectural level. Speaking the domain language fluently helps even without direct experience.

Prepare two or three sharp questions for the interviewer. Good examples for a Zippee TPM role: 'What does the most complex ongoing program look like right now, and where are the hardest dependencies?' or 'How does the TPM function interact with the engineering roadmap process here?'

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Sounding like a coordinator rather than an owner. Talking only about tracking tasks, running standups, or updating dashboards signals you are a coordinator. Use language that shows accountability: 'I decided', 'I escalated', 'I pushed back', 'I restructured the scope.'

Vague outcomes in STAR answers. Ending a story with 'the project was a success' gives interviewers nothing to evaluate. Even without exact numbers, describe what changed: the system handled higher load, the launch happened with no rollback needed, the timeline recovered from a significant slip.

Avoiding the failure question. Many candidates respond to 'tell us about a program that failed' with a barely-failure story. Zippee interviewers typically see through this. Pick a real miss, own what went wrong clearly, and focus the bulk of your answer on what you changed as a result.

Downplaying technical knowledge. TPM candidates sometimes underplay technical understanding to seem humble. In a logistics startup, technical credibility is a hard requirement. Talk about architecture decisions you participated in, infrastructure constraints you navigated, or technical trade-offs you helped the team evaluate.

Not asking questions. Leaving the 'do you have any questions?' slot empty signals low curiosity. Come with two or three specific questions about the current programs, the team structure, or the technical challenges Zippee is working through right now.
