03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk me through a time you had to manage competing priorities across multiple engineering projects.

*Situation:* I was running two squads simultaneously: one mid-way through a platform migration with a hard external deadline, and another ramping up a new customer-facing feature the product team had been waiting months for.

*Task:* Both leads came to me in the same week with blockers that needed our one available senior engineer. I had to decide who got that resource without demotivating either team or slipping a critical date.

*Action:* I mapped out the dependencies and risk profiles for both projects in a shared doc, then brought both leads into a single call so each could see the full picture. I directed the senior engineer to the migration for one sprint because a slip there carried a contractual penalty. I then went directly to the product manager for the feature and negotiated an extension by sharing the risk data, rather than just saying 'no.'

*Result:* The migration shipped on time. The feature launched slightly later but with no escalations, because the product manager had been part of the decision. Both leads reported feeling heard rather than overruled.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you had to influence a decision without having direct authority.

*Situation:* A cross-functional initiative needed a shared data platform. The team owning that platform sat under a different VP and had no formal obligation to prioritise work for us.

*Task:* I needed them to commit to a multi-week integration effort that was not on their roadmap, without being able to mandate it.

*Action:* I started by learning what pressures their team was facing. I found they had a reliability target they were struggling to meet. I reframed our integration as something that would reduce their support burden by removing manual pipelines they were maintaining for our team. I wrote a short brief, got my VP aligned, and presented it as a joint proposal rather than a one-sided request.

*Result:* They added the work to their next planning cycle. The integration shipped, and their on-call load for those pipelines dropped noticeably, something they mentioned in their own team retrospective.

---

Q: How do you handle an underperforming engineer on your team?

*Situation:* An engineer on my team had missed deadlines and delivered work requiring heavy rework across two consecutive sprints. Other team members were starting to feel the impact.

*Task:* I needed to address the performance quickly, without losing the engineer if the issue was fixable, and without letting team dynamics deteriorate further.

*Action:* I had a private, candid conversation focused on specific examples rather than general labels. I found out the engineer was dealing with unclear scope expectations, not a skills gap. I set up weekly check-ins, wrote down explicit acceptance criteria for each ticket in the next sprint, and paired them with a senior engineer for a few weeks.

*Result:* Delivery quality returned to the team baseline within a couple of months. The engineer later told me that period was a turning point for them. I also updated our ticket-writing process so scope ambiguity was caught earlier for the whole team.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR (the backbone of every behavioural answer)

Most Engineering Manager interviews follow a competency-based format, and candidates report that Mirvac is no different. STAR keeps your answers structured: Situation (one or two sentences of context), Task (what you specifically were responsible for), Action (the things you did personally, not your team), Result (a concrete outcome or observable change). The most common mistake is spending too long on Situation and rushing through Action. Interviewers score you on what you did, not what happened around you.

SPADE (for decision-making questions)

When asked about a major technical or product decision, SPADE helps you show deliberate thinking: Setting (context and constraints), People (who needed to be involved), Alternatives (what options you considered), Decide (what you chose and why), Explain (how you communicated it). This signals that you make decisions carefully rather than reactively.

The three-lens check (for balancing questions)

For questions about competing priorities, show you hold three things at once: business priority, engineering health, and team capacity. Name all three explicitly, state how you ranked them for that specific situation, and explain why the ranking made sense given the context.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Mirvac operates in a sector where engineering must work reliably alongside physical, regulatory, and financial constraints. Candidates report that interviewers look for Engineering Managers who demonstrate four things consistently.

The ability to translate between worlds. Property and construction stakeholders care about timelines, capital costs, and compliance. Interviewers want to see that you explain technical risk in business terms, not just engineering terms. Bring examples where you turned a technical decision into a business conversation.

Ownership of delivery, not just process. There is a clear difference between a manager who runs Agile ceremonies and one who actively unblocks delivery. Show specific examples of how you removed obstacles, renegotiated scope, or made hard calls to keep things moving, rather than describing the framework you follow.

Intentional people development. Mirvac is building long-term capability, not just shipping product. Interviewers want concrete stories about how you have grown engineers, handled difficult performance conversations, and shaped team culture over time.

Technical credibility that has not atrophied. You do not need to be writing production code, but you should be able to challenge architectural decisions, spot risks in technical plans, and earn the respect of senior engineers in a room. Show that your technical judgement is still sharp even though your day-to-day is now management.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week one: research and story-mining

Read Mirvac's annual reports, investor updates, and any recent announcements about their digital or technology initiatives. Understand the sectors they operate in: residential, commercial, and industrial property. Then identify five to six career moments that map to their priorities: delivery under pressure, stakeholder alignment, team building, and technical decision-making. Write these down as raw bullet points before shaping them into STAR stories.

Week two: answer preparation and rehearsal

Write out structured answers for each of the twelve question types listed above. Time yourself: each answer should run roughly 90 seconds to 2 minutes when spoken aloud. Anything shorter likely lacks detail; anything longer risks losing the interviewer's attention before you reach the result.

Mock interviews

Do at least two mock interviews with a peer, ideally someone who has hired or interviewed for Engineering Manager roles before. Ask them to push back with follow-up questions like 'what would you have done differently?' or 'how did you know it was working?' These follow-ups are where interviewers typically probe depth, and candidates who have only rehearsed the main story often stumble at this point.

Questions to prepare for your interviewer

Prepare three to four thoughtful questions. Good options for Mirvac: how does engineering interface with property development teams day to day? What does a successful Engineering Manager look like here after 12 months? What is the biggest challenge the current team is trying to solve right now?

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating every question as a technical question. Engineering Manager interviews assess leadership, communication, and judgement as much as technical depth. Candidates who answer every question with architecture choices or system details miss what interviewers are actually scoring.

Using 'we' instead of 'I'. Interviewers are assessing you, not your team. When you say 'we shipped' or 'we decided,' they cannot determine what you personally contributed. Replace 'we decided' with 'I recommended,' 'I pushed back,' or 'I made the call.'

Skipping the result. Many candidates give a strong Situation, Task, and Action, then trail off with a vague ending. Always close with what changed because of your actions, even if the result was a learning rather than a clear win.

Arriving without company context. Mirvac is a property and development company, not a pure tech firm. Candidates who frame all examples in generic software company contexts, without connecting to physical-world constraints, typically read as underprepared to interviewers in this sector.

Over-rehearsing word-for-word scripts. Interviewers at this level notice when an answer sounds memorised. Prepare the key beats of each story, not a script. Leave room to adapt when follow-up questions arrive.

Underplaying process changes you drove. Engineering Managers who only discuss project outcomes miss a chance to show systemic impact. If you changed how the team hires, handles incidents, or does code review, say so explicitly. These changes signal a leader, not just a delivery manager.
