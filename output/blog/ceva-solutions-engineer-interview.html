01 Overview

Overview

Ceva is a semiconductor IP licensing company whose processor and connectivity IP is embedded in chips for wireless communications (5G, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth), audio and voice processing, computer vision, and AI inference at the edge. A Solutions Engineer at Ceva bridges the company's engineering depth and its customers' chip design teams: you run product evaluations, support SoC integration, answer architectural questions, and work alongside the sales team to move licensing opportunities forward.

Candidates report the process typically runs 3-5 rounds, mixing a recruiter call, a technical screen covering DSP or embedded concepts, sometimes a take-home or architecture deep-dive session, and a final round with senior engineers or a hiring manager. Rounds are not always given formal names, so treat every conversation as substantive.

Ceva currently lists 184 open roles globally. In India, Solutions Engineer demand as a category is strongest in Bangalore (55 openings tracked on knok jobradar as of July 2026), followed by Mumbai (23), Delhi (20), Pune (12), Hyderabad (6), and Chennai (5), across a market of 1,270 Solutions Engineer openings.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions surface repeatedly in Ceva Solutions Engineer interviews, based on candidate reports and the nature of the role.

  1. 'Walk me through how you would explain Ceva's IP licensing model to a chip design team evaluating third-party processor IP for the first time.'
  2. 'Describe a time you helped a customer through a complex technical integration problem, from first report to resolution.'
  3. 'How do you handle a situation where a customer requirement is something the product genuinely cannot deliver?'
  4. 'You are running a proof-of-concept for a customer evaluating our NeuPro AI processor IP. What does your approach look like week by week?'

  10. 'Which area of Ceva's product portfolio interests you most and why?'
  11. 'Describe a situation where you had to deliver disappointing news to a customer: a missed timeline or a missing feature. What did you say and what happened next?'

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time you helped a customer through a complex technical integration problem.

*Situation:* A fabless chip customer was integrating a third-party DSP IP into their SoC and hitting unexplained latency spikes in their audio pipeline during simulation.

*Task:* As the pre-sales Solutions Engineer supporting their evaluation, I was responsible for keeping their confidence up and getting them to a working proof-of-concept before their internal design review.

*Action:* I set up a brief daily sync with their lead engineer, reviewed their memory bus configuration together, and identified that their AHB arbitration priority settings were starving the DSP of bandwidth during burst transfers. I shared a sanitised reference configuration from a similar design, wrote a short integration note, and walked them through applying the fix.

*Result:* The latency spikes disappeared, their simulation passed, and they extended the evaluation to a second IP block. The account progressed in the sales cycle.

---

Q: How do you handle a customer requirement the product cannot meet?

*Situation:* During a pre-sales call, a customer asked whether our DSP IP natively supported a proprietary encoding scheme their team had developed internally.

*Task:* I needed to give an honest answer without losing the evaluation opportunity or damaging trust.

*Action:* I confirmed with our product team the same day (the answer was no, not on the current roadmap). I went back to the customer with full transparency, explained what the IP could do natively, and offered two options: a software layer their team could build on top, or a co-development discussion with our engineering team if the use case had enough commercial scale.

*Result:* The customer appreciated the direct response. They chose the software layer route, completed integration, and became a paying licensee within the same quarter.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you worked closely with a sales team to close a technically complex deal.

*Situation:* Our sales lead was in late-stage negotiations with an OEM that was uncertain whether our wireless connectivity IP could meet their power envelope for a wearable device.

*Task:* I needed to build enough technical confidence to unblock the commercial conversation before the customer's next internal review.

*Action:* I prepared a power analysis comparison using Ceva's published datasheet figures alongside publicly reported benchmarks from industry surveys, ran a focused Q&A with the customer's hardware architects, and put together a concise summary mapping their constraints to the IP's configurability options.

*Result:* The architects signed off internally and legal finalised the license agreement in the weeks that followed. The sales lead cited the technical session as the turning point.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use STAR for behavioural questions. Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task brief (two or three sentences together) and spend most of your time on Action and Result. Ceva interviewers want to understand what you specifically did, not just the context around it.

Use a structured walk-through for technical questions. Lead with your constraints (latency, power, bandwidth), state your chosen approach, then call out trade-offs explicitly. Saying 'it depends' is fine as long as you immediately name what it depends on.

For customer objection questions, use a three-part structure: First, acknowledge the concern genuinely and without defensiveness. Second, reframe with a concrete example or a data point. Third, offer a clear next step: a proof-of-concept, a reference design, or an intro to the engineering team.

For prioritisation questions, name your criteria out loud: customer impact, deal stage, contractual commitments, and estimated time to resolve. Ceva is a global business, so mentioning async communication and time-zone coordination signals maturity.

Match depth to your audience. Ceva interviews typically involve both engineers and managers. With engineers, go deep on architecture. With managers, connect your technical point to a business outcome.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Ceva Solutions Engineers are customer-facing technical experts, not pure engineers and not pure salespeople. Interviewers are looking for four things in particular.

Technical credibility. You should be able to discuss DSP architectures, SoC integration flows, or wireless protocol stacks at a level that earns the trust of a chip design team. You do not need to know every Ceva product line by heart, but demonstrating genuine curiosity and the ability to get up to speed quickly matters.

Customer empathy. Ceva's customers make long-term licensing commitments. Interviewers want to see that you treat a customer's frustration or scepticism as a problem to solve, not an obstacle to push past.

Communication across audiences. You should be equally comfortable writing a technical integration note for a hardware engineer and summarising a business case for a VP of Engineering. Show range in your examples.

Ownership mindset. Solutions Engineers who wait to be told what to do next rarely thrive. Give concrete examples of times you took initiative: flagging a risk early, preparing a document nobody asked for, or looping in the right internal expert without being prompted.

Ceva operates across multiple time zones (headquarters in Israel, US offices, India engineering centres). Comfort with async and global collaboration is a soft signal interviewers notice.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know the product.
Read everything publicly available about Ceva's product lines: the DSP IP families (CEVA-X, CEVA-XC), the NeuPro AI processor, the Bluetooth and Wi-Fi platforms, and the RivieraWaves connectivity IP. Understand what IP licensing means commercially and technically. Review Ceva's investor presentations and recent press releases for context on their market positioning.

Week 2: Sharpen your technical stories.
Identify three to five projects from your own background where you solved a customer or integration problem. Map each one to the STAR format. For each story, be ready to go one level deeper on technical detail if the interviewer probes. Practise out loud, not just in your head.

Week 3: Practise customer scenarios.
Role-play the common objection situations: 'Why should I license IP instead of building it myself?', 'Your competitor's IP costs less', 'We need a feature that's not on your roadmap.' Have a clear, honest response for each. Ask a colleague or friend to play the sceptical customer.

Before each round:
Check Ceva's LinkedIn page and recent news for product announcements or partnerships. Review the job description line by line and map your experiences to each listed responsibility. Prepare two or three specific questions that show you have done your homework on the team or the product area.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating it like a pure technical interview. Ceva wants Solutions Engineers who can manage a customer relationship, not just solve an architecture puzzle. Candidates who only discuss technology without once mentioning the customer's perspective typically do not move forward.

Vague STAR answers. Saying 'I helped the team resolve an issue' without specifying what you did, what the root cause was, or what the outcome was leaves interviewers with nothing concrete to evaluate. Name the technology, name the action, name the result.

Bluffing on product knowledge. If you do not know a specific Ceva IP or feature, say so clearly and explain how you would find out. Interviewers who work on these products daily will spot a bluff immediately, and it damages your credibility for the rest of the conversation.

Underselling business impact. Solutions Engineers sit close to revenue. If your work helped close a deal, retain a customer, or shorten an evaluation cycle, say so explicitly. Do not assume the interviewer will infer commercial relevance from a technical story.

Not preparing questions. Candidates who ask nothing at the end of a round signal low engagement. Prepare genuine questions about the team's current customer challenges, how success is measured in the first year, and what collaboration with the product engineering team looks like day to day.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Ceva Solutions Engineer interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically involves 3-5 rounds. These commonly include a recruiter or HR screen, one or two technical rounds covering DSP or embedded concepts, and a final conversation with a senior engineer or hiring manager. Some candidates mention a short take-home exercise or an architecture deep-dive as part of the process, though this varies by team.

Do I need semiconductor or chip design experience to apply?

Deep chip design experience is helpful but not always required. Candidates from embedded software, wireless systems, and pre-sales engineering backgrounds have reported success in the role. What matters most is the ability to earn the trust of hardware engineers while also navigating a commercial relationship. If your background skews toward software, prepare to show that you understand the hardware stack well enough for credible conversations with SoC architects.

What salary can I expect for this role at Ceva in India?

Ceva has not publicly disclosed India-specific compensation bands for Solutions Engineer roles. For market benchmarks, Glassdoor and levels.fyi carry community-reported figures for similar roles at semiconductor IP companies, though sample sizes for India are small. Prepare your expectation based on your experience level and cross-reference it with publicly reported ranges on those platforms before your HR discussion.

How should I prepare for the technical portion of the interview?

Focus on DSP architecture fundamentals (VLIW, memory hierarchies, DMA, interrupt handling), wireless protocol basics relevant to Ceva's portfolio (Bluetooth, Wi-Fi, 5G modem concepts), and SoC integration flows. You do not need to know Ceva's internal tools, but be ready to discuss how you would diagnose a customer's integration issue at a conceptual level. Reading Ceva's publicly available product briefs gives you vocabulary that interviewers will recognise.

Is the role mostly pre-sales or post-sales support?

Candidates and public job descriptions suggest the role spans both. Pre-sales work includes running evaluations and supporting the sales team in competitive situations, while post-sales work covers integration support, issue escalation, and helping customers reach production. The balance varies by team and region, so ask directly during your interview where the current priority sits.
