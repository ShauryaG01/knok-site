01 Overview

Overview

Five9 is a US-listed cloud contact-centre software company, known for its AI-powered voice, digital channel, and workforce-management tools used by enterprise customer-service teams globally. The Technical Program Manager role at Five9 sits at the crossroads of product engineering and business delivery. You own end-to-end program plans, remove blockers for engineering teams, manage cross-functional dependencies, and keep senior stakeholders informed on risks and timelines.

Knok jobradar tracked 313 TPM openings across India as of July 2026, with Bangalore leading at 41 roles, followed by Delhi (14), Pune (13), Hyderabad (12), Chennai (5), and Mumbai (1). Five9 itself carries 165 open roles on the platform, a signal of active expansion across product, engineering, and operations. The TPM interview at Five9 typically spans three to four rounds covering program management depth, technical credibility in cloud environments, leadership and stakeholder situations, and sometimes a take-home program-planning exercise. Candidates report the process is structured but conversational, with a strong focus on real examples from your own work.

Salary data for Five9 TPM roles in India is limited on public platforms. Glassdoor and levels.fyi are the most useful references for current figures from candidates who have recently interviewed or joined.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up consistently in Five9 TPM interviews, based on what candidates report across review platforms.

  1. Walk me through a large-scale technical program you managed end to end. What was your process from kickoff to delivery?
  2. Five9's platform spans voice, digital channels, and AI tools. How do you get up to speed on a technical domain you are new to?
  3. How do you handle scope creep when engineering teams are already at capacity?
  4. Describe a time two senior stakeholders disagreed on a technical direction. How did you resolve it?
  5. How do you track and communicate program health to executives? What signals matter most to you?
  6. Tell me about a program that was at serious risk of missing its deadline. What did you do?
  7. How do you prioritise across multiple concurrent programs when you cannot staff all of them adequately?
  8. How comfortable are you going deep on API integrations, SLA requirements, or cloud scalability trade-offs with engineering teams?
  9. Describe how you keep a program roadmap accurate as priorities shift week to week.
  10. Tell me about a time you pushed back on product leadership on a feature request or timeline. How did that conversation go?
  11. How do you manage dependencies across teams with different release cadences or sprint cycles?
  12. What does 'done' mean to you for a technical program, and how do you drive quality without owning QA directly?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a program that was at serious risk of missing its deadline.

*Situation:* I was leading a platform migration for a B2B SaaS product. Three weeks before our external launch date, the data-pipeline team flagged that a critical third-party API had changed its contract and our integration would fail in production.

*Task:* I needed to protect the launch date for two enterprise customers who had contractual go-live commitments, while giving engineering enough time to fix the integration properly.

*Action:* I called an immediate war-room session with the engineering lead, product owner, and customer-success manager. We mapped two options: a short-term patch and a full re-architecture. I negotiated with customer success to phase the rollout, launching customers on features unaffected by the broken API first. I created a daily status note for the VP and set clear decision checkpoints so no one was surprised.

*Result:* We hit the contractual go-live date for both customers. The patch held through the re-architecture window and the full fix shipped on the planned date. The VP cited the communication cadence as a model for future escalations.

---

Q: Describe a time two senior stakeholders disagreed on a technical direction.

*Situation:* During a cloud infrastructure consolidation program, the VP of Engineering wanted to re-platform on a single cloud provider for cost reasons, while the Chief Architect preferred a multi-cloud approach for resilience.

*Task:* My job was to keep the program moving without taking sides, and to get a decision made in time for the quarterly planning deadline.

*Action:* I facilitated a structured decision session. I asked each stakeholder to document their top three concerns and the evidence behind them, then built a comparison table mapping each option against cost, resilience, timeline, and team capability. I surfaced a middle path: single-cloud for the core platform with a documented exit strategy if vendor risk grew. I shared the framing with both stakeholders before the meeting so no one felt ambushed.

*Result:* The team aligned on the middle path in one session. The decision was documented and approved the same week, keeping the program timeline intact.

---

Q: How do you handle scope creep when engineering teams are at capacity?

*Situation:* Mid-sprint, a product manager requested adding a new reporting feature to an already-locked release, citing a request from a key enterprise account.

*Task:* I had to protect engineering bandwidth while keeping the customer relationship intact and avoiding conflict between product and engineering.

*Action:* I set up a quick trade-off session with the PM and engineering lead. I asked the PM to rank the new feature against the three items already in scope using an impact-versus-effort grid. One lower-priority item was a clear candidate to defer. I documented the change formally in the program log and updated the release notes so all stakeholders had visibility.

*Result:* The new feature shipped in that release without an extended sprint. The PM appreciated the structured trade-off approach and we used the same grid for two more scope-change requests later that quarter.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions. Every situation question at Five9 expects a clear Situation, Task, Action, Result structure. Keep the Situation and Task brief (two to three sentences each) and spend most of your time on the Action. Results should describe real outcomes: shipped on time, reduced escalations, got executive sign-off.

RACI for stakeholder questions. When asked how you manage stakeholders or dependencies, reference a RACI or responsibility matrix explicitly. It signals that you manage accountability in a structured way rather than by feel.

Risk register for delivery questions. For questions about program health or deadline risk, describe how you maintain a living risk log with likelihood, impact, owner, and mitigation for each item. Candidates report that Five9 interviewers respond well to structured risk vocabulary.

Trade-off framing for prioritisation questions. When asked how you prioritise, avoid generic answers. Describe the axes you use: customer impact, engineering effort, strategic alignment, and time sensitivity. A simple two-by-two grid (impact versus effort) shows you think rigorously rather than going by gut feel.

Executive summary format for communication questions. Describe a consistent structure for your stakeholder updates: current status, key decisions needed, risks on the horizon, and next milestones. This matches what Five9 leadership teams typically expect from a TPM.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Ownership without authority. Five9 TPM interviewers consistently probe whether you drive outcomes through influence rather than direct command. They want to hear you take responsibility for program success even when you do not control the engineering team.

Technical credibility. You do not need to write code, but you do need to hold real conversations about API design, cloud architecture trade-offs, SLA definitions, and release engineering. Candidates who deflect all technical questions to 'that is the engineer's call' typically do not advance past the early rounds.

Structured communication. Five9 runs programs across time zones and functions. Interviewers look for candidates who communicate proactively, set a predictable update cadence, and escalate early rather than late.

Comfort with ambiguity. The role involves navigating changing product priorities and incomplete information. Interviewers test whether you can make reasonable decisions with limited data, document your assumptions, and course-correct as you learn more.

Customer empathy. Five9 sells to enterprise customers with strict uptime SLAs. TPM candidates who connect program decisions to customer impact, not just internal metrics, tend to stand out.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know Five9's product and business context. Read Five9's investor materials and recent press to understand their AI strategy, major product lines (CX Cloud, Agent Assist, Workforce Management), and key competitors. Understand at a basic level what a cloud contact centre does: how outbound dialers, IVR systems, and omnichannel routing work. This comes up in domain-fit questions.

Week 2: Build your story bank. List six to eight programs you have owned. Cover at least one major deadline crisis, one stakeholder conflict, one scope-change situation, and one cross-team dependency challenge. Write each in STAR format and practise speaking them aloud within two minutes per story.

Week 3: Sharpen your frameworks. Practise explaining how you build a program plan, maintain a risk register, run a RACI, and structure a weekly status update. Be ready to sketch a simple roadmap or milestone table in a shared doc during the interview itself.

Week 4: Mock interviews and final research. Do at least two timed mock interviews with a peer or mentor. Check Glassdoor and Blind for recent Five9 interview reports to spot questions that come up frequently. Review the job description line by line and map each requirement to a specific story from your background.

On the day: Prepare two or three thoughtful questions about Five9's current programs, how the TPM function is organised, and what success looks like in the first six months. This signals genuine interest and strategic thinking.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Being too vague in STAR answers. Saying 'I managed a complex program' without specific timelines, team dynamics, or outcomes tells interviewers nothing. Be concrete, even if the details are approximate.

Skipping the technical layer. Some TPM candidates over-index on process and avoid all technical detail. At Five9, a cloud-product company, you need to show you can engage meaningfully with engineering teams on architecture and integration topics.

Not knowing Five9's product. Treating Five9 as a generic tech company is a consistent miss. Interviewers expect you to know what Five9 sells, who their customers are, and how a TPM fits into that delivery model.

Waiting to be asked about risks. Strong TPM candidates proactively raise risks in their stories rather than only mentioning them when asked directly. A STAR answer with no risk or mitigation element suggests you only managed programs under ideal conditions.

Talking about what the team did instead of what you did. Use 'I' for your actions and 'we' for team outcomes. Interviewers need to understand your specific contribution, not just the group result.

A weak Result. Candidates often give a strong Situation and Action but end with 'the project launched'. Describe the business impact: customer satisfaction improved, escalations dropped, leadership approved the approach. The Result is what the interviewer remembers.

How many rounds does a Five9 TPM interview typically have?

Candidates report three to four rounds, though Five9 has not published an official process. A typical sequence includes an initial recruiter screen, one or two rounds with engineering or product managers covering both technical depth and behavioural questions, and a final round with senior leadership. Some candidates also mention a take-home program-planning exercise, though this is not universal.

Does Five9 ask coding or whiteboard questions for the TPM role?

Generally no. Candidates report that Five9 TPM interviews do not require you to write code. However, you should be ready to discuss system design concepts, API integrations, cloud scaling trade-offs, and SLA definitions at a conceptual level. Technical credibility matters even when hands-on coding does not.

Five9 does not publish India-specific salary bands publicly. Glassdoor and levels.fyi are your best sources for current figures from people who have recently joined or interviewed there. How much should I know about the contact-centre industry before interviewing?
