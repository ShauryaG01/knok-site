01 Overview

Overview

Celigo is a cloud-based integration platform (iPaaS) that connects business apps like Salesforce, NetSuite, and Shopify through automated workflows. Their DevOps engineers support a multi-tenant SaaS product that runs integrations for enterprise customers around the clock, making reliability, deployment velocity, and security all critical concerns. As of mid-2026, knok's job radar shows 2 open DevOps roles at Celigo.

The interview process typically spans 3-4 rounds: a recruiter or HR screen, a technical phone or video interview, a practical assignment or live system design session, and a final panel with senior engineers or an engineering manager. Candidates report the process taking a few weeks from application to offer. The technical focus areas are cloud infrastructure (AWS is commonly cited for iPaaS companies at this scale), Kubernetes, CI/CD pipeline design, and observability. Expect questions about reliability engineering and incident response alongside standard DevOps tooling, since Celigo's customers run business-critical processes on the platform.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Here are the questions candidates at Celigo and similar iPaaS companies most commonly report encountering in DevOps interviews:

  1. Walk us through how you would design a CI/CD pipeline for a microservices-based SaaS product with multiple teams deploying daily.
  2. Celigo's platform runs customer integrations that need near-continuous uptime. How do you approach zero-downtime deployments?
  3. Describe your hands-on experience managing Kubernetes clusters in production. How do you handle node scaling, pod disruption budgets, and cluster upgrades?
  4. How would you set up observability, covering logs, metrics, and distributed traces, for a high-volume API integration platform?
  5. Walk us through your approach to secrets management in a multi-tenant cloud environment.
  6. How have you used Terraform or a similar infrastructure-as-code tool? What does your remote state management strategy look like?
  7. Describe a production incident you owned end to end. How did you detect it, contain it, and conduct the post-mortem?
  8. How do you build security scanning (container image scanning, SAST, dependency checks) into a CI/CD pipeline without slowing down releases?
  9. What is your approach to managing database schema migrations safely inside an automated deployment pipeline?
  10. How do you balance cost optimization with performance and reliability in a cloud environment?
  11. How would you structure on-call rotations and runbooks for a small but growing DevOps team?
  12. Describe your experience with GitOps workflows. How does it differ from traditional CI/CD, and when would you prefer one over the other?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

These three answers use the STAR format. Adapt the specifics to your own experience.

Q: Describe a time you improved deployment frequency without sacrificing reliability.

*Situation:* At my previous company, the team deployed to production once a week because manual QA and approval steps were bottlenecks. Releases were stressful and occasionally caused rollbacks.

*Task:* My goal was to move to daily deployments by automating quality gates, without increasing production incidents.

*Action:* I introduced automated integration tests as a mandatory CI gate, added canary deployments using Argo Rollouts so new versions received a small slice of traffic first, set up automated rollback triggers tied to error-rate thresholds, and replaced the manual sign-off step with a lightweight Slack approval bot.

*Result:* Within two months we were deploying to production daily. The rollback rate actually dropped because problems were caught earlier and affected fewer users before automated rollback kicked in.

---

Q: Walk us through a production incident you owned.

*Situation:* A memory leak in one of our Node.js microservices caused pods to restart every few hours, triggering brief API timeouts for end users.

*Task:* I was on-call and needed to identify the root cause, reduce customer impact immediately, and prevent recurrence.

*Action:* I first increased the pod memory limit and replica count to stabilize the service while I investigated. I used Prometheus and Grafana to correlate memory growth with a specific API endpoint added in the previous release. I worked with the dev team to add heap profiling, which confirmed an object accumulation bug. We patched and deployed within four hours, then updated our runbook and added a memory-growth alert as a permanent monitor.

*Result:* End-user impact was contained to a brief window during the incident. The same alert has since caught similar issues in other services before they reached customers.

---

Q: How have you used Terraform to manage cloud infrastructure?

*Situation:* Our infrastructure had been built by clicking through the AWS console over two years, with no version control. Changes were risky and environments drifted from each other.

*Task:* I was asked to bring all infrastructure under infrastructure-as-code as part of a reliability initiative.

*Action:* I imported existing AWS resources into Terraform state using 'terraform import', organized the codebase into reusable modules covering VPC, EKS cluster, RDS, and IAM, stored remote state in S3 with DynamoDB locking, and wired Terraform plan and apply into the CI pipeline with required code review before any apply.

*Result:* Within three months, all three environments were managed through code. Environment drift incidents dropped to zero, and new environments could be provisioned in under 30 minutes.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for experience questions: Lead with context (Situation), state what you personally owned (Task), walk through what you specifically did step by step (Action), and close with a concrete outcome (Result). The Action step is where most candidates go vague. Name the actual tools, commands, and configurations you used rather than speaking in generalities.

For system design or 'how would you' questions: Think out loud. State your assumptions first (expected scale, team size, cloud provider), then structure your answer in layers: pipeline or architecture design first, observability second, security and cost controls third. Since Celigo's platform is multi-tenant and API-heavy, mentioning reliability patterns like canary releases, circuit breakers, and rate limiting shows you understand the product context, not just generic DevOps.

For trade-off or opinion questions: Acknowledge that both options have merit, explain the key trade-off, then state your preference with a reason tied to real experience. Interviewers at product companies test engineering judgment, not whether you know a single correct answer.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Hands-on cloud depth: Celigo runs on the cloud and their platform is always on. Interviewers want engineers who can describe not just what a tool does but how they configured, debugged, and operated it in production. 'I have used Kubernetes' is far weaker than 'I managed a production EKS cluster and handled node group rolling upgrades using PodDisruptionBudgets.'

Reliability mindset: Because Celigo's customers run business-critical integrations, interviewers pay close attention to how you think about failure. Expect follow-up questions like 'what would break first?' or 'how would you know something had gone wrong?'

Ownership and communication: DevOps engineers at a mid-sized SaaS company work across multiple engineering teams. Candidates who explain technical decisions clearly to non-DevOps engineers and who show end-to-end ownership of incidents tend to stand out.

Security awareness: With enterprise customers, compliance and security are not afterthoughts. Knowing where secrets live, how container images are scanned, and how IAM roles are scoped shows you understand the responsibility that comes with production access.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Follow this sequence in the week before your Celigo interview:

Days 1-2: Review the job description line by line and map each requirement to a specific project from your own experience. Write two or three STAR bullet points for each requirement. If Kubernetes or Terraform is listed, prepare to go deep, not just surface level.

Days 3-4: Practice system design out loud. Pick a scenario like 'design a CI/CD pipeline for a multi-tenant SaaS API' and talk through it for ten minutes without stopping. Record yourself if possible. Notice where you go vague and fill those gaps before the interview.

Day 5: Research Celigo's product. Understand what iPaaS means, how connectors and flows work, and what reliability or scale challenges their platform likely faces. This context helps you give answers that feel relevant rather than generic.

Day 6: Prepare three strong questions for your interviewers. Good examples include asking about the team's biggest current reliability challenge, how on-call is structured, and what the deployment frequency looks like today.

Day 7: Rest, review your notes once, and test your setup (camera, audio, Zoom or Google Meet) the evening before.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Staying at the tool-name level: Saying 'I know Docker and Kubernetes' without describing how you used them in production is a common reason candidates do not move forward. Name the specific problem you solved and the outcome you achieved.

Skipping the Result in STAR answers: Many candidates tell a detailed story but forget to close with a measurable or observable outcome. Even 'deployment time roughly halved' or 'we had zero rollback incidents that quarter' is far stronger than trailing off at the end.

Treating security and cost as bonus topics: At a product company like Celigo, security and cloud cost are part of every infrastructure decision. Candidates who treat them as optional extras rather than core engineering values tend to score lower.

Not asking questions: Candidates who ask nothing signal low interest. Interviewers typically want to see genuine curiosity about the team's real challenges and how the role fits into the broader engineering org.

Over-preparing for algorithms: DevOps interviews at companies like Celigo focus far more on systems, operations, and past experience than on LeetCode-style problems. Spend your prep time on practical scenarios and incident walkthroughs, not sorting algorithms.
