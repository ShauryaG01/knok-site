01 Overview

Overview

Fujitsu is actively hiring DevOps Engineers across India, with 26 open roles tracked by knok jobradar as of July 2026. Out of 811 total DevOps openings nationally, the heaviest city concentrations sit in Bangalore (187 roles across all employers), Delhi (40), and Pune (37).

Fujitsu is a Japanese multinational with strong India delivery centers, specialising in IT services, cloud migration, managed infrastructure, and enterprise digital transformation. Their DevOps teams typically work on large enterprise accounts, meaning you will deal with regulated environments, legacy modernisation, and strict change-management processes alongside modern toolchains.

Candidates report a process that typically includes a technical screening call, one or two hands-on technical rounds, and a managerial or HR discussion. Questions tend to mix practical tool knowledge (Kubernetes, Terraform, Jenkins or similar CI/CD tools) with scenario-based and behavioral questions. Soft skills like communication and stakeholder management matter more here than at a pure product startup, given the client-facing nature of much of Fujitsu's work.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates for Fujitsu DevOps Engineer roles commonly report questions across these themes:

  1. Walk me through a CI/CD pipeline you designed and owned end to end.
  2. How do you manage infrastructure as code for large, multi-environment setups?
  3. Describe your hands-on experience with Kubernetes, including how you handle scaling and failure scenarios.
  4. How have you handled a production outage? What was your incident response process?
  5. What monitoring and observability stack have you worked with, and how did you set alerting thresholds?
  6. How do you embed security into your pipelines (shift-left or DevSecOps practices)?
  7. Fujitsu serves enterprise clients with compliance needs. How have you managed audit trails, access controls, or regulatory requirements in a pipeline?
  8. Describe a time you reduced deployment lead time or improved release frequency for a team.
  9. How do you manage secrets and credentials in a multi-cloud or hybrid environment?
  10. How would you approach migrating an on-premises monolith to the cloud while keeping the business running?
  11. How do you work with development and QA teams to improve pipeline reliability without slowing them down?
  12. What is your approach to cost optimisation on cloud infrastructure?

Fujitsu interviews typically emphasise enterprise scenarios over startup-scale problems, so frame your answers around reliability, compliance, and cross-team communication.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk me through a CI/CD pipeline you designed and owned end to end.

*Situation:* My team had a Java microservices application where releases were done manually every two weeks. Deployments frequently failed in production because testing in staging was inconsistent.

*Task:* I was asked to design and own a fully automated CI/CD pipeline that would allow us to deploy multiple times per week with confidence.

*Action:* I built a pipeline in Jenkins (later migrated to GitHub Actions) with stages for code build, unit tests, integration tests against a containerised test database, static code analysis via SonarQube, Docker image build and push to our private registry, and automated deployment to staging via Helm charts on Kubernetes. I added a manual approval gate before production. I wrote all pipeline configuration as code in version control so the pipeline itself was reviewable and auditable.

*Result:* Deployment frequency went from fortnightly to multiple times per week. Production rollback time dropped significantly because every release was a versioned Helm chart. The team could trace exactly which code change triggered which deployment, which mattered for our enterprise client's audit requirements.

---

Q: How have you handled a production outage? What was your incident response process?

*Situation:* A critical API service began failing for about a third of requests on a Friday evening. Alerts fired within minutes, but the error messages pointed to a database connection issue that turned out to be a symptom, not the root cause.

*Task:* As the on-call engineer, I needed to restore service quickly and then find the real root cause to prevent recurrence.

*Action:* I checked our Grafana dashboards to correlate the timing of the spike with any recent deployments or infrastructure events. I found a deployment had gone out two hours earlier. I rolled back the Helm release to the previous version, which immediately reduced errors. I then compared the new version's configuration with the old one, finding a misconfigured connection pool size that caused the database to be overwhelmed under load. I fixed the configuration, added a test for it in our pipeline, and documented the incident with a timeline and action items.

*Result:* Service was restored within about half an hour of the alert firing. The post-mortem action items were completed within the next sprint, and the same class of misconfiguration has not recurred since.

---

Q: How do you embed security into your pipelines?

*Situation:* My previous team had no security scanning in CI. A dependency vulnerability was discovered in a third-party library only after a client's security audit flagged it.

*Task:* I was asked to add automated security checks to our pipeline so we could catch issues earlier, ideally before code merged to main.

*Action:* I integrated three layers of security tooling into our pipeline: a secrets scanner (Gitleaks) on every commit to block accidental credential leaks, a software composition analysis tool (OWASP Dependency-Check) to flag vulnerable dependencies, and a container image scanner (Trivy) in the Docker build stage. I configured these to fail the build on high-severity findings and generate reports for lower-severity ones so the team could triage without being blocked constantly. I also worked with the security team to define acceptable risk thresholds per environment.

*Result:* Within the first month, the secrets scanner caught two accidental API key commits before they reached the repository history. The dependency scanner flagged a critical CVE in a logging library before it ever shipped to production.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For technical questions about tools, architecture, or processes, use a three-part structure: what you did, why you made that choice, and what you would do differently now. Interviewers at enterprise-focused companies like Fujitsu want to see that you can justify your decisions, not just name tools.

For behavioral questions, use the STAR format consistently: Situation (brief context), Task (your specific responsibility), Action (what you personally did, not what the team did), and Result (measurable or observable outcome). Keep the Situation short and spend most of your time on Action and Result.

For system design or 'how would you approach X' questions, start by clarifying requirements and constraints before jumping to a solution. Ask about scale, compliance needs, existing tooling, and team size. Fujitsu's enterprise context means compliance and auditability are often implicit requirements worth surfacing early.

For incident or debugging questions, walk through your thinking out loud. Interviewers want to see your diagnostic process, not just the answer. Show that you start with observability data, narrow down hypotheses, and avoid changing multiple things at once.

A simple self-check before answering: pause for two to three seconds, outline your answer in one sentence, then expand. This prevents rambling and signals structured thinking.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Fujitsu's DevOps interviews typically prioritise a few qualities specific to their delivery model:

Enterprise context awareness. Fujitsu runs large managed services and transformation engagements for banks, government bodies, and global corporations. They want engineers who understand that change management, compliance documentation, and rollback plans are not optional extras but core parts of the job.

Breadth across the stack. Candidates report that Fujitsu interviewers tend to probe across cloud platforms, containerisation, CI/CD, monitoring, and scripting rather than going very deep on one tool. You are expected to be conversant in multiple areas, even if your strongest depth is in one.

Communication and collaboration. Because DevOps engineers at Fujitsu often work embedded in client delivery teams, interviewers look for people who can explain technical decisions to non-technical stakeholders and push back constructively when requirements create operational risk.

Ownership and reliability. Past experience running on-call, owning SLAs, and improving reliability metrics resonates strongly. Phrases like 'I owned this end to end' and 'here is what I would have done differently' signal maturity.

Practical tool knowledge. Be ready to discuss real configurations, not just conceptual knowledge. If you say you have used Kubernetes, expect follow-up questions on how you handled node failures, pod scheduling, or resource limits.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Phase 1: Core technical review (first week)

Review your fundamentals across CI/CD (Jenkins, GitHub Actions, or GitLab CI), containerisation (Docker and Kubernetes including Helm), infrastructure as code (Terraform and Ansible are most commonly cited in Fujitsu job descriptions), and scripting in Python or Bash. For each tool, make sure you can describe a real project where you used it, not just define what it does.

Phase 2: Company and enterprise context (second week)

Research Fujitsu's publicly known service offerings and cloud partnerships. Review the specific job description carefully and map your experience to each listed requirement. Prepare two or three STAR stories for each major competency area: incident management, pipeline design, security, and collaboration with developers.

Phase 3: Final preparation (last two days before interview)

Do a mock interview with a peer or record yourself answering the most asked questions listed above. Check Glassdoor and similar platforms for recent candidate reports on the Fujitsu DevOps interview process. Prepare two or three thoughtful questions to ask the interviewer about the team's current toolchain, on-call practices, and how they measure deployment success.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Listing tools without showing depth. Saying 'I have experience with Kubernetes' without being able to describe a real production scenario is a common reason candidates do not progress. Be ready to go one level deeper on anything you list.

Treating the process as purely technical. Fujitsu interviews include behavioral rounds that carry real weight. Candidates who prepare only for technical questions are often caught off guard when asked about conflict resolution, stakeholder communication, or handling ambiguous requirements.

Vague results in STAR answers. 'Deployment got faster' is weak. 'Deployment frequency increased from fortnightly to multiple times per week' is strong. Use concrete, observable outcomes even when exact numbers are not available.

Not asking questions. Candidates who ask nothing are often perceived as disengaged. Prepare at least two genuine questions about the team or the role.

Overselling tool breadth while underselling ownership. Enterprise employers like Fujitsu value engineers who owned something, maintained it, and dealt with its failures. A single deep ownership story is more compelling than a long list of tools you have touched briefly.

Ignoring the enterprise compliance angle. If you have experience with audit trails, change management, or regulated environments, bring it up. Many candidates from startup backgrounds skip this entirely, missing a chance to differentiate themselves.

How many rounds does the Fujitsu DevOps interview typically have?

Candidates report the process typically involves a technical screening call, one or two technical interview rounds covering tools and scenarios, and a final HR or managerial discussion. The exact number of rounds varies by level and team. Some candidates report an additional system design round for senior positions.

What salary can I expect as a DevOps Engineer at Fujitsu in India?

Based on knok jobradar data, DevOps Engineer salaries in India broadly fall in ranges of 6-12 LPA at entry level (0-2 years), 15-28 LPA at mid level (3-5 years), 30-50 LPA at senior level (6-9 years), and 45-70+ LPA at lead or staff level. Fujitsu-specific compensation is not publicly disclosed in detail, and Glassdoor reviews for the company show a wide band depending on business unit and client account. Use these ranges as a reference point, not a guarantee.

Does Fujitsu ask coding or scripting questions in DevOps interviews?

Candidates commonly report scripting questions in Python or Bash, such as writing a script to parse logs, automate a deployment task, or check system health. Pure data structures and algorithm questions are less commonly reported for DevOps roles at Fujitsu compared to product companies. Focus your scripting prep on automation and operational tasks rather than competitive programming.

Is Kubernetes knowledge required for Fujitsu DevOps roles?

Kubernetes appears frequently in Fujitsu DevOps job descriptions and is commonly cited in candidate interview reports. You should be comfortable with core concepts like deployments, services, config maps, and resource limits. Hands-on experience with Helm and cluster troubleshooting is a plus. That said, not every Fujitsu DevOps role is Kubernetes-heavy, so check the specific job description you are applying to.

How important is cloud experience for Fujitsu DevOps interviews?

Cloud experience is important, with AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud all publicly reported as active areas for Fujitsu. Experience on any major cloud platform is valuable. Interviewers typically focus on how you have used cloud services in real projects rather than testing certification knowledge.
