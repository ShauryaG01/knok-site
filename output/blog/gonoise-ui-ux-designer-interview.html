01 Overview

Overview

Gonoise (the brand behind Noise) is one of India's fastest-growing consumer electronics companies, known for smartwatches, truly wireless earbuds, and fitness trackers. The company competes with global players and invests heavily in product design to stand out on platforms like Amazon and Flipkart. As of July 2026, knok's jobradar shows 20 open roles at gonoise across all positions, and 29 UI/UX Designer openings nationally, with Delhi (8 openings) and Bangalore (6 openings) leading the list.

As a UI/UX Designer at gonoise, you would typically work on the NoiseFit companion mobile app, device pairing flows, health tracking dashboards, and digital touchpoints used by millions of Indian consumers daily.

Candidates report a process that typically runs 3-4 rounds: a portfolio review or take-home design task, one or two rounds of design critique and case-study discussion, and a final round focused on culture fit. The panel typically includes a design lead and a product manager. Expect questions that probe both your craft and your product thinking.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions are commonly reported by candidates for UI/UX Designer roles at consumer electronics companies similar to gonoise. Specific phrasing may vary, but the themes recur.

  1. Walk us through a product in your portfolio where you improved a user flow. What was the problem and what did you change?
  2. Gonoise products reach a wide range of users across India, including first-time smartwatch buyers. How do you design for users with low digital literacy?
  3. How would you redesign the NoiseFit app's onboarding to reduce drop-off during new device pairing?
  4. Describe a time you worked within tight hardware or platform constraints. How did those constraints shape your design decisions?
  5. How do you balance visual appeal with usability when designing for a consumer electronics brand?
  6. Tell us about a design decision that was challenged by engineering. How did you resolve it?
  7. How do you conduct user research when timelines are short and budgets are limited?
  8. Walk us through your process for creating and maintaining a design system or component library.
  9. How would you approach designing a sleep quality tracking feature for the NoiseFit app?
  10. Have you designed for both Android and iOS? How do you keep a consistent brand identity while following each platform's guidelines?
  11. Tell us about a time a usability test revealed something that surprised you. What did you change as a result?
  12. How do you present and justify design decisions to stakeholders who are not designers?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use these STAR-format examples as a guide. Replace the project details with your own real experience.

---

Q: Walk us through a product in your portfolio where you improved a user flow.

*Situation:* At my previous company, we had a mobile app for fitness activity tracking. Analytics showed a high drop-off rate on the goal-setting screen during onboarding.

*Task:* I was asked to find out why users were abandoning that screen and redesign the flow to improve completion.

*Action:* I ran five moderated user tests and found that users felt overwhelmed by the number of goal options shown at once. I redesigned the screen using a progressive disclosure pattern, showing three core goals first with a 'customise further' option below. I also rewrote the copy in plainer, conversational language.

*Result:* An A/B test showed a clear improvement in onboarding completion (per internal product data), and the updated flow shipped within the same sprint.

---

Q: Tell us about a time a usability test revealed something surprising.

*Situation:* I was designing a settings screen for a wearable companion app, similar to NoiseFit, and felt confident the icon-based navigation was intuitive.

*Task:* Before handing off to development, I ran a quick test with five users to validate the design.

*Action:* Three of the five users could not find notification settings because they assumed the bell icon controlled alarms, not app notifications. I went back to the designs, added text labels below each icon, and validated the fix with two more users.

*Result:* Both validation users completed the task without hesitation. The labelled navigation shipped, and we received no support tickets about that screen in the first two weeks after launch.

---

Q: Tell us about a design decision that was challenged by engineering.

*Situation:* I had designed an animated product discovery carousel with custom gesture interactions for a consumer electronics app.

*Task:* The engineering lead flagged that implementing the animation would require a custom library, adding extra time to the sprint.

*Action:* I set up a working session with the engineer to understand the constraint. Together we explored using a standard React Native component with a simplified transition. I adjusted the design to work within that constraint without losing the core interaction feel, then updated the prototype for the product manager to review.

*Result:* The feature shipped on schedule, and the product manager appreciated that the design team had surfaced the trade-off early rather than discovering it during QA.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

For portfolio walkthroughs: use a Problem-Process-Proof structure. Open with the user problem in one sentence, walk through your design process (research, wireframes, iterations), and close with a concrete outcome. Avoid narrating tool usage ('I opened Figma and drew...') and focus instead on the decisions you made and why.

For 'how would you design X' questions: open by naming the specific user you are designing for (for example, a first-time smartwatch buyer in a Tier 2 city vs. a daily fitness enthusiast who tracks workouts). Then state the key job-to-be-done. Then outline your approach. This signals systems thinking rather than jumping straight to visual solutions.

For conflict and collaboration questions: use the STAR method, but make sure the Action step describes what you personally did, not what 'the team' did. Interviewers want to understand your individual contribution and your approach to professional disagreement.

For constraint-based questions: acknowledge the constraint early, explain how you adapted your design to work within it, and highlight what you preserved from the original intent. This signals maturity in working with real-world conditions rather than ideal design scenarios.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Gonoise operates in a fast-moving consumer market where product cycles are short and competition is intense. Interviewers typically look for designers who connect their decisions to user behaviour and business outcomes, not just aesthetics.

Portfolio depth over breadth. Candidates report that interviewers ask probing follow-up questions on one or two projects rather than skimming all of them. Be ready to defend every decision in your chosen case study, including what you would do differently today.

Empathy for the Indian consumer. Gonoise serves a broad user base, including first-time buyers in Tier 2 and Tier 3 cities. If you have designed for users with varying digital literacy or for vernacular language contexts, highlight that experience prominently.

Cross-functional collaboration. The role sits at the intersection of product, engineering, and brand. Interviewers typically want evidence that you can communicate design rationale clearly to non-designers and navigate trade-offs without creating friction.

Product curiosity. Interviewers expect you to have used the NoiseFit app and formed genuine opinions about it. Candidates who arrive with specific, thoughtful observations about the product consistently stand out.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Step 1: Use the product. Download and use the NoiseFit app for at least a week before your interview. Note three things that work well and three things you would improve. Be ready to speak to both with clear reasoning.

Step 2: Prepare focused case studies. Select two or three portfolio projects and prepare to walk through each one end-to-end. Know the user problem, your process, the constraints, and the outcome. Keep each walkthrough to under five minutes.

Step 3: Research the brand. Review gonoise's full product range: smartwatches, earbuds, and accessories. Understand how the NoiseFit app connects to hardware and what features it covers, such as health tracking, sleep monitoring, and notification management.

Step 4: Review platform guidelines. Brush up on Material Design and Apple HIG. Be ready to explain how you would maintain a consistent gonoise brand identity while respecting Android and iOS conventions.

Step 5: Practise presenting out loud. Walk through your designs with someone who is not a designer. This simulates the stakeholder communication scenario that often comes up in interviews and reveals gaps in your explanation before they matter.

Step 6: Prepare questions for the panel. Good ones include: 'How does the design team work with product during a sprint?' and 'What does the user feedback loop look like at gonoise?' Thoughtful questions signal genuine interest in the role.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Presenting too many projects without depth. Candidates report that interviewers prefer one well-explained case study over five shallow ones. Pick your strongest project and know it inside out.

Jumping to solutions in open-ended design questions. Always name the user and state the problem before proposing any UI. Starting with the interface signals that you skip research.

Ignoring the Indian consumer context. Gonoise's core audience includes users in smaller cities who rely on simple, often vernacular interfaces. Designs that assume a power-user mindset can fall flat in the interview discussion.

Using 'we' without clarifying your individual role. Interviewers need to understand what you personally contributed. Be specific about your own decisions within team projects.

Not using the NoiseFit app before the interview. Coming to a product design interview without having used the core product is a common and easily avoidable mistake. Interviewers notice immediately.

Skipping the 'why' behind design choices. Saying 'I used a card layout because it looks clean' is weaker than 'I used a card layout because users in our research scanned vertically and needed chunked, scannable information.' Always connect your choices back to a user insight or clear rationale.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the gonoise UI/UX Designer interview typically have?

Candidates report a process that typically runs 3-4 rounds. This usually includes a portfolio review or design task, one or two design discussion rounds with a design lead and product manager, and a final round focused on culture fit. Round structure can vary by team and hiring urgency, so confirm the details with your recruiter when you get the interview call.

Is there a take-home design assignment?

Many candidates report receiving a brief design challenge, either as a take-home task before the first round or as a live exercise during the interview. The brief typically asks you to redesign a screen or propose a new feature for a consumer electronics product. Treat it as a mini case study: document your process and reasoning, not just the final screens.

What design tools should I know for this role?

Figma is the industry standard and you should be proficient in it, including prototyping and developer handoff workflows. Familiarity with basic user research methods and component-based design thinking is expected. Knowledge of motion design or tools like Lottie is a bonus for consumer electronics apps where micro-interactions and animations matter.

Does gonoise hire freshers or only experienced designers?

Gonoise has posted roles at multiple seniority levels. For junior roles, a strong portfolio with two or three well-documented projects (including academic or freelance work) is typically enough to get through screening. For mid-senior roles, candidates report that direct-to-consumer mobile app experience carries more weight than agency or purely B2B work.

How important is it to know the NoiseFit app before the interview?

Very important. Interviewers expect you to have used the product and formed a genuine opinion on it. Candidates who arrive with specific observations about the app's onboarding, health tracking UX, or device pairing flow consistently make a stronger impression than those with only generic design knowledge. Using the app before your interview is one of the easiest preparation steps you can take.

What salary can I expect for this role at gonoise?

Gonoise does not publish official salary bands. Based on publicly reported data and industry surveys, UI/UX Designer compensation in India varies widely by experience level and location. Platforms like Glassdoor and levels.fyi carry community-submitted figures for specific companies and role levels. Having a clear expectation range ready before the offer stage puts you in a stronger negotiating position.
