02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates who have interviewed at companies similar to Eastencher Software typically report questions across three areas: coding and problem solving, system design, and behavioural. Here are the questions most commonly surfaced:

  1. Write a function to reverse a linked list. Explain your time and space complexity.
  2. How would you design a URL shortener? Walk through your components, data model, and scaling decisions.
  3. Tell me about a time you debugged a difficult production issue. What steps did you take?
  4. How do you handle disagreements with a teammate or senior engineer about a technical approach?
  5. Explain the difference between a stack and a queue. Where would you use each in a real project?
  6. Design a notification service that sends emails and push alerts. How do you ensure reliability?
  7. What does RESTful mean, and how would you version an API that is already in production?
  8. Tell me about a project where you had to learn a new technology under pressure. What happened?
  9. How do you write unit tests for code that depends on an external database or API?
  10. Describe a feature you built end-to-end. What trade-offs did you make?
  11. Given an array of integers, find the pair that sums to a target value. What is the optimal approach?
  12. How do you prioritise when you have multiple tasks and a tight deadline?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Below are three STAR-format answers for common Software Engineer interview questions. Adapt the details to your own experience.

Q: Tell me about a time you debugged a difficult production issue.

*Situation:* Our backend service began returning errors intermittently for a subset of API calls after a recent deployment.

*Task:* I was the on-call engineer and needed to identify the root cause quickly to restore normal service.

*Action:* I pulled the application logs, compared the error timestamps against the deployment timeline, and isolated the issue to a new database query that was missing an index. I added the index on a read replica first to confirm the fix, then applied it to the primary database during a low-traffic window.

*Result:* Error rates dropped immediately after the fix. I wrote a post-mortem and added a query-performance check to the CI pipeline so the same class of issue would be caught before deployment in future.

---

Q: Describe a feature you built end-to-end.

*Situation:* The product team needed a CSV export feature for a reporting dashboard. Users had been requesting it for several months and the backlog item had stalled.

*Task:* I picked up the ticket independently and was responsible for design, implementation, and delivery.

*Action:* I mapped out the data flow, chose a background job approach so large exports would not time out the browser, and coordinated with the frontend engineer on the download experience. I wrote unit tests for the export logic and ran a manual end-to-end test with a realistic dataset before raising the pull request.

*Result:* The feature shipped within the sprint. The product manager reported positive feedback from the customer success team, and the export format became the basis for additional reporting features later that quarter.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you had to learn a new technology under pressure.

*Situation:* Our team decided mid-project to switch from REST to GraphQL for a new mobile client, a technology I had not used before.

*Task:* I was assigned the backend GraphQL schema and resolvers, with the same deadline as the original plan.

*Action:* I spent the first few days reading the official documentation and building a small proof-of-concept on a separate branch. I then migrated incrementally, resolver by resolver, keeping the REST endpoints live until the GraphQL equivalents were tested. I asked a senior colleague to review my schema design before committing to it.

*Result:* I delivered the GraphQL layer on time. The mobile team reported that query flexibility significantly reduced their round trips, and the experience gave me a foundation I have used on later projects.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use STAR for every behavioural question. STAR stands for Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task brief so you spend most of your time on Action and Result. Interviewers are listening for what *you* specifically did, not what 'we' as a team did.

For coding questions, think out loud. Before writing any code, state your understanding of the problem, your initial approach, and its trade-offs. Candidates report that interviewers at Eastencher Software value clear communication as much as the correct solution.

For system design, structure your answer in layers. Start with requirements (functional first, then non-functional), then move to a high-level diagram, then drill into the components the interviewer shows most interest in. Avoid jumping to a specific database or framework without first justifying the choice.

For trade-off questions, give both sides. If asked 'which approach would you choose', name the upside and the downside of each option before giving your recommendation. This shows engineering maturity.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Candidates report that Eastencher Software interviewers look for three things above all:

Clear thinking under pressure. Can you break an unfamiliar problem into smaller pieces and make progress without being told exactly what to do? This shows up in coding rounds when the problem is harder than it looks at first.

Ownership and follow-through. Behavioural questions are designed to check whether you finish what you start, flag risks early, and own your mistakes. Answers that say 'I raised the concern but the manager decided otherwise' are weaker than answers that show you influenced the outcome.

Practical engineering judgment. Interviewers want to see that you can make reasonable trade-offs, not just recite theory. For system design, they are looking for decisions you can defend, not a perfect architecture.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Coding fundamentals. Practise arrays, strings, hash maps, and linked lists. These are the most commonly tested topics in screening rounds, according to candidates who have gone through similar processes.

Week 2: System design. Study how to design a rate limiter, a messaging queue, and a read-heavy database-backed service. Focus on explaining trade-offs clearly rather than memorising a single 'correct' design.

Week 3: Behavioural and role-specific prep. Write out several stories from your past work using the STAR format. Cover: a technical challenge you solved, a conflict you navigated, a deadline you hit or missed and what you learned, and a time you picked up something new quickly.

Day before: Review Eastencher Software's public presence (company website, LinkedIn, any recent news). Be ready to answer 'Why Eastencher Software?' with something specific, not a generic answer about growth or culture.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping into code before thinking. Many candidates start typing the moment they read the problem. Take a moment to restate the problem and ask a clarifying question first. This alone puts you ahead of most candidates.

Vague STAR answers. Saying 'I helped improve the system' tells the interviewer nothing. Be specific about what *you* did, what decision *you* made, and what changed as a result.

Ignoring edge cases. When you write a function, walk through at least one edge case out loud (empty input, single element, negative numbers). Candidates report that skipping this is one of the fastest ways to lose points in a coding round.

Not asking questions about the role. Interviewers typically expect you to have a few questions ready. Asking nothing signals low interest. Ask about the team's current technical challenges, the onboarding process, or how success is measured in the first few months.

Overselling or underselling experience. Match your language to your actual level. If you are applying for a mid-level role, do not describe your work as if you were a principal engineer, and do not downplay it either.
