01 Overview

Overview

OKX is a global cryptocurrency exchange and Web3 platform with a large international user base. Their product teams design for one of the most demanding spaces in tech: real-time trading dashboards, crypto wallets, DeFi tools, and onboarding flows that must work for both complete beginners and professional traders at the same time.

As of July 2026, knok's jobradar shows 29 UI/UX Designer openings across India. Delhi leads with 8 positions, Bangalore has 6, Mumbai has 4, and Hyderabad and Pune each have 1 open role. OKX currently has 305 open roles in India overall, a signal of active, sustained hiring rather than a one-off sprint.

Designers at OKX typically work on cross-functional squads alongside product managers and engineers. You will be expected to own end-to-end design, from user research and wireframes through to final handoff and iteration based on metrics. Candidates report that interviews test both your craft (portfolio and process) and your understanding of fintech or crypto products specifically.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates who have interviewed at OKX for design roles report a mix of portfolio deep-dives, behavioural questions, and product-thinking exercises. Typically, you can expect several rounds covering different aspects of your work.

  1. Walk us through a complex financial or data-heavy product you designed. How did you make it simpler for first-time users?
  2. OKX serves both first-time crypto buyers and professional traders. How do you design a single interface that works for such different users?
  3. Describe a time you redesigned a confusing or high-drop-off flow. What research methods did you use, and what was the outcome?
  4. How do you approach designing real-time data displays, such as order books, candlestick charts, or portfolio dashboards, without overwhelming the user?
  5. Tell us about a time you pushed back on a product or business requirement because it would hurt the user experience. How did you handle that conversation?
  6. How do you ensure design consistency across web, iOS, and Android when building or contributing to a design system?
  7. Describe your process for conducting user research inside a fast-moving team where timelines are tight.
  8. Walk us through a time you used data or testing to validate or change a design decision.
  9. Fintech products must meet regulatory and compliance requirements that vary by country. How would you incorporate those constraints into your design without making the product feel clunky?
  10. Describe a technically constrained project where you had to compromise on the ideal design. How did you collaborate with engineers to reach the right solution?
  11. How do you stay current with trends in crypto, Web3, or fintech product design, and how have you applied any of them in your work?
  12. Pick your strongest portfolio piece and walk us through the full process, from brief to shipped.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk us through a time you redesigned a high-drop-off flow.

*Situation:* At my previous company, our mobile KYC (know-your-customer) flow had a publicly reported drop-off problem. Internal tracking showed most users abandoned on the document upload step.

*Task:* I was asked to redesign the flow to improve completion before a major product launch.

*Action:* I ran a set of moderated usability tests and discovered users were confused about which document formats were accepted and why the process asked for a selfie. I rewrote the microcopy, added inline guidance, broke the upload step into smaller stages, and added a progress indicator so users could see how close they were to finishing. I also added a 'why we need this' tooltip to address trust concerns.

*Result:* After we shipped, the team tracked completion rates over several weeks. Industry surveys commonly note that clearer KYC copy and a progress indicator yield meaningful improvements in completion. Our internal data reflected that trend, and the PM highlighted the redesign in the quarterly review.

---

Q: Tell us about a time you pushed back on a product requirement for the sake of UX.

*Situation:* A product manager wanted to add several promotional banners to the home screen of our trading app to surface new features quickly.

*Task:* My job was to design the home screen, but I felt multiple banners would clutter the interface and bury the primary actions users came for.

*Action:* I pulled session recordings showing that users who saw more than one interstitial element in their first session had lower retention the following week. I presented this data to the PM, proposed a single rotating banner with a clear dismiss option, and showed how a less cluttered home screen would still surface the new features through contextual tooltips later in the flow.

*Result:* The PM agreed to the single-banner approach. The feature launched on schedule. Post-launch session data showed users spending more time on core trading actions, which was the team's primary activation metric.

---

Q: Describe how you handle designing across web and mobile for the same feature.

*Situation:* My team was building a new portfolio summary feature that needed to launch simultaneously on web and on iOS and Android.

*Task:* I owned the design for all three surfaces and had to ensure visual consistency while respecting the distinct interaction patterns of each platform.

*Action:* I started with mobile first, since it forced the most constraint-driven thinking. I documented every component in our Figma design system with responsive variants, used auto-layout to handle different screen sizes, and held a joint review with iOS and Android engineers early, before high-fidelity mocks, to surface any platform-specific technical limits.

*Result:* We shipped all three surfaces within the same release window. The engineers noted that early collaboration saved them from several late-stage rework requests, and QA reported fewer design-deviation bugs than in previous multi-platform launches.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use the STAR format for all behavioural questions. Situation (brief context), Task (what you were responsible for), Action (what you specifically did, not 'we'), Result (a measurable or observable outcome). Keep Situation and Task short so you spend most of your time on Action and Result.

For portfolio walkthroughs, use the Problem-Process-Outcome structure. Start with the user problem and the business context. Walk through your research, key decisions, and any pivots. End with what shipped and what you would do differently. Interviewers at product-driven companies like OKX want to see that you make decisions based on evidence, not aesthetics.

For design critique or take-home exercises, use a 'users first, then heuristics' approach. Start by identifying who the user is and what they are trying to do. Then evaluate the existing design against that goal before reaching for standard heuristics. This shows you think like a product designer, not just a visual designer.

For 'how would you design X' questions, use a structured framing. State your assumptions, ask a clarifying question or two, define success metrics before sketching, then walk through one or two design directions with trade-offs. OKX interviewers typically want to see structured thinking, not a polished final answer produced on the spot.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Deep domain curiosity. OKX builds products for crypto and Web3. You do not need to be an expert trader, but interviewers want to see that you have genuinely used a crypto app, understand why trading interfaces are dense by necessity, and have thought about the specific trust and safety challenges in this space.

Evidence-based design thinking. Candidates report that OKX interviewers push hard on 'how did you know that?' for every major design decision. Have data, research findings, or user quotes ready to back up your choices. Opinions without evidence are a common red flag.

Systems thinking. OKX has large-scale design systems and multi-platform products. Show experience with or strong opinions about component libraries, token-based design systems, and cross-platform consistency. Interviewers want to know you can design at scale, not just one-off screens.

Collaboration and communication skills. Designers at OKX work closely with product managers, engineers, and data analysts. Interviewers look for candidates who can articulate trade-offs clearly to non-designers and who treat engineering constraints as creative challenges rather than blockers.

A portfolio with shipped, real-world products. Side projects are acceptable for junior roles, but mid-level and senior candidates are typically expected to show work that went live, had real users, and was iterated on based on actual feedback.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Know the product. Download the OKX app and explore the web platform. Use the onboarding flow, look at the trading dashboard, and explore the wallet and DeFi sections. Take notes on what works well and what feels confusing. Candidates report that almost every interview includes a question about what you personally think of the OKX product.

Week 1: Audit your portfolio. Pick 2-3 projects that best demonstrate research, structured decision-making, and measurable outcomes. For each, prepare a verbal walkthrough using the Problem-Process-Outcome structure. Be ready for the question 'what would you do differently?'

Week 2: Practice STAR answers. Write out answers for the 12 questions listed above. Focus especially on pushback, cross-functional collaboration, and data-driven decisions, as these come up most often in fintech design interviews.

Week 2: Study fintech and crypto UX patterns. Review how other exchanges and neobanks handle onboarding, KYC, real-time data, and trust signals. Being able to reference specific products by name shows genuine domain awareness.

Before your interview: Prepare smart questions. Ask about the design team structure, how design decisions get made, and what the biggest UX challenge the team is currently working on. Thoughtful questions signal genuine interest and seniority.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Showing only visual work with no process. OKX hires for product design, not visual design. If your portfolio is all final screens with no research, wireframes, or decision rationale, interviewers will struggle to evaluate your thinking.

Ignoring the crypto or fintech context. Candidates who treat the OKX interview like any other product design interview, without acknowledging the specific complexity of financial products and regulation, tend not to progress past early rounds. Use the product before you walk in.

Saying 'we' instead of 'I' in STAR answers. Interviewers need to understand your specific contribution. Always clarify what you personally did, even when the work was collaborative.

Skipping the result in your stories. A STAR answer without a result sounds unfinished. If you cannot share exact metrics, describe the qualitative outcome or the feedback you received from stakeholders.

Not asking clarifying questions during design exercises. Jumping straight into solutions without scoping the problem first is a common red flag. Take a moment to state your assumptions and ask one or two questions before you start designing.

Over-engineering the take-home assignment. Some candidates spend many hours going far beyond what was asked, trying to impress with visual polish. Interviewers typically want structured thinking and clear rationale. Scope your effort to what was actually requested.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the OKX UI/UX Designer interview typically have?

Candidates report that the process typically includes a portfolio screening call, one or two design deep-dive rounds, and a final culture or leadership round. Some roles also include a take-home design exercise in between. The exact number of rounds can vary by team and seniority level, so it is worth asking your recruiter at the start of the process.

Do I need to know about crypto or blockchain to get hired as a designer at OKX?

You do not need to be a crypto expert, but you should have used a crypto product and be able to speak intelligently about the user experience challenges specific to this space. Interviewers commonly ask for your honest opinion of the OKX product itself. Showing genuine curiosity about the domain matters more than deep technical knowledge of how blockchains work.

What should my portfolio include for an OKX interview?

Focus on 2-3 strong case studies that show your full process, from research and problem framing through to shipped product and iteration based on feedback. Include work that involved complex data displays, high-stakes user decisions, or cross-platform design if you have it. Fintech or consumer app experience is a strong signal, though candidates from other product domains are considered too.

Is there a take-home assignment, and how much time should I spend on it?

Many candidates report receiving a take-home design exercise with a short turnaround, typically a couple of days. The brief usually asks you to redesign a specific flow or propose a new feature for an existing product. Aim for clear thinking and a well-documented rationale rather than polished final mocks. Spending far more time than the brief suggests rarely improves outcomes and can result in over-engineered solutions that miss the point.

What salary can I expect as a UI/UX Designer at OKX in India?

OKX does not publicly post salary ranges for India roles on their listings. Glassdoor and levels.fyi show a wide range for senior designer roles at global crypto and fintech firms. Your best move is to ask the recruiter directly during your first call. Compensation at OKX commonly includes a base, a performance bonus, and in some roles, token-based components, so compare the total package, not just the base figure.

How competitive is it to get a UI/UX Designer role at OKX right now?

As of July 2026, knok's jobradar shows 29 UI/UX Designer openings across India, with OKX running 305 total open roles. That level of hiring suggests active team growth rather than simple backfill. Competition for a globally recognised crypto brand is real, but the volume of openings means your chances of reaching a screening call are meaningfully better than at companies hiring for just one or two design roles at a time.
