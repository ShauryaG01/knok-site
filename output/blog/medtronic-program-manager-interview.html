01 Overview

Overview

Medtronic is one of the largest medical device companies globally, and its Technical Program Manager interviews reflect that scale. With 140 open roles tracked by knok as of July 2026, the company is hiring actively across engineering, product, and operations program functions. Candidates report the process typically includes a recruiter screen, a hiring manager discussion, and a panel round with engineers, product managers, and sometimes clinical or regulatory colleagues.

The questions test whether you can run complex, multi-team programs in a regulated environment where a misstep has patient safety implications. Strong candidates bring a mix of execution rigour, cross-functional influence, and comfort with quality processes. If you come from a software background, expect questions about how you would adapt to the slower, more documentation-heavy pace of medical device development.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Program management and execution

  1. Tell me about a program you managed that had strict regulatory or compliance requirements. How did you structure oversight?
  2. Walk me through how you track risks on a hardware-software program. What does your risk register look like?
  3. Describe a program that was falling behind schedule. What actions did you take, and what was the outcome?
  4. How do you prioritise when two critical workstreams are competing for the same engineering resources?
  5. How do you set up governance for a multi-team program from scratch?

Stakeholder and cross-functional work

  1. Describe a time you had to align engineering, clinical, and regulatory teams on a single timeline.
  2. How do you communicate a technical delay to senior leadership who are not technical?
  3. Tell me about a time you pushed back on a stakeholder request that would have compromised program integrity or quality.
  4. How do you manage a program that spans multiple geographies with different regulatory approval timelines?

Domain and adaptability

  1. Describe your experience with quality management systems, design history files, or similar regulated documentation.
  2. How do you onboard yourself quickly to a product area you have no prior experience in?
  3. How do you handle scope changes in a regulated environment where changes require formal impact assessments?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a program with strict compliance requirements.

*Situation:* I was managing a firmware update program for a connected device that required sign-off from both engineering and a quality team before any build could advance to the next stage.

*Task:* My job was to keep the engineering team moving at speed without bypassing a single quality checkpoint.

*Action:* I built a stage-gate tracker that made every quality checkpoint visible alongside the engineering milestone. I held a weekly sync with the quality lead so blockers surfaced early. When one checkpoint was delayed, I used the buffer I had planned and communicated the revised date to leadership that same day.

*Result:* We delivered the program on the revised date with zero quality escapes, and the quality lead asked to use our tracker format as a team standard.

---

Q: Describe a time you pushed back on a stakeholder.

*Situation:* A senior product leader wanted to skip a validation test phase to hit a launch date.

*Task:* I had to protect the program's integrity without damaging the relationship.

*Action:* I prepared a one-page risk summary showing what could go wrong downstream if the phase was skipped, including the rework cost and timeline impact. I offered two alternatives: a reduced-scope validation that still met the quality bar, or a short date shift. I presented this privately to the leader before any group discussion.

*Result:* The leader chose the reduced-scope option. The program launched on the revised date, passed all checks, and the leader later told me the private conversation helped them make the case to their own team.

---

Q: How do you align engineering, clinical, and regulatory teams on one timeline?

*Situation:* On a new product introduction, our engineering timeline assumed a certain regulatory submission date, but the regulatory team had a different assumption.

*Task:* I needed one agreed master timeline before the program kicked off formally.

*Action:* I ran a focused joint planning session with leads from all three functions. We mapped each team's critical path on a shared board and identified the key conflicts. For each conflict, we agreed on who had the deciding vote and what data would drive the decision. I documented the outcome in a single master schedule that all leads signed off on.

*Result:* The program ran without a significant timeline dispute throughout, because every team had been part of building the plan from the start.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

The STAR structure works well here, with one Medtronic-specific addition. After your Result, add a brief note on what the outcome meant for the patient, the product quality, or the regulatory standing of the program. Medtronic interviewers consistently respond well to candidates who connect execution wins to downstream patient or quality impact.

How to open your answers. Start with one sentence that names the company or program type, one sentence on what made it complex, then move into Task. Keep the Situation and Task sections brief so you have room for the Action, which is where interviewers form their opinion.

Handling 'how do you' questions. When asked about your method rather than a past example, describe your approach in a few concrete steps, then offer a real example as evidence. Do not speak only in abstract process terms. Medtronic interviewers want to see that you have actually done the thing you are describing.

Specifics help. Team sizes, number of workstreams, timeline length, how many stakeholders were involved. These details make your answers credible. You do not need financial outcomes if you do not have them. Operational metrics work just as well.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Patient safety mindset without being told. Medtronic TPMs are expected to internalise that quality failures can harm patients. Candidates who mention this without being prompted signal strong cultural fit. You do not need to be dramatic about it. A simple reference to 'the downstream impact on the patient' in one of your answers is enough.

Cross-functional authority without the title. TPMs at Medtronic influence engineering, clinical, regulatory, and supply chain teams without direct reporting lines. Interviewers will probe whether you can hold a diverse group accountable through process and relationship rather than authority.

Comfort with regulated documentation. Even if your background is software, you should be able to speak to design history files, change control processes, or equivalent quality documentation. Candidates who have never encountered these should at least show curiosity and a clear plan to learn.

Structured communication. The ability to translate a complex technical situation into a clear one-page summary for leadership is a core skill. Expect a question, directly or indirectly, about how you communicate upward.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Start with Medtronic's public presence. Read their recent earnings calls and press releases to understand which product divisions are growing and what programs they are investing in. Note the language they use around quality, innovation, and patient outcomes.

Understand the regulated environment. You do not need to be a regulatory expert, but you should know how quality management systems work in medical device companies, what design controls involve, and why change management is more formal here than in a typical software company. Candidates report that showing awareness of this context, even at a high level, is noticed positively.

Prepare your program inventory. List several programs you have managed, with a one-sentence summary of what made each complex. Tag each with the relevant interview themes: regulatory, cross-functional, resource conflict, stakeholder management, schedule recovery. This lets you pull the right story quickly during the interview.

Practice out loud. Record yourself answering a few questions and listen back. Medtronic panels typically include non-technical stakeholders, so clarity and pacing matter as much as content.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Speaking only in software terms. If your background is in software program management, do not assume Medtronic interviewers will automatically translate. Explicitly note how your skills apply to a regulated, hardware-software environment.

Skipping the patient impact. Candidates who treat Medtronic like any other tech or industrial company miss an important cultural signal. Patient safety is a value, not a compliance checkbox.

Being vague about process. Saying 'I kept stakeholders aligned' is not enough. Name the meeting cadence, the artifact, the escalation path. Specificity is what separates a strong answer from a generic one.

Underestimating the regulatory knowledge bar. You do not need deep expertise, but showing no awareness of how medical device quality systems differ from software release processes will raise doubts about your readiness for the role.

Not asking good questions. Medtronic panels expect candidates to ask thoughtful questions. Prepare a few questions about the program portfolio, team structure, or how the team measures success.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Medtronic TPM interview process typically have?

Candidates report the process typically involves three to four rounds: a recruiter screen, a hiring manager conversation, and one or two panel rounds with cross-functional stakeholders. Some roles also include a brief written exercise or a take-home case. The exact structure varies by team and level, so ask the recruiter what to expect when you schedule your first call.

Do I need medical device experience to apply for a TPM role at Medtronic?

Not always. Medtronic hires TPMs from software, semiconductor, and industrial backgrounds. What they consistently look for is experience managing complex, multi-team programs with a quality or compliance component. If you do not have medical device experience, show that you have worked in regulated environments and that you understand why documentation and change control matter more here than in a typical software company.

What is the best way to show familiarity with Medtronic's industry during the interview?

Read about how medical device companies manage product development differently from software companies: design controls, formal change requests, validation and verification phases. You do not need to cite specific regulation codes by number. Showing that you understand the general principles and the reason behind them is what matters. Referencing a Medtronic product area relevant to the role you are applying for also signals preparation.

Which cities in India have the most Technical Program Manager openings?

Based on knok's job radar data from July 2026, Bangalore leads with 41 TPM openings across the market, followed by Delhi with 14 and Pune with 13. Hyderabad has 12 openings and Chennai has 5. Medtronic specifically had 140 open roles tracked at that time, spread across its India engineering and operations centres.

Should I prepare for a case study or whiteboard exercise in the Medtronic interview?

Candidates report that Medtronic TPM interviews are mostly behavioural, but some teams include a program planning or prioritisation exercise. It is worth preparing a simple framework for how you would structure a new program from scratch: goals, stakeholders, risks, and milestones. If you are asked to whiteboard, keep it structured and talk through your thinking as you go.

How long does the Medtronic TPM hiring process typically take from first screen to offer?

Candidates report the timeline typically runs several weeks from the initial recruiter screen to an offer, though this varies by team and time of year. Medical device companies can move slower than pure software firms due to internal approvals and compliance reviews. Following up with the recruiter after each round is reasonable and expected.
