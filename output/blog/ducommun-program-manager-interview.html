01 Overview

Overview

Ducommun is a US-based aerospace and defence electronics and structures manufacturer, delivering components and systems for commercial aviation and defence programmes. A Technical Program Manager (TPM) at Ducommun sits at the intersection of engineering rigour and delivery discipline, owning cross-functional programme execution from requirements through closure, under the stringent quality and regulatory frameworks standard to the aerospace sector.

As of July 2026, knok jobradar recorded 134 open roles at Ducommun and 313 TPM openings across India, with Bangalore leading at 41 postings. The interview process typically spans several rounds covering behavioural competencies, technical programme management depth, and scenario questions specific to regulated-industry delivery.

This guide covers what Ducommun interviewers typically probe, how to structure answers that land well, and how to prepare in a focused way.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Candidates for the TPM role at Ducommun typically report questions across four themes: programme governance, risk and escalation, stakeholder management, and regulated-industry delivery. Below are the questions most commonly cited.

  1. Walk us through a complex, multi-team programme you managed end-to-end. What was your governance model and how did you keep stakeholders aligned?
  2. How do you handle a supplier or vendor who is consistently slipping milestones and threatening programme schedule?
  3. Ducommun operates in a regulated environment. How have you managed compliance requirements alongside aggressive delivery timelines?
  4. Describe a time you had to escalate a programme risk to senior leadership. What did you present and what was the outcome?
  5. How do you build and maintain an integrated master schedule across engineering, manufacturing, and procurement?
  6. Tell us about a time a key stakeholder pushed back on your programme plan. How did you handle it?
  7. What metrics and dashboards do you use to track programme health, and how do you tailor status communication for executive audiences?
  8. How do you manage scope creep when customer or regulatory requirements evolve mid-programme?
  9. Describe a recovery situation: a programme behind schedule or over budget. What steps did you take?
  10. How do you coordinate between co-located and distributed or international teams without losing alignment?
  11. Walk us through your approach to building and maintaining a programme risk register.
  12. How do you ensure engineering decisions stay within programme cost, schedule, and quality constraints?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time you escalated a programme risk to senior leadership.

*Situation:* I was managing a hardware integration programme for an avionics supplier. Three weeks before a major customer review, our primary component vendor flagged a manufacturing defect that could delay delivery by several weeks.

*Task:* My job was to contain the risk, inform leadership quickly, and present a credible recovery path rather than just the bad news.

*Action:* I immediately convened the engineering and supply chain leads to quantify the impact. By end of that day I sent a written risk memo to the programme director covering the root cause, the schedule impact, three mitigation options with trade-offs, and a recommended path. I also prepared a one-page brief for the customer review that framed the issue as 'identified and in recovery' rather than 'unknown.'

*Result:* Leadership approved the recommended mitigation. The customer review proceeded with full transparency and the programme closed with a shorter slip than originally feared. The escalation approach was cited in the programme retrospective as a model for risk communication.

---

Q: How do you manage scope creep when requirements evolve mid-programme?

*Situation:* On a defence electronics programme, the customer introduced a new interface requirement partway through design that was not in the original statement of work.

*Task:* I needed to accommodate the requirement without blowing up cost and schedule, while keeping the team from informally absorbing the work.

*Action:* I called a change control board meeting within two days of the request. The team documented the requirement formally, estimated the impact on schedule and cost, and I presented the customer with three options: accept the change with a schedule and budget adjustment, descope another feature to make room, or defer to the next programme increment. Nothing was worked until the customer signed off on the chosen path.

*Result:* The customer accepted the schedule adjustment and a modest budget increase. The formal process prevented the team from absorbing undocumented work, which had caused overruns on a prior programme. The change was delivered on the revised plan.

---

Q: Tell us about a time a key stakeholder disagreed with your programme plan.

*Situation:* A VP of Engineering on one of my programmes disagreed with my proposal to move a design review milestone earlier, arguing his team needed more time.

*Task:* I needed to persuade him with data or find a workable compromise, without creating a culture of the programme manager overriding technical owners.

*Action:* I scheduled a one-on-one, came with the dependency map, and showed him exactly why the downstream manufacturing slot would be lost if we held the original date. I asked what specifically was at risk in the design and offered to bring in a senior engineer for a focused pre-review to de-risk those areas.

*Result:* He agreed to the earlier milestone once the downstream dependency was visible. We ran the pre-review, found and resolved two open items early, and the formal design review passed on the first attempt. The VP later said the pre-review approach was something he wanted to use on future programmes.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use STAR for every behavioural question. Situation and Task together should take roughly a third of your answer. Action should be the longest section, with specific steps you personally took. Result must include a measurable or observable outcome, not just 'it went well.'

For risk and escalation questions, use a simple four-part structure:

  1. What was the risk and how did you identify it?
  2. Who did you tell, when, and in what format?
  3. What options did you present (not just the problem)?
  4. What was the outcome and what would you do differently?

For scheduling and governance questions, lead with the tool or method you used, explain why you chose it for that context, then walk through a concrete example. Ducommun interviewers in a regulated environment will likely probe whether you understand integrated master scheduling, earned value concepts, and formal change control, so name these explicitly if you have used them.

For stakeholder conflict questions, show that you separate the person from the position. Describe how you understood their concern before countering it, and how you found a path that preserved the relationship.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Ducommun is an aerospace and defence company where programme slips carry real cost and reputational consequences with defence customers. Interviewers are typically looking for four things in a TPM candidate.

Programme rigour over agility buzzwords. Candidates who talk about sprints and velocity without grounding it in formal programme governance tend to score lower. Show you understand integrated schedules, change control, and formal risk management.

Regulated-industry awareness. Even if your background is not aerospace specifically, show you have worked in environments with quality requirements, audit trails, and formal documentation. Reference the kinds of standards and processes you have followed without overstating your credentials.

Escalation instinct. In a high-stakes environment, hiding bad news is a career-ending move. Interviewers want to see that your instinct is to surface risks early with options attached, not to manage them quietly and hope.

Cross-functional influence without authority. TPMs at Ducommun work across engineering, supply chain, manufacturing, and finance. Show you can drive alignment across teams that do not report to you, using data and relationships rather than hierarchy.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week 1: Company and role research. Read Ducommun's investor relations materials and recent press releases to understand which programmes and product lines are currently active. Note the language they use about quality, delivery, and customer relationships. Map your own programme history to their domain.

Week 2: Behavioural story bank. Write out eight to ten programme stories covering a recovery situation, a risk escalation, a stakeholder conflict, a scope change, a cross-functional alignment challenge, and a process improvement you drove. For each, make sure your Result includes a concrete outcome.

Week 3: Technical and domain prep. Refresh your knowledge of integrated master scheduling, earned value management concepts, and formal change control processes. If you have worked under aerospace quality certifications or ITAR requirements, prepare specific examples. If you have not, be honest and explain the nearest equivalent from your background.

Week 4: Practice and logistics. Do at least two mock interviews with someone who will push back on vague answers. Prepare three strong questions to ask the panel. Good ones include: 'What does programme success look like in the first quarter for this role?' and 'What are the biggest programme challenges the team is navigating right now?'

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Talking about tools instead of outcomes. Saying 'I used MS Project' or 'we ran SAFe' is not an answer. Interviewers want to know what you delivered and how you handled problems, not which software you opened.

Giving team answers for individual questions. When asked what 'you' did, candidates often say 'we.' Ducommun interviewers are assessing your specific contribution. Use 'I' and be precise about your role versus the team's role.

Skipping the result. Many candidates give rich Situation and Action sections but end with 'and it worked out.' Quantify or qualify the outcome: the programme closed on schedule, the customer renewed, the defect rate improved, the team delivered the milestone.

Underselling regulated-industry experience. If you have worked in pharma, banking, or any compliance-heavy environment, that transfers. Do not assume aerospace experience is a hard requirement and undersell your background.

Not asking about the programme. Finishing without a substantive question about the specific programme or team signals low interest. Prepare at least two specific questions that show you have researched the company.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the Ducommun TPM interview typically have?

Candidates typically report two to four rounds, though the exact structure varies by team and hiring manager. The process commonly includes an initial screening call, one or two technical and behavioural rounds, and a final panel or leadership discussion. Ducommun's interview structure is not publicly standardised, so confirm the format with your recruiter after the first call.

Do I need aerospace industry experience to get a TPM role at Ducommun?

Aerospace experience is a strong advantage but candidates report being considered with backgrounds in defence electronics, automotive, pharma, or any other regulated manufacturing environment. The key is demonstrating familiarity with formal programme governance, quality systems, and compliance-driven delivery. Be prepared to draw explicit parallels between your industry and aerospace if your background is different.

What is the salary range for a TPM at Ducommun in India?

Ducommun does not publicly disclose India-specific salary bands for TPM roles. Publicly reported ranges on platforms like Glassdoor and levels.fyi for senior TPM roles at US-listed aerospace companies in India vary widely by level and location. Research current postings on knok jobradar and use offer data from Glassdoor as a reference point when negotiating.

Is there a case study or take-home assignment in the process?

Some candidates report a scenario-based exercise, such as reviewing a programme plan with issues and presenting a recovery approach. Others report purely interview-based assessments. Ask your recruiter explicitly what to expect after the initial screening call so you can prepare the right materials in advance.

How important is PMP or other certification for this role?

Certifications like PMP are commonly cited as preferred rather than required in TPM postings. Ducommun's regulated environment means practical experience with formal programme management processes matters more than the credential itself. If you have the certification, mention it. If not, focus on demonstrating the underlying skills through your programme stories.

What is the best way to follow up after the Ducommun interview?

Send a brief thank-you note to your recruiter within a day of each round, referencing one specific topic from the conversation to show genuine engagement. If you do not hear back within the timeframe the recruiter mentioned, a single polite follow-up is appropriate. Avoid multiple follow-ups in quick succession, as it can work against you in a structured hiring process.
