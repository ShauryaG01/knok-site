01 Overview

Overview

SolarSquare is one of India's fastest-growing residential solar companies, handling everything from site survey to installation to post-sale service. A Business Analyst there works at the crossroads of sales, operations, and product, turning data on leads, installations, and customer feedback into decisions that move revenue and efficiency forward.

Candidates report the process typically runs across two or three rounds, mixing case-based problem solving, a practical SQL or Excel task, and behavioral questions. knok's jobradar currently shows 398 Business Analyst openings across the market, and SolarSquare alone is carrying 177 open roles as of mid-2026, so hiring is active and rounds can move fast. 03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Describe a time you used data to challenge a business assumption.

*Situation:* My team believed customers in Tier 2 cities were unlikely to choose the premium product tier, so marketing was not promoting it there.

*Task:* I was reviewing sales data before a new pricing launch and noticed this assumption had never been tested with real numbers.

*Action:* I pulled two quarters of sales records, segmented by city category, and compared the share of customers selecting the premium option. I found that Tier 2 customers who did convert were choosing the higher-margin package at a rate comparable to metro buyers. I packaged this into a short briefing and walked the sales and marketing leads through it.

*Result:* The team updated messaging for Tier 2 campaigns to feature the premium tier prominently. The next campaign cycle saw stronger uptake in those markets, validating the data-led call.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you worked cross-functionally to deliver a business outcome.

*Situation:* Our installation team was consistently missing committed timelines, and customers were escalating. Nobody had a clear view of where the bottleneck was.

*Task:* I volunteered to map the end-to-end workflow and find where delays were accumulating.

*Action:* I sat with the survey team, the procurement lead, and site supervisors over a week to log timestamps at each handoff. I built a tracker in Google Sheets and visualised the average time between stages. The data showed the gap between survey completion and material dispatch was the main culprit, not the installation crew.

*Result:* We introduced a dispatch trigger rule tied to survey sign-off. Our internal cycle time numbers improved within the first month, and customer escalations on timelines dropped noticeably.

---

Q: How did you handle competing priorities from multiple stakeholders?

*Situation:* During a quarter-end crunch, the sales head wanted a city-wise performance dashboard urgently, while the product team needed a feature adoption analysis for an investor review happening the same week.

*Task:* Both deadlines were real. I had to make a call on sequencing and communicate it clearly to both teams.

*Action:* I listed the minimum viable output each team actually needed, then ran a short joint call with both leads to align on what 'good enough by when' meant for each. The sales dashboard needed live data but only a handful of metrics. The product analysis needed more depth but could use last month's export. I delivered the dashboard first, then the analysis before the investor review.

*Result:* Both deadlines were met. The joint call upfront saved multiple rounds of revision requests and set a useful precedent for triaging analyst work when demand spikes.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioral questions. Every 'tell me about a time' question expects: Situation (context in one or two sentences), Task (what you specifically owned), Action (what you actually did, not what the team did), and Result (a concrete outcome, even if qualitative). Keep Situation short and spend most of your words on Action and Result.

Hypothesis-first for case questions. When asked something like 'how would you find solar adoption potential in a new city', start with a hypothesis ('I would first filter by roof ownership rates, average electricity bills, and subsidy availability'), then walk through the data you would gather and how you would validate or reject each leg. Interviewers at product-led companies like SolarSquare respond well to structured thinking over exhaustive lists.

Metric trees for 'how would you measure' questions. Pick the north star metric first ('installations completed per month'), then break it into two or three levers that drive it (leads generated, conversion rate, installation cycle time). This shows you think in systems, not just reports.

Acknowledge trade-offs. For prioritization questions, show that you understand choosing one thing means deprioritizing another. Name the trade-off explicitly. Interviewers flag candidates who present every decision as painless.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Genuine curiosity about solar and clean energy. SolarSquare is a mission-driven company in a competitive sector. Candidates who have done basic homework on government solar targets, net metering policy, and the competitor landscape consistently stand out. You do not need to be an energy expert, but showing you care about why the company exists goes a long way.

Comfort with ambiguity and messy data. Growth-stage companies rarely have clean, well-labelled datasets. Interviewers want to see that you can define a problem, make reasonable assumptions, and move forward rather than wait for perfect information.

Cross-functional communication. A BA at SolarSquare works with field sales teams, installation crews, finance, and sometimes customers directly. Interviewers probe whether you can translate data insights into plain language that a non-analyst can act on.

Ownership mindset. Candidates report that interviewers respond well to answers where you took initiative, flagged a problem before being asked, or followed up on a recommendation to check whether it worked. Passive answers like 'I prepared the slides and sent them' score lower than active ones like 'I noticed the data told a different story and pushed back on the initial plan'.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week one: know the company and the market. Read SolarSquare's publicly available content (website, LinkedIn, any press coverage). Understand what they sell, how the customer journey works from inquiry to installation, and what problems a residential solar buyer faces. Read up on India's PM Surya Ghar scheme and rooftop solar policy basics, because these topics come up in interviews.

Week two: sharpen your analytical tools. Practice SQL queries covering joins, aggregations, and window functions. Review Excel pivot tables and basic dashboard building. If you have not done case interviews before, practice two or three market-sizing or metric-definition cases out loud, not just in your head.

Week three: prep your stories. Write out five to six STAR answers covering: using data to drive a decision, cross-functional collaboration, handling a difficult stakeholder, working under ambiguity, and a project you are proud of. Rehearse until they flow naturally without sounding scripted.

Before the interview. Prepare two or three thoughtful questions for your interviewer. Good ones: ask what the most important metric the BA team is tracking right now, how the team decides which analysis to prioritize, and what the biggest data challenge the business is working through. These signal that you are thinking like a practitioner, not just a candidate.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Giving generic answers without solar context. Saying 'I would look at market size and growth rate' in response to a SolarSquare-specific case is a missed opportunity. Anchor your answers to residential customers, rooftop area, electricity bills, and government subsidies where relevant.

Overselling technical tools without showing business impact. Listing skills without connecting them to outcomes leaves interviewers without a sense of the value you add. Instead of 'I know SQL and Tableau', say 'I used SQL to cut weekly reporting time, which freed the sales team to act on insights faster'.

Vague results in STAR answers. 'The project went well' or 'the team was happy' are weak closings. Even if you cannot share a specific number, describe what changed: a decision was made, a product was launched, a process was simplified.

Not asking any questions. Candidates who have nothing to ask at the end of the interview signal low engagement. Prepare at least two genuine questions about the team and the business.

Underestimating the domain knowledge check. Interviewers commonly ask what you know about Indian solar policy or how SolarSquare makes money. A blank answer here can undo a strong case performance. Spend thirty minutes on this before you go in.

Frequently asked

How many interview rounds does SolarSquare typically have for a Business Analyst role?

Candidates report two to three rounds in most cases, though this can vary by team and seniority level. The process typically includes a screening call, a practical analytical task or case round, and a final round with a senior stakeholder. Timelines can move quickly given how actively SolarSquare is hiring right now.

Is there a SQL or Excel test in the SolarSquare BA interview?

Candidates report that a practical data task is common, typically involving SQL queries, Excel analysis, or both. The problems tend to be business-grounded (for example, analysing a sales funnel or computing a simple return-on-investment scenario) rather than pure algorithmic puzzles. Brush up on joins, aggregations, and pivot tables before you go in.

Publicly reported ranges on platforms like Glassdoor and levels.fyi for Business Analyst roles at growth-stage clean energy companies in India vary widely by experience and city. Bangalore and Delhi tend to command higher bands than other locations, based on industry surveys.

Do I need prior experience in the solar or energy sector to apply?

Prior solar experience is not typically a hard requirement for a BA role. What matters more is your analytical foundation and your ability to pick up domain knowledge quickly. That said, showing up with basic knowledge of how residential solar works, the customer journey, and relevant government policy will clearly set you apart from candidates who have done no homework.

How should I prepare for the case round specifically?

Practice structuring your answers with a hypothesis first, then the data you would need, then how you would validate it. Common case themes at companies like SolarSquare include market sizing for a new city, diagnosing a drop in conversions, and evaluating a new product or pricing change. Speaking your logic out loud, even when practising solo, builds the habit of showing your thinking rather than just stating your conclusion.

What is the best way to research SolarSquare before the interview?

Start with the company website to understand the product, the customer journey, and the financing options they offer. Check their LinkedIn page for recent announcements and hiring patterns. Read a few articles on India's rooftop solar market and the PM Surya Ghar scheme so you can speak to the policy environment. Preparing two or three informed questions for your interviewer based on this research will leave a strong impression.
