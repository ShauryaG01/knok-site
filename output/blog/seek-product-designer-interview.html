01 Overview

Overview

Seek is one of Asia-Pacific's largest employment marketplaces, connecting job seekers and employers across Australia, Southeast Asia, and beyond. As of July 2026, Seek had 70 open Product Designer roles, making it one of the more active product design hirers in the market right now. Candidates report that the interview process typically spans three to four stages: a recruiter or HR screen, a portfolio review with the design team, a design exercise or take-home challenge, and a final round with cross-functional stakeholders including product managers and engineers.

Seek's designers work on complex, two-sided marketplace problems, balancing the needs of job seekers and employers at the same time. Interviewers typically look for candidates who combine user empathy with business thinking, communicate design decisions clearly to non-design partners, and use data to support their choices.

The broader market for Product Designers in India is active. As of July 2026, there were 393 open roles tracked across India, with the highest concentrations in Bangalore (62 roles) and Delhi (33 roles). Salary bands range from 6-12 LPA at entry level to 36-55+ LPA for Lead and Principal positions.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions are commonly reported by candidates who have gone through Product Designer interviews at companies like Seek. Prepare answers for all of them before your interview.

  1. Walk us through your portfolio. Which project best shows your end-to-end process, from discovery to launch?
  2. Seek connects job seekers and employers on the same platform. How do you design for a two-sided marketplace where both sides have competing needs?
  3. Describe a time you used research or analytics to challenge a product assumption your team had made. What was the outcome?
  4. Tell us about a project where you had to simplify a complex user flow. How did you decide what to remove or restructure?
  5. How do you design for users with varying levels of digital literacy or unreliable internet access?
  6. Walk us through how you work with engineers during design handoff. How do you make sure the final product matches your intent?
  7. Tell us about a design decision that did not go as planned. What happened and what did you do next?
  8. How have you contributed to or maintained a design system? What problems did you run into?
  9. Seek operates across different markets and cultures. Have you ever adapted a design for users in different regions? How did you approach that?
  10. Describe a time when product, engineering, and design all disagreed on a direction. How did you help the team move forward?
  11. How do you prioritize your design work when multiple projects are competing for your time and attention?
  12. Tell us about a time you influenced product strategy through design thinking, not just execution.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR format for behavioral questions: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task brief. Spend most of your answer on Action. Here are three worked examples relevant to a Seek interview.

Q: Tell us about a time you simplified a complex user flow.

*Situation:* At a previous company, the job application form had grown considerably over several years as different teams kept adding required fields and steps.

*Task:* My goal was to reduce drop-off at a specific step in the flow without removing information that employers actually needed.

*Action:* I ran several moderated usability sessions and found that users were abandoning at a step that asked for a cover letter and references at the same time. I worked with the product and sales teams to audit every field and separate 'must-have' data from 'nice-to-have.' I redesigned the flow to be significantly shorter, moved references to a post-application state, and added a clear progress indicator so users could see how close they were to finishing.

*Result:* Drop-off at that step fell noticeably after launch. The same audit approach was then applied to two other flows the following quarter, with the team driving the process independently.

---

Q: Describe a time you used data to challenge a product assumption.

*Situation:* The product team assumed job seekers wanted more filter options on the search results page, and a new filter panel was already on the roadmap.

*Task:* I was asked to design the filter panel, but I wanted to validate the assumption before starting.

*Action:* I reviewed session recordings and found that a small fraction of users actually interacted with any existing filter. I presented this to the PM alongside a counter-proposal: improve the default sort algorithm instead of adding more filters. I also ran two short user interviews to validate the direction before pitching it to the wider team.

*Result:* The team agreed to deprioritize the filter panel. This freed up several weeks of engineering time for higher-impact work, and the sort improvement shipped ahead of schedule.

---

Q: How do you handle stakeholder conflict over a design decision?

*Situation:* At a previous role, the marketing team wanted a prominent homepage banner for a campaign. The research team had data showing that similar banners increased bounce rates.

*Task:* I needed to find a path forward that both teams could accept, without just 'splitting the difference' and producing a weak design.

*Action:* I set up a joint session with both teams and framed the conversation around the shared goal: converting visitors into registered users. I presented the research data clearly, then proposed an A/B test comparing a smaller, less disruptive banner against the full version. Both teams agreed because neither had to give up entirely.

*Result:* The test ran for several weeks. The smaller banner performed comparably for campaign clicks, while the full banner showed a higher bounce rate, consistent with what the research had predicted. The data resolved the disagreement without anyone losing face.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioral questions. Every 'tell me about a time' question expects four parts: Situation (set the scene in two sentences), Task (your specific responsibility, not the team's), Action (what you personally did, using 'I' not 'we'), and Result (what changed). Vague outcomes like 'it went well' are weak. Anchor results in something observable, even if you cannot cite a precise figure.

Portfolio walkthrough structure. For each case study: state the problem and who it affected, clarify your role versus the team's role, explain the research or data that shaped your decisions, walk through two or three key design choices and why you made them (not just what they look like), and share what you would do differently today. Seek interviewers typically want to understand your reasoning, not just see polished visuals.

Design critique framing. When asked to review a product or interface, use a simple structure: start with what is working and why, identify one or two friction points with a hypothesis about the cause, then suggest a direction to explore rather than a finished solution. This signals product thinking, not just aesthetic taste.

Handling 'why Seek.' Candidates report that interviewers respond better to specific answers tied to Seek's marketplace model or its expansion into new markets, rather than generic lines about 'making an impact.' Connect your past work to a specific challenge Seek is solving, and show that you have actually used the product.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Product thinking over visual polish. Seek's design team works closely with product managers and engineers. Interviewers typically want to see that you understand user problems and business context. Come prepared to explain why you made each decision, not just what you made.

Comfort with ambiguity. Marketplace products involve many user types and competing priorities. Interviewers look for candidates who define the problem before jumping to a solution, and who ask clarifying questions rather than assuming.

Data fluency. You do not need to be an analyst, but you should describe how you have used analytics, research findings, or usability data to make or change a decision. Candidates who point to specific outcomes, even qualitative ones, stand out.

Collaboration signals. Expect questions that probe how you work with engineers, PMs, and other stakeholders. Interviewers typically want to see that you push back constructively when needed, and that you bring people along rather than designing in isolation.

Clear communication. Seek operates across markets and time zones, so the ability to explain design decisions simply and confidently matters a lot. Practice narrating your portfolio decisions out loud before the interview so the logic flows naturally.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

One to two weeks before the interview.
Use Seek's platform as both a job seeker and an employer to spot UX strengths and friction points. Note three to five specific observations you could bring up naturally during the interview. Read any recent news about Seek's product direction or market focus.

Select three to four portfolio projects. For each one, write a brief narrative covering the problem, your role, your key decisions, and the outcome. Practice presenting each in under ten minutes without reading from notes.

Three to five days before.
Prepare STAR answers for each of the twelve questions in the 'Most Asked Questions' section. Record yourself answering two or three of them and listen for filler words, vague outcomes, or overuse of 'we' instead of 'I.'

If a design exercise is part of the process (candidates typically report this at some stage), practice a rapid design sprint: define the problem clearly, sketch two or three directions, pick a recommended approach, and document your reasoning as clearly as your visuals. Interviewers care more about how you think than how refined the output looks.

One day before.
Prepare two or three thoughtful questions for the interviewers. Good options: how the team measures design success, how design and business goals are balanced when they conflict, or what the first few months look like for a new designer joining the team. Confirm the interview format, time zone, and any screen-sharing tools you will need.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Presenting only the final design. Interviewers at product companies want to see your process, including early sketches, dead ends, and pivots. A polished prototype with no context tells them very little about how you think.

Saying 'we' throughout your portfolio. Credit the team where it is due, but interviewers need to know what you specifically did. Replace 'we decided' with 'I recommended' or 'my role was to.'

Skipping business context. Saying a design 'improved the user experience' without tying it to a business outcome (retention, conversion, support volume) makes your work hard to evaluate. Anchor results in something measurable or at least observable.

Over-designing the take-home challenge. Candidates sometimes produce a polished prototype when annotated wireframes were expected. Read the brief carefully and match your output to the scope asked for, not the scope you wish you had been given.

Not preparing questions for the interviewer. Showing up without questions signals low interest. Prepare at least three, and avoid questions whose answers are already on Seek's careers page.

Being defensive about critique. If an interviewer pushes back on a decision, they are often testing how you handle feedback. Acknowledge the perspective, explain your original reasoning calmly, and show you are open to exploring alternatives. Designers who get defensive under pressure rarely make it to the next round.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many interview rounds does Seek typically have for Product Designer roles?

Candidates report that the process typically involves three to four stages: a recruiter screen, a portfolio review with the design team, a design exercise, and a final round with senior stakeholders. The exact structure can vary by team and seniority level. Ask your recruiter upfront what to expect so you can prepare accordingly.

Does Seek give a design exercise or take-home challenge?

Candidates report that a design exercise is a common part of the process, either as a take-home task or a live problem-solving session. The brief typically focuses on a marketplace or platform design challenge. Document your reasoning as clearly as your visuals, and match your output to the scope that was asked for rather than going far beyond it.

What salary should I expect for a Product Designer role at Seek?

Across the Product Designer market in India, entry-level roles (0-2 years) typically fall in the 6-12 LPA range, mid-level (3-5 years) in the 14-24 LPA range, and senior roles (6-9 years) in the 26-40 LPA range. For Lead and Principal positions, Glassdoor and levels.fyi commonly cite figures in the 36-55+ LPA range. Seek's specific offers will depend on your experience, the team you are joining, and how you negotiate.

Is a portfolio website mandatory, or can I present from Figma or a PDF?

A dedicated portfolio website is not strictly required. Candidates report presenting from Figma, Notion, and PDF decks successfully. What matters more is that your case studies tell a clear story: problem, process, decisions, and outcomes. Make sure your chosen format is easy to share or screen-share without technical friction during the call.

What if I have never worked on a marketplace product before?

You do not need direct marketplace experience, but you should be able to speak to the challenges of designing for two-sided platforms: competing user needs, trust signals, and discoverability. Spend time using Seek's platform as both a job seeker and an employer before the interview, note what works and what does not, and come with specific observations. Transferable experience from e-commerce, fintech, or platform products is relevant and worth framing explicitly in your answers.

How competitive is the Seek Product Designer hiring process right now?

Seek had 70 open Product Designer roles in the July 2026 data, which is a meaningful volume for a single company. The broader market had 393 Product Designer openings across India at the same time, with the highest demand in Bangalore and Delhi. A strong portfolio with clear business outcomes and solid preparation for behavioral questions will help you stand out in what is typically a competitive shortlist.
