01 Overview

Overview

Procol is a B2B procurement SaaS platform that helps large enterprises run reverse auctions, manage vendors, and automate sourcing workflows. The company is Bangalore-based and serves major corporates across manufacturing, retail, and infrastructure sectors. As of July 2026, Procol has 12 open Product Manager roles, which points to an active hiring phase.

Working as a PM at Procol means owning products that sit at the heart of enterprise procurement operations. You will work closely with enterprise sales, customer success, and engineering teams to ship features that affect how large organisations plan and execute spending. Candidates report that the culture values domain depth, structured thinking, and genuine curiosity about how procurement works in practice.

Interviews typically span two to four rounds covering product sense, metrics and analytics, behavioural questions, and a domain understanding discussion. The exact structure varies by seniority level, so prepare for both case-style questions and deep dives into your past work.

  11. Tell me about a time you partnered with sales or customer success to close a product gap that was hurting renewals or new deals.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a product feature you shipped that did not perform as expected.

*Situation:* At my previous company, we shipped a supplier onboarding self-serve flow to reduce manual work on the customer success team. The expectation was that suppliers would complete onboarding independently within two business days.

*Task:* I owned the end-to-end product spec and was responsible for tracking adoption post-launch.

*Action:* Within two weeks of launch, completion rates were well below our projections. I ran a funnel analysis and discovered suppliers were dropping off at the document upload step. I then ran five user interviews with suppliers who had abandoned the flow and found that many were on mobile devices and could not easily upload PDFs. I prioritised a quick fix: allowing image uploads as an alternative and adding a 'save and resume later' option. I also flagged this as a discovery gap in our retro, since we had not tested the flow on mobile during the design phase.

*Result:* Completion rates improved within four weeks of the fix going live, and the customer success team reported fewer manual escalations. More importantly, we added mobile testing as a mandatory checkpoint in our launch checklist going forward.

---

Q: How do you prioritise features when enterprise sales, customer success, and engineering all want different things?

*Situation:* At my last role, we were three weeks from a quarterly planning cycle. We had feature requests from four enterprise accounts through customer success, a list of 'deal-blockers' from sales, and a backlog of technical debt items flagged by engineering.

*Task:* I needed to produce a prioritised roadmap that the entire cross-functional team could align on before the planning deadline.

*Action:* I mapped every request to one of three buckets: revenue risk (losing a renewal or deal), growth opportunity (unlocking a new segment), or operational health (reducing internal friction). I used a simple scoring model weighted by revenue impact and implementation effort, then ran a two-hour alignment session with the heads of sales, CS, and engineering. I walked through the scores, invited pushback, and where items were close, deferred to the team with the most direct customer context.

*Result:* We went into planning with a single prioritised list that all three teams had contributed to and agreed on. Two of the four 'deal-blocker' features made it into the quarter, one enterprise renewal was secured earlier than expected, and engineering got a meaningful technical debt sprint included.

---

Q: Describe a time you used data to change a product decision.

*Situation:* We had been building a 'smart recommendations' feature for our sourcing tool, based on the assumption that procurement managers wanted the system to automatically suggest preferred vendors.

*Task:* I was leading the feature and had already invested two sprints into the recommendation engine when a routine usage audit flagged something unexpected.

*Action:* The data showed that the vast majority of active users were procurement executives who rarely used the vendor search feature at all. They were primarily on the platform for approvals and spend reports. I paused the recommendation feature, ran a short survey with our most active accounts, and found that the real pain point was approval delays, not vendor discovery. I took this to the engineering lead and proposed redirecting the next sprint to an approval-flow acceleration feature instead.

*Result:* The recommendation feature was moved to a later quarter. The approval-flow feature shipped and, according to our customer success team, became one of the most-cited reasons for positive feedback in quarterly business reviews with three of our top accounts.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

RICE for prioritisation. Use this when ranking competing features or requests. Score each item on Reach (how many users or accounts it affects), Impact (how much it moves your north star metric), Confidence (how sure you are of your estimates), and Effort (engineering time). RICE works especially well in B2B contexts because 'Reach' can represent number of enterprise accounts rather than individual end users.

Jobs-to-be-Done for discovery. Instead of asking 'what feature do you want,' ask 'what job are you trying to get done and what is getting in the way?' This is particularly relevant at Procol, where procurement officers have specific workflow jobs (running an auction, approving a purchase order, onboarding a vendor) that do not map neatly onto feature lists.

MECE for metric diagnostics. When asked to diagnose a drop in a metric, structure your answer so every possible cause is covered with no overlap. If auction completion rates fall, split causes into supply-side (vendor issues), demand-side (buyer issues), and platform issues. This shows structured thinking without feeling mechanical.

North star metric framing. For any product question, anchor on one metric that best represents value delivered to the customer. For a vendor management platform, a strong north star candidate is 'active vendors completing at least one transaction per quarter,' because it captures both adoption and real usage rather than just logins.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Domain curiosity. Procol works in procurement, a space most PMs have not thought deeply about before joining. Interviewers are not expecting you to know procurement jargon on day one, but they want to see that you ask smart questions and can learn a complex domain quickly. Candidates report that genuine interest in how enterprises buy things goes a long way in these interviews.

Structured communication. B2B products at Procol touch multiple stakeholders: enterprise buyers, procurement officers, finance teams, and internal sales and CS teams. Interviewers want to see that you can organise your thinking clearly, communicate trade-offs honestly, and hold your position calmly when pushed back on.

Customer empathy grounded in evidence. Saying 'users want this' is not enough. Interviewers look for candidates who can describe how they validated a customer pain point through research, usage data, or direct conversations before committing to a solution.

Cross-functional collaboration. PMs at Procol work closely with sales and customer success, which is different from most consumer-product roles. Show that you understand how to work with revenue teams and that you treat them as partners rather than just a source of feature requests.

Ownership and follow-through. Candidates report that interviewers ask what happened after a feature shipped, not just what you built. Be ready to talk about post-launch metrics, iterations, and lessons learned.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

  1. Understand Procol's product. Sign up for a free trial or watch product demo videos on their website. Map out the core workflows: e-auction, vendor onboarding, spend analytics, and approval flows. You should be able to describe these in your own words before the interview.
  1. Read about procurement as a domain. Spend a few hours understanding how enterprise procurement works: the typical purchase order lifecycle, the role of a Chief Procurement Officer, and why companies use platforms like Procol instead of email and spreadsheets. This context will sharpen your product sense answers.
  1. Prepare two to three strong STAR stories. Cover at minimum: a feature that underperformed and what you did about it, a time you managed conflicting stakeholder priorities, and a time data changed your direction. Practise these out loud until each runs to about two minutes.
  1. Practise north star metric questions. Pick a Procol module (for example, the e-auction feature) and define a north star metric, secondary metrics, and guardrail metrics. Be ready to defend your choices when the interviewer pushes back.
  1. Prepare a brief competitor landscape view. Know two or three other procurement SaaS companies and be ready to articulate where Procol's positioning differs. Focus on product differentiation rather than funding or valuation data.
  1. Do a timed run through the 12 questions above. If any answer runs past four minutes, practise cutting it down. Interviewers typically appreciate concise, structured answers over exhaustive ones.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating it like a consumer PM interview. Procol is B2B enterprise software. Analogies to consumer apps like Swiggy or Instagram can make you look like you have not done your homework. Use enterprise product examples where possible and show you understand the B2B buying cycle.

Ignoring the commercial context. PMs at Procol need to understand that revenue comes from enterprise contracts, not ad clicks or per-user subscriptions. Feature decisions are tied to renewals, upsells, and deal closures. Candidates who skip this commercial lens typically score lower on prioritisation questions.

Being vague about metrics. Saying 'I would track engagement' is not enough. Name the specific metric, explain why it reflects real customer value, and describe how you would measure it.

Skipping the result in STAR answers. Many candidates describe what they built but stop before explaining the outcome. Always close a STAR answer with a concrete result, even if it is qualitative (for example, 'the customer success team reported fewer escalations after launch').

Over-claiming domain knowledge. If you do not know procurement deeply, say so honestly and then show how you would learn it quickly. Candidates report that interviewers typically respect intellectual honesty over bluffing.

Not asking questions. Candidates report that interviewers at Procol appreciate curiosity. Prepare two to three thoughtful questions about the product roadmap, customer segments, or how the PM team collaborates with sales before you go in.

How many PM roles does Procol currently have open?

How many rounds does the Procol PM interview process typically have?

Candidates report that the process typically involves two to four rounds, though this varies by level and the specific team hiring. Rounds generally cover a mix of product sense, metrics and analytical thinking, a behavioural discussion, and sometimes a case study or take-home assignment. Confirm the exact structure with your recruiter after you receive the interview invite.

Do I need procurement domain knowledge to interview at Procol?

You do not need deep procurement expertise before your first round, but you should understand the basics: what a purchase order is, how enterprise buying decisions are made, and why companies use procurement software. Candidates report that interviewers value genuine curiosity about the domain more than memorised jargon. Spending a few hours reading about enterprise procurement before your interview will give you a meaningful edge.

What is the best way to show product sense in a Procol interview?

Pick a specific Procol product (such as the e-auction module or vendor onboarding flow) and walk through how you would improve it: start with the user problem, define a north star metric, propose two or three solutions, evaluate trade-offs, and explain how you would measure success. Showing that you understand B2B procurement workflows, not just generic UX principles, will make your answer stand out from candidates who give generic product sense responses.

Is Procol a good company for a PM early in their career?

Procol gives early-career PMs exposure to enterprise B2B products, which is a valuable and relatively rare experience compared to consumer startups. Working in procurement SaaS builds domain expertise that transfers well to other enterprise roles. Candidates report that PMs get meaningful ownership early and that the pace is fast. As with any role, the quality of mentorship and team culture matters most, so ask about these directly during your interviews.
