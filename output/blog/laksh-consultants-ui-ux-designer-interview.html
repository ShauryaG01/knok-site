02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions come up frequently for UI/UX Designer roles at Laksh Consultants, based on what candidates typically report across rounds.

  1. Walk us through a project in your portfolio you are most proud of. What was your specific contribution?
  2. How do you approach user research when you know very little about the target audience?

  10. How do you decide which features to prioritize when time and resources are limited?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Use the STAR method (Situation, Task, Action, Result) to structure your answers. Here are three worked examples for common questions.

Q: Walk us through a portfolio project you are most proud of.

*Situation:* I worked with a fintech startup where users were abandoning the loan application form before completing it. Their own analytics flagged it as a critical issue for the business.

*Task:* My goal was to redesign the form to reduce drop-offs without changing the underlying business requirements.

*Action:* I started with user interviews to understand where and why people stopped. I found the form asked for too much information at once. I broke it into shorter steps, added a progress indicator, and moved optional fields to the end. I tested two prototypes with real users before finalizing the design.

*Result:* After launch, the product team reported a meaningful improvement in completion rates. I do not have the exact figure since analytics access stayed with the client, but they confirmed the change had a clear positive impact.

---

Q: Describe a time you had to simplify a complicated user flow.

*Situation:* At a previous company, the onboarding flow for new users had many steps and a confusing navigation structure. Users in usability tests consistently got lost.

*Task:* I was asked to redesign onboarding so new users could complete setup in one session without needing help.

*Action:* I mapped the existing flow end to end and identified steps that could be combined or removed. I worked with the product manager to agree on what was truly required versus optional. I built a clickable Figma prototype and ran three rounds of testing with five users each, iterating after every round.

*Result:* The final flow had fewer screens and clearer labels. Test participants completed onboarding independently, compared to needing guidance in earlier versions. The product manager signed off after the second iteration.

---

Q: Tell us about a design that did not perform the way you expected.

*Situation:* I redesigned a search feature for an e-commerce app. I was confident the new layout was cleaner and easier to use.

*Task:* The goal was to help users find products faster and increase add-to-cart actions.

*Action:* We launched the new design and tracked user behavior through analytics. I also ran a short post-launch survey.

*Result:* The data showed users were clicking around more, not less. In follow-up interviews, they said the filters were harder to find than before. I moved the filters back to a visible position and ran another usability round. The revised version performed better. The main lesson: my assumptions about what felt 'cleaner' did not match what users actually needed.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

The STAR method is your foundation for behavioral questions. Anything that starts with 'tell me about a time' calls for a Situation, Task, Action, Result structure. Keep the Situation brief, spend most of your answer on the Action (what you specifically did), and close with a concrete Result.

The Double Diamond is useful when explaining your design process. Describe how you first opened up possibilities through research, discovery, and ideation, then narrowed down to a solution through defining, prototyping, and testing. Interviewers at design-oriented firms respond well to this structure because it shows a deliberate process.

The Why-What-How opener works well for portfolio walkthroughs. Start with why the project existed (the business or user problem), then explain what you designed (the solution), and finally cover how you got there (your process and key decisions). This keeps the interviewer oriented before you go deep on specifics.

The trade-off frame helps with stakeholder conflict questions. Name the competing priorities (user need vs. business constraint vs. technical limit), explain which you prioritized and why, and describe how you got alignment. This shows you understand that design decisions are never made in isolation.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Portfolio depth, not breadth. Laksh Consultants interviewers typically want to see two or three projects you can explain in full detail, rather than ten projects with thin descriptions. Be prepared to answer follow-up questions on any project you include.

Clear process thinking. Can you explain why you made each design decision? Interviewers listen for reasoning, not just outcomes. Saying 'I chose this layout because user research showed people scan left to right on mobile' carries far more weight than 'I felt it looked better.'

User empathy. Show that users, not aesthetics, drive your decisions. Reference actual users you spoke to, specific pain points you observed, and how those shaped your design choices.

Collaboration skills. Designers placed by staffing firms often work across multiple client environments. Interviewers want to see that you can work with developers, product managers, and business stakeholders without friction.

Practical tool knowledge. Figma is the industry standard today. Knowing how to use components, auto-layout, and design tokens matters. Be honest about what you know and what you are still learning.

Adaptability. Because Laksh Consultants places designers with different client teams, they value candidates who can pick up a new product domain or company context quickly.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

One week before the interview:

Pick two or three portfolio projects and prepare a five-minute walkthrough for each. Practice explaining the problem, your process, and the outcome out loud. Record yourself once to check your pacing and clarity.

Research Laksh Consultants on LinkedIn to understand the industries and client types they typically serve. If you have the end-client role description, read it carefully and note the keywords they use.

Prepare written STAR stories for at least five behavioral scenarios: handling stakeholder pushback, delivering under a tight deadline, a design that failed, a collaboration challenge, and your proudest design decision.

Two to three days before:

Review your Figma skills. Make sure you can demo auto-layout, components, and prototyping flows if asked in a practical round. Brush up on basic accessibility standards, since these come up often in interviews.

Prepare two or three questions to ask the interviewer. Strong ones: 'What does success look like for this role in the first three months?' and 'What does the design review process look like on this team?'

Day of:

Open your portfolio in a clean browser window before the call. Have Figma files ready to share. If the interview is in person, bring a tablet or printed version. Speak slowly during portfolio walkthroughs since interviewers need time to look at visuals while listening.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Talking about looks, not problems. Saying 'I chose this color because it looks professional' is weak. Say 'I chose this color because users in testing said the previous one felt untrustworthy.' Always tie visual decisions back to user or business reasoning.

Skipping the problem statement. Many candidates jump straight to what they built without explaining why it needed to exist. Start every portfolio story with the problem.

Being vague about your role. If you worked in a team, be specific: 'I owned the mobile flows while my teammate handled the desktop version.' Interviewers need to know what you did personally, not what the team did collectively.

Getting defensive during critique. If an interviewer pushes back on a design choice during your portfolio review, they are often testing how you handle feedback, not saying your work is bad. Respond with curiosity: 'That is a fair point. The trade-off I was thinking about was...'

Ignoring results. Close every portfolio story with what happened after launch. If you do not have metrics, share qualitative feedback from users or stakeholders. If the project never shipped, say so and explain what you learned from it anyway.

Over-explaining tools. Do not spend several minutes listing software. Mention the tool once, then focus on the decisions you made and the outcomes you achieved.

How many rounds does the Laksh Consultants UI/UX Designer interview typically have?

Candidates typically report two to three rounds. The first is usually a recruiter screening call, followed by a portfolio review with the hiring manager or design lead. A final round may involve a practical design task or a conversation with the end client. The exact number of rounds varies depending on which client Laksh Consultants is placing for.

Is there a design assignment or take-home task in the process?

Some candidates report receiving a short design brief or case study exercise as part of the process. This typically involves solving a real-world UX problem within a set time frame. If you receive one, focus on showing your thinking process clearly, not just polished final screens. Present it as a walkthrough, explaining every key decision you made.

Do I need to know Figma specifically, or will other design tools work?

Figma is the current industry standard for most teams in India. Interviewers typically expect familiarity with it, particularly for components, prototyping, and collaboration features. If your portfolio is built in Adobe XD or Sketch, be ready to explain your tool choices and show that you can adapt. Demonstrating Figma fluency gives you a practical advantage in any design round.

How should I present my portfolio for a remote interview?

Have your portfolio open in a browser tab and key Figma files ready to share before the call starts. Walk the interviewer through one project at a time, pausing after each section to check for questions. Avoid scrolling too quickly through visual screens. Keep a PDF backup ready in case screen sharing has technical issues.
