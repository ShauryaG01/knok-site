01 Overview

Overview

CommIT is one of the most active Data Scientist hirers in India right now, with 172 open roles tracked by knok jobradar as of July 2026. If you have an interview lined up, that demand is good news, but it also means competition is real.

The CommIT Data Scientist interview typically spans multiple rounds covering statistics, machine learning, SQL, and business problem-solving. Candidates report the process is thorough: expect both technical depth and questions about how you communicate and collaborate. This guide walks you through what to expect, how to prepare, and the mistakes that cost candidates offers.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Based on candidate reports, CommIT Data Scientist interviews commonly include questions like these:

  1. Walk me through a machine learning project you have built end to end, from data to deployment.
  2. How do you handle missing data in a real-world dataset? What drives your decision?
  3. Explain the difference between precision and recall. When would you prioritise one over the other?
  4. A business team says the model is not performing well in production. How do you investigate and what do you check first?
  5. How would you design an A/B test to measure the impact of a new product feature?
  6. Explain overfitting. How do you detect it and what steps do you take?
  7. You have a heavily imbalanced dataset for a classification task. How do you approach it?
  8. How do you communicate a model's limitations to someone who does not have a data background?
  9. Write a SQL query to find the second-highest salary in each department.
  10. How would you build a recommendation engine for a product catalogue?
  11. Tell me about a time you disagreed with a business decision based on your data analysis. What did you do?
  12. How do you decide which features to include in a model, and when do you remove them?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk me through a machine learning project you built end to end.

*Situation:* My team was seeing a customer retention problem that the business had flagged as urgent.
*Task:* I was asked to build a churn prediction model so the retention team could reach out to at-risk customers before they left.
*Action:* I pulled two years of transaction and support-ticket data, cleaned it, and engineered features like days-since-last-purchase and complaint frequency. I trained and compared several classifiers, picking the one with the best balance of precision and recall on a held-out validation set. I then worked with engineering to deploy it as a weekly batch scoring job.
*Result:* The retention team started using the scores the following month. Full business attribution took a further quarter to confirm, but early signals were positive and the model stayed in production.

---

Q: Tell me about a time you communicated a model's limitations to a non-technical stakeholder.

*Situation:* I had built a demand forecasting model for a retail client. The product head wanted to use it for decisions well outside its intended scope.
*Task:* I needed to set realistic expectations without undermining confidence in the work or my team.
*Action:* I prepared a one-page summary in plain language showing where the model was reliable and where it was not. I used a simple chart instead of technical metrics and focused on what the business could and could not depend on.
*Result:* The stakeholder appreciated the transparency, scoped the use case appropriately, and the rollout went smoothly. Trust held up through the first review cycle, which is often where over-promised models fall apart.

---

Q: How did you handle a heavily imbalanced dataset?

*Situation:* I was working on a fraud detection problem where genuine fraud cases made up a very small fraction of overall transactions.
*Task:* Build a classifier that could catch fraud without flooding the operations team with false alerts.
*Action:* I tried SMOTE oversampling and class-weight adjustments, then evaluated models using the precision-recall curve rather than overall accuracy, which would have been misleading given the imbalance. I also tuned the decision threshold based on the business cost of missing a real case versus generating a false alert.
*Result:* The final model flagged a useful share of actual fraud in testing while keeping false positives at a level the operations team could handle. They confirmed it fit their review workflow before we went live.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioural questions: Structure every 'tell me about a time' answer as Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep the Situation brief (two or three sentences) and spend most of your time on the Action, since that is what interviewers are evaluating. The Result should be concrete where you can make it so, even if outcomes were still developing.

Problem decomposition for case questions: When given an open-ended prompt like 'how would you build X', interviewers want structured thinking, not an immediate algorithm. A reliable approach: restate the goal, identify what data you would need, outline your modelling approach, discuss how you would evaluate success, and describe what failure would look like and how you would catch it.

Clarify before you answer: For ambiguous questions, ask one or two clarifying questions before diving in. Candidates report this is well-received because it mirrors how real data problems work: the brief is never complete when you receive it.

Show trade-offs: For every technical choice, briefly mention what you considered but did not use and why. 'I chose a gradient boosting model over logistic regression here because the relationships were non-linear, but I kept logistic regression as a baseline for interpretability checks' signals the kind of thinking senior interviewers are looking for.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates report from CommIT interviews, interviewers are looking for a combination of technical depth and practical judgement.

Technical fundamentals: You should be solid on statistics, probability, common algorithms, SQL, and at least one ML framework. Surface-level answers tend to get probed quickly.

Business orientation: Data Scientists at CommIT are expected to connect their work to real outcomes. Interviewers often probe whether you understand why a model matters, not just how it works.

Communication: Can you explain a complex finding clearly to someone outside data? Candidates report that several rounds involve some version of 'explain this to a non-technical person.'

Ownership: Examples where you drove something end to end, dealt with messy data, or pushed back on a flawed brief tend to land well. Passive answers where everything worked cleanly are less convincing.

Collaboration: CommIT teams are cross-functional. Interviewers look for evidence that you have worked alongside engineers, product managers, or business stakeholders, not only other data scientists.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A focused three-to-four week plan covers most of what candidates report being tested on.

Week 1: Foundations
Revise core statistics and probability: distributions, hypothesis testing, and Bayesian thinking. Practise SQL window functions and aggregations. Review bias-variance trade-off, regularisation, and cross-validation until you can explain each one out loud without notes.

Week 2: Algorithms in depth
Go deep on three or four algorithms you already know rather than skimming all of them. For each: how it works, when to use it, what can go wrong, and how you tune it. Practise explaining these aloud without jargon, since interview pressure makes rehearsed explanations much sharper.

Week 3: Case practice and communication
Work through two or three open-ended case problems each day and time yourself. Narrate your thinking as you go. Silent problem-solving in an interview reads as uncertainty, even when the underlying reasoning is sound.

Week 4: Mocks and CommIT specifics
Do at least two full mock interviews with someone who will give honest feedback. Read any CommIT engineering or data content that is publicly available. Prepare four or five concrete project stories using STAR format that cover different skills.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping to algorithms too fast: Interviewers want to see that you understand the problem before you start solving it. Candidates who launch into modelling without discussing data availability, business context, or evaluation criteria often get marked down even when the technical answer is correct.

Vague behavioural answers: 'I worked on a team project' is not a story. CommIT interviewers typically want a specific situation, your specific role, and a specific outcome. Generic answers signal low self-awareness.

Treating overall accuracy as the only metric: In most real problems, it is not the right one. If your answer defaults to 'I would optimise for accuracy,' expect a follow-up challenge. Be ready to discuss precision, recall, AUC, F1, or business-defined thresholds depending on the problem type.

Ignoring data quality: Candidates who jump to modelling without addressing missing values, outliers, or data drift look inexperienced. Messy data is the default in production, and interviewers at CommIT know it.

Not asking clarifying questions: A vague case question is an invitation to show structured thinking. Candidates who answer immediately without clarifying often miss the actual intent of the question.

Underselling collaboration: CommIT Data Scientists work closely with business teams. If your answers make you sound like someone who only talks to other data scientists, that is a gap the interviewer will notice.

Q Questions

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the CommIT Data Scientist interview typically have?

Candidates typically report three to five rounds, covering an initial screening call, one or two technical rounds on statistics, ML, and SQL, a case or take-home assignment, and a final conversation that may include a business or stakeholder-facing component. The exact structure can vary by team and the seniority of the role you are interviewing for.

Is there a take-home assignment?

Many candidates report receiving a take-home assignment, typically a dataset analysis or modelling task. These usually involve cleaning data, building a model, and presenting findings clearly. Treat the communication side as seriously as the technical side: how you explain your choices and limitations often carries as much weight as the model itself.

What salary can I expect as a Data Scientist at CommIT?

Based on the knok jobradar data for July 2026, Data Scientist salaries in India broadly range from 8-16 LPA at entry level (0-2 years) up to 45-70+ LPA for lead and principal roles. CommIT-specific figures are not publicly confirmed; check Glassdoor and levels.fyi for self-reported numbers from current and former CommIT employees to get a more targeted picture.

How important is Python versus R for CommIT interviews?

Python is the dominant language for Data Scientist roles in India, and candidates report that CommIT interviews assume Python proficiency. R knowledge can be a bonus in certain research-focused teams but is rarely a deciding factor. Focus your preparation on Python, standard ML libraries, and SQL.

Do I need deep learning knowledge for this role?

It depends on the specific team, but candidates report that most CommIT Data Scientist rounds focus more on classical ML, statistics, and SQL than on deep learning. Having a working understanding of neural networks and being able to discuss when they are and are not the right tool is generally sufficient, unless the job description specifically calls out deep learning or NLP work.
