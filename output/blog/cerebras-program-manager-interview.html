01 Overview

Overview

Cerebras Systems is one of the most closely watched AI hardware companies in the world, known for its Wafer-Scale Engine chips built to train and run large AI models. With 99 open roles listed as of July 2026, Cerebras is hiring aggressively, and Technical Program Manager is a key role in that growth. TPMs at Cerebras typically sit at the intersection of hardware, software, and systems engineering, coordinating complex cross-functional programs in a fast-paced startup environment.

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

  1. Walk us through a program you managed from concept to delivery. What was your role, and what were the key milestones?
  2. Cerebras builds cutting-edge AI hardware. How would you get up to speed on a deeply technical product you have not worked with before?
  3. Tell us about a time you managed competing priorities across engineering, product, and business teams. How did you make trade-off decisions?
  4. How do you track and communicate program health to leadership? What metrics or signals do you rely on?
  5. Describe a situation where a critical program was at risk of missing its deadline. What did you do?
  6. How do you handle ambiguity in requirements when multiple stakeholders have conflicting visions?
  7. Cerebras operates at the pace of a startup. How do you keep programs on track when plans, priorities, or team structures shift rapidly?
  8. Tell us about a time you identified a process gap and built a new workflow or tool to fix it.
  9. How do you build trust with engineering leads who may be skeptical of program management overhead?
  10. Describe your experience coordinating hardware and software development timelines. What makes hardware programs different?
  11. How would you handle a situation where a partner team's delay is blocking your program, and escalation has not resolved it?
  12. What draws you to Cerebras specifically, and how does this role fit your career goals?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell us about a time a critical program was at risk of missing its deadline. What did you do?

*Situation/Task:* I was leading a platform migration program at my previous company. Midway through, our infrastructure team flagged that a key dependency would ship two weeks late, putting the entire release at risk.

*Action:* I called a war-room meeting with the tech leads from all three workstreams. We mapped out every task on the critical path and identified two that could run in parallel instead of sequentially. I also negotiated with the product team to defer one non-essential feature to the next release, which freed up engineering bandwidth. I updated the executive dashboard the same day so leadership had full visibility.

*Result:* We delivered the migration on the original date, minus the deferred feature. That feature shipped in the following cycle. Leadership appreciated the early transparency, and we adopted the 'critical-path audit' as a standard practice for future programs.

---

Q: How do you build trust with engineering leads who may be skeptical of program management?

*Situation/Task:* When I joined a new team, several senior engineers saw program management as overhead. They had experienced TPMs who created process for process's sake, and they were resistant to status meetings and tracking tools.

*Action:* Instead of rolling out templates on day one, I spent my first few weeks sitting in on engineering standups, reading design docs, and learning the team's pain points. I discovered their biggest frustration was unclear cross-team dependencies that caused last-minute surprises. I built a lightweight dependency tracker (a shared sheet, not a heavy tool) and started sending a weekly one-paragraph summary highlighting risks and blockers. I made sure every communication saved them time rather than adding overhead.

*Result:* Within a quarter, the same leads who had pushed back were actively asking me to coordinate new initiatives. The dependency tracker caught two blocking issues early, preventing what the team estimated would have been significant delivery delays.

---

Q: How do you handle ambiguity when stakeholders have conflicting requirements?

*Situation/Task:* On a data pipeline project, the product team wanted a flexible schema that could support future use cases, while the infrastructure team wanted a rigid, performance-optimized schema. Both had valid reasoning, and there was no clear directive from leadership.

*Action:* I set up a structured decision-making session. Each side presented their case with trade-offs written out. I then facilitated a 'what would have to be true' exercise: for each option, we listed the assumptions that needed to hold. This made the risks concrete rather than abstract. I documented the decision, the reasoning, and the owner, then circulated it to leadership for final sign-off.

*Result:* We went with a phased approach: rigid schema for the first release (meeting the performance need), with a planned extension point for flexibility in the next cycle. Both teams felt heard, and the documented decision prevented revisiting the debate later.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR method. This is the gold standard for behavioural questions. Structure every answer as Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep Situation and Task brief, just a few sentences combined. Spend most of your answer on Action, because that is where interviewers learn how you think. End with a measurable or clearly defined Result.

The 'So What' test. After stating your result, ask yourself: why does this matter to the business? If your result is 'we shipped on time,' add the business impact, for example, 'which meant the sales team could demo the feature at the annual conference.' Cerebras interviewers, like most at fast-paced companies, want to see that you connect execution to outcomes.

Technical Translation framework. For questions about working across hardware and software, show that you can translate between technical and non-technical audiences. Describe the same problem in two ways: how you explained it to engineers, and how you explained it to leadership or external partners. This is especially valuable at Cerebras, where programs span chip design, compilers, and ML frameworks.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Cross-functional leadership. Cerebras builds complex systems that span chip design, firmware, compilers, and ML frameworks. Interviewers want to see that you can drive alignment across teams with very different technical vocabularies and incentive structures.

Comfort with deep technology. You will not be expected to design a chip, but you should demonstrate curiosity and the ability to learn quickly. Show that you have a pattern for getting up to speed on unfamiliar technical domains.

Bias toward action in ambiguity. Startups rarely have perfect information. Interviewers look for candidates who can make progress without waiting for every detail to be finalized.

Transparent communication. Cerebras values TPMs who surface risks early rather than hiding problems. Your examples should highlight moments where you raised a flag, not just moments where everything went smoothly.

Genuine interest in the mission. Cerebras is competing to reshape AI infrastructure. Interviewers notice when a candidate has done their homework on the product, the competitive landscape, and why this work matters.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Week one: Research. Read Cerebras's published blog posts, technical papers, and press coverage. Understand what the Wafer-Scale Engine is, how it differs from GPU-based training, and who their customers are. Review the job description line by line and map each requirement to an experience from your career.

Week two: Story bank. Prepare a handful of STAR stories covering: a program you rescued, a cross-functional conflict you resolved, a process you built from scratch, a technically complex program you managed, a stakeholder you won over, and a situation where you operated with incomplete information. Practice telling each story out loud until it feels natural and concise.

Week three: Mock interviews. Run mock interviews with a friend or mentor, ideally someone with a technical background. Ask for honest feedback on clarity, conciseness, and whether your answers show impact. Refine your stories based on that feedback.

On the day. Have specific questions ready about Cerebras's current programs, team structure, and growth plans. Thoughtful questions signal genuine interest. Arrive early (or log in early for video calls), and keep a short notes document handy with your key stories and data points.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Giving vague, high-level answers. Saying 'I coordinated across teams' without explaining how you did it or what the outcome was. Interviewers want specific actions and results.

Ignoring the technical context. Cerebras is a deep-tech company. Candidates who cannot engage with the technical aspects of program management (even at a high level) may struggle to convince interviewers they can operate effectively in this environment.

Talking too long. Keep your STAR answers focused and concise. A well-structured answer is far more compelling than a rambling one. If the interviewer wants more detail, they will ask.

Not preparing questions for the panel. Asking no questions, or asking only generic ones ('What is the culture like?'), suggests you have not researched the company. Prepare questions that reference Cerebras's specific products, challenges, or recent milestones.

Over-indexing on process. TPMs who talk only about Jira boards, sprint ceremonies, and status templates can come across as process-heavy. Balance process talk with examples of judgment, influence, and problem-solving.

Underselling your results. Many candidates describe what they did but forget to state the outcome clearly. Always close your answer with a concrete result and its business impact.

Candidates report that Cerebras typically conducts a recruiter screen, a hiring manager conversation, and a panel of technical and cross-functional interviews. The exact structure can vary by role and level, so ask your recruiter for a clear outline early in the process.

Does Cerebras ask coding questions for TPM positions?

TPM interviews at Cerebras are typically not coding-heavy, but candidates report being asked to demonstrate technical fluency. You may be asked to read or discuss code, explain system architectures, or walk through how you would debug a cross-team technical issue.

How important is hardware experience for this role?

Having hardware program experience is helpful but not always required. Cerebras values the ability to learn quickly and work across hardware and software boundaries. If you lack hardware experience, highlight examples where you ramped up fast on an unfamiliar technical domain.
