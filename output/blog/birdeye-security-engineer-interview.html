01 Overview

Overview

BirdEye is a SaaS platform for reputation management and customer experience, serving businesses across multiple industries. The company handles sensitive customer data at scale, which makes security a core function rather than an afterthought. As of July 2026, knok jobradar shows BirdEye has 36 open roles across India, with Security Engineer among them. Across the broader Indian market, 628 Security Engineer roles are currently listed on knok jobradar, with Bangalore accounting for the largest share.

Candidates report the interview process typically spans three to four rounds: an initial screening call with HR or a recruiter, one or two technical rounds covering fundamentals and hands-on scenarios, and a final discussion with a senior engineer or hiring manager. The exact structure can vary, so confirm the rounds with your recruiter after the first call.

The Security Engineer role at BirdEye typically sits at the intersection of application security, cloud infrastructure security, and compliance. Expect questions on secure design, vulnerability management, incident response, and the security posture of cloud environments (AWS and GCP come up most often in candidate reports).

02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

These questions are drawn from publicly reported interview experiences and common patterns for Security Engineer roles at Indian SaaS product companies. Exact questions vary by round and interviewer.

  1. Walk me through how you would do a security review of a new feature before it ships.
  2. How do you identify and prioritize vulnerabilities in a large codebase or running application?
  3. Describe your experience with cloud security, particularly in AWS or GCP environments.
  4. What is your approach to threat modeling for a SaaS product that handles customer data?
  5. How have you handled a security incident from detection through to resolution?
  6. What experience do you have with penetration testing or coordinating with external pen testers?
  7. How do you stay current with emerging threats and CVEs relevant to your product stack?
  8. What is your experience with compliance frameworks such as SOC 2 or the ISO information security management standard?
  9. How do you work with engineering teams to get security fixes prioritized and shipped without blocking releases?
  10. Describe a time you had to push back on a product or engineering decision for security reasons.
  11. How would you design a secrets management strategy for a microservices architecture?
  12. What application security testing tools do you use (SAST, DAST, SCA) and how do you integrate them into CI/CD?

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: How have you handled a security incident from detection to resolution?

*Situation:* At my previous company, our monitoring system flagged unusual outbound traffic from a production server late one evening.

*Task:* I was the on-call security engineer and needed to contain the threat, assess the damage, and restore normal operations.

*Action:* I immediately isolated the affected instance from the network to prevent lateral movement. I pulled logs from our SIEM and traced the traffic to a compromised third-party dependency. I coordinated with the DevOps team to redeploy a clean image and rotated all credentials that may have been exposed. I then wrote a detailed incident report and proposed adding the vulnerable package to our automated dependency scanner.

*Result:* The fix was deployed the following day. The post-incident review led to a new dependency auditing policy that we embedded directly into our CI/CD pipeline, catching similar issues automatically going forward.

---

Q: How do you work with engineering teams to get security fixes prioritized?

*Situation:* After an internal security review, I found several medium-severity injection vulnerabilities in an older API service. The engineering team had a packed sprint focused on an upcoming product launch.

*Task:* I needed to get these fixed without derailing the launch timeline.

*Action:* I prepared a risk summary in plain language, showing potential business impact rather than just a CVSS score. I paired with the lead developer to walk through each finding and offered to write the fix for two of them myself. I proposed a phased timeline: critical issues before launch, medium issues in the following sprint.

*Result:* All critical issues were resolved before launch and the medium issues shipped in the next sprint. The engineering lead later said the collaborative approach made security feel like a shared responsibility rather than a gate.

---

Q: Walk me through how you would do a security review of a new feature.

*Situation:* Our product team wanted to add a third-party OAuth integration that would let users connect their social accounts.

*Task:* I was asked to review the design before any code was written.

*Action:* I ran a threat modeling session with the engineer and product manager using the STRIDE framework. We identified risks around token storage, redirect URI validation, and account-linking attacks. I documented each threat with a recommended control and created a security checklist the team could use during code review.

*Result:* Several high-severity issues were caught in the design phase and never made it to code. The checklist became a reusable template for future integrations across the team.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

Use a clear structure for technical questions. State your understanding of the problem, describe your approach or methodology, give a concrete example from past work, and explain the outcome. Avoid jumping straight to tools or commands without first framing the context.

Use STAR for behavioural questions. Situation (the context), Task (your responsibility), Action (what you specifically did), Result (the outcome and what you learned). Keep Situation and Task brief. Spend the majority of your answer on Action and Result, because that is where interviewers assess your thinking and ownership.

For compliance questions, show the 'why' not just the 'what'. Naming a framework is not enough. Explain how you implemented a specific control, how you ran a gap assessment, or how you worked with a team to close audit findings. Interviewers at product companies care about execution, not certification knowledge alone.

For architecture and design questions, think out loud. Interviewers want to follow your reasoning. State your assumptions, call out trade-offs, and invite feedback. A candidate who thinks clearly and communicates well is often preferred over one who jumps to the 'right answer' without explanation.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

BirdEye handles data for a large number of businesses, so the security team typically needs engineers who can balance rigour with shipping velocity. Candidates report that interviewers value people who communicate risk clearly to non-security stakeholders, not just those who write detailed vulnerability reports.

Expect the interview to test whether you can own a problem end-to-end: from spotting a vulnerability to getting the fix shipped and validated. Demonstrating hands-on experience with cloud security (AWS, GCP, or Azure) and application security testing is commonly cited as important for this type of role.

Interviewers also look for curiosity and self-direction. Candidates who follow security research, track CVEs relevant to SaaS stacks, and have clear opinions on the tools they use tend to stand out. Being able to explain why you chose a particular approach, not just what you did, signals depth of understanding.

Collaboration is a recurring theme in candidate reports. Security engineers at product companies must influence without authority. Show that you know how to build trust with engineering and product teams rather than acting as a gatekeeper.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Phase 1: Understand the product and context. Spend a day or two learning how BirdEye works: what data it collects, who its customers are, and how its platform is structured. This gives you concrete context for questions about protecting a SaaS product. Read the job description carefully and note any specific technologies or compliance frameworks mentioned.

Phase 2: Refresh your technical fundamentals. Review the OWASP Top Ten, common cloud misconfigurations in AWS or GCP, threat modeling basics (STRIDE is a solid starting point), and incident response workflows. If the job description mentions specific tools such as a SIEM, SAST scanner, or secrets manager, make sure you can speak to your experience with them or close equivalents.

Phase 3: Prepare your stories. Map your past experience to common interview themes: incident response, vulnerability management, working cross-functionally, and secure design decisions. Write out your STAR answers and practice saying them out loud. Aim for stories that are specific about your personal actions rather than what 'we' did as a team.

Phase 4: Practice and refine. Do a mock round with a peer or record yourself. Review your answers for vague language and replace it with concrete details. Prepare two or three questions to ask the interviewer about the team's current challenges and how security is embedded in the development process.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Treating compliance as a topic to name-drop. Candidates who list framework names such as SOC 2 or the ISO information security management standard without explaining how they implemented controls tend to get screened out. Be ready to describe what you actually built, changed, or audited.

Skipping the business context. Security engineers who talk only about technical severity without connecting it to business risk often struggle in cross-functional roles. Interviewers at product companies want to see that you understand why a finding matters to the business, not just to the security team.

Giving vague incident stories. Saying 'I handled a breach' without walking through your specific actions and the outcome is a common red flag. Practice your STAR stories until each step, especially Action and Result, is concrete and specific.

Not asking questions. Candidates who have nothing to ask the interviewer often come across as disengaged. Ask about the team's current security challenges, how incidents are handled, or what the security roadmap looks like for the coming year.

Underestimating cloud security depth. For a SaaS company, cloud security knowledge is typically as important as application security. Brush up on IAM policies, network segmentation, logging and alerting, and common misconfigurations in your preferred cloud provider before the interview.
