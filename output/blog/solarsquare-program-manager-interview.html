01 Overview

Overview

SolarSquare is one of India's fastest-growing rooftop solar companies, focused on helping homeowners and businesses switch to clean energy. The company operates across hardware (panels, inverters), software (monitoring dashboards, customer portals), and a field services layer, which makes the Technical Program Manager role genuinely cross-functional.

As of the knok jobradar snapshot from July 2026, SolarSquare had 177 open roles across the company, a sign of a company in active growth. A TPM here is expected to coordinate engineering, product, hardware vendors, and field operations teams toward shared delivery goals.

Candidates typically go through a structured process covering program management fundamentals, technical depth, cross-functional collaboration, and alignment with SolarSquare's mission. The process commonly includes a recruiter screen, one or two technical or case-based rounds, and a final leadership conversation, though candidates report variations in the exact format.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Tell me about a complex cross-functional project you managed end to end.

*Situation:* Our company was launching a new customer-facing energy monitoring app that needed input from three engineering squads (mobile, backend, IoT firmware), a hardware vendor, and the sales team.

*Task:* I was responsible for delivering the MVP within a fixed quarter while keeping all five groups aligned on scope and timelines.

*Action:* I set up a shared delivery tracker visible to all teams, ran a weekly cross-squad sync, and created a dependency map that flagged risks early. When the firmware team hit a chip shortage, I coordinated a parallel workstream so backend and mobile could proceed using a simulator, keeping the overall timeline intact.

*Result:* We shipped the MVP on schedule. The product team later described adoption in the first month as exceeding their projection, and the launch became a reference point for how cross-functional delivery should run.

---

Q: Describe a time you pushed back on an unrealistic deadline.

*Situation:* A senior stakeholder asked my team to deliver a full API integration with a third-party logistics platform in a single sprint.

*Task:* I needed to protect delivery quality while keeping the stakeholder confident in the team.

*Action:* I prepared a scope breakdown showing the actual work involved, the dependency on the vendor's sandbox environment, and the risk of rushing QA. I proposed a phased plan: a read-only integration first, then write access in the following sprint. I walked the stakeholder through the trade-off in a focused meeting.

*Result:* The stakeholder agreed to the phased approach. The first phase shipped without issues, and the second phase had zero critical production bugs. The stakeholder later credited the phased structure for the clean delivery.

---

Q: How do you handle competing priorities across teams?

*Situation:* I was managing a platform migration when three different product teams each escalated their blockers as 'critical' on the same day.

*Task:* I needed to triage fairly without losing team trust or slipping the migration deadline.

*Action:* I called a joint triage call with all three leads, shared the migration critical path on screen, and asked each team to map their blocker to a specific milestone. Two of the three blockers turned out to share a downstream dependency, so I consolidated them into a single fix. The third was independent and I scheduled it for the following sprint.

*Result:* All three leads felt heard, the consolidated fix reduced rework, and the migration stayed on track.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioral questions. Structure every 'tell me about a time' answer with Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep the Situation and Task portions brief so you can spend the bulk of your speaking time on the Action (what you specifically did) and the Result (what changed because of you). A common mistake is spending too long on context and not enough on your actual contribution.

Clarify before answering hypotheticals. For questions like 'how would you structure a roadmap for our IoT product', pause briefly and ask one or two clarifying questions before you dive in. For example: 'Is this a new product or a rebuild? Who is the primary user?' Interviewers at SolarSquare typically appreciate structured thinking because the company deals with genuinely complex, multi-stakeholder programs.

Anchor answers with specifics from your own experience. Where you can, use real metrics from your past work: team size, number of parallel workstreams, or delivery timelines. If you don't have a precise number, describe the outcome qualitatively but concretely.

Mirror the company's domain language. SolarSquare sits at the intersection of hardware, software, and services. Using terms like 'field operations', 'firmware dependencies', or 'installation timelines' where relevant shows you understand the domain, not just the PM craft.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Cross-functional ownership. SolarSquare's TPMs work across engineering, hardware vendors, and field teams. Interviewers want evidence that you can hold a program together when the teams involved have very different cadences, tools, and definitions of 'done'.

Structured communication. Expect to be assessed on how clearly you explain a complex situation. Answers with a clear beginning, middle, and end signal that you can write the status update emails and run the stakeholder meetings the role demands.

Mission alignment. The company operates in the clean energy space and candidates report that interviewers probe for genuine interest in the problem, not just the title. Be ready to talk about why solar energy or climate tech appeals to you.

Bias toward action. SolarSquare is growing fast. Interviewers look for candidates who make decisions with incomplete information rather than waiting for perfect clarity. Stories where you moved the needle without having every answer tend to land well.

Stakeholder management under pressure. Questions about pushback, difficult deadlines, and competing priorities are common. Interviewers want to see that you can hold a firm position when you are right without damaging relationships.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

Before the first round: Research SolarSquare's product line, specifically their solar monitoring app, customer portal, and any IoT hardware they produce. Read publicly available coverage of their growth story and understand the customer journey from a sales inquiry through installation to ongoing monitoring.

Prepare your story bank: Select four or five programs from your experience and map them to common TPM themes: a cross-functional delivery, a missed milestone, a stakeholder conflict, a process improvement, and a technical risk you surfaced early. Practice telling each story concisely using the STAR structure.

For the technical round: Brush up on core TPM tools: dependency mapping, critical path analysis, risk registers, and sprint planning basics. If you have experience with IoT, firmware, or field service operations, prepare specific examples. If you don't, prepare honest answers about how you would learn quickly on the job.

For case or hypothetical questions: Practice thinking aloud. Interviewers want to see your reasoning process, not just your conclusion. Sketch your approach briefly before speaking, and clarify assumptions before diving in.

Questions to ask the interviewer: Ask about team structure (how many engineers per TPM, how many programs run in parallel), the current biggest delivery challenge, and how success is measured in the first few months. These questions show you are evaluating fit as seriously as they are.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Vague impact statements. Saying 'the project was successful' without explaining what changed is a missed opportunity. Even without exact numbers, describe the outcome specifically: what shipped, what risk was avoided, what the team could do next as a result.

Overloading on tools and process. Mentioning Jira, Confluence, and OKRs in every answer can feel like name-dropping. What interviewers care about is why you chose a particular approach and whether it worked.

Skipping the conflict. Many candidates tell only success stories. Interviewers at growth-stage companies often learn more from how you handled a failure or a disagreement. Don't sanitize the story.

Not connecting to the company's domain. Generic TPM answers that could apply to any company miss the chance to show you have thought about solar energy, hardware timelines, or field deployment challenges specifically.

Underselling your own role. Use 'I' when describing your decisions and actions, not 'we'. It is fine to credit the team, but the interviewer is assessing you. Make your individual contribution clear.

Jumping to answers too quickly. For complex hypotheticals, diving straight into a solution without framing the problem first signals shallow thinking. Take a brief moment to structure your approach before you speak.

Frequently asked

How many rounds does the SolarSquare TPM interview typically have?

Candidates report a process that typically includes a recruiter or HR screening call, one or two technical or program management interviews, and a final round with a senior leader or hiring manager. The exact number can vary based on the seniority of the role and the team's schedule. Confirm the full process with your recruiter after the first call so you can prepare accordingly.

Does SolarSquare expect TPM candidates to have a hardware or solar background?

Not necessarily. Candidates from software-only backgrounds have reported receiving and accepting offers at SolarSquare. What matters more is your ability to learn a new domain quickly and to coordinate teams with very different working styles. Showing genuine curiosity about clean energy or IoT during the interview does help your case.

Is there a case study or take-home assignment in the SolarSquare interview?

Some candidates report a case-style discussion in the technical round rather than a formal take-home assignment. This typically involves a program scenario and a request to walk through your approach out loud. Preparing to think aloud and frame your answer clearly before diving in is useful regardless of the exact format.

How important is a PMP or Agile certification for this role?

Certifications are rarely the deciding factor for a TPM role. Interviewers typically care more about demonstrated outcomes from programs you have actually run. Being fluent in Agile and Scrum terminology is still useful because most engineering teams at product companies work in sprints and expect their TPMs to understand the cadence.
