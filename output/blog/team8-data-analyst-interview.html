02 Most Asked Questions

Most Asked Questions

Technical and SQL

  1. Walk me through a dashboard or report you built end to end. What business question did it answer?
  2. Write a SQL query to find users who made their first transaction within a week of signing up.
  3. How do you approach a messy dataset you have never seen before, with no documentation?
  4. How would you analyze user churn for a fintech product with limited historical data?
  5. How would you design a metrics framework for a new B2B cybersecurity product entering a market?

Behavioral and stakeholder

  1. A business stakeholder says your numbers look wrong. How do you handle that conversation?
  2. Describe a time you found an insight that changed a business decision. What was the outcome?
  3. You have data requests from three different teams, all marked urgent. How do you decide what to work on first?
  4. Describe a time your analysis produced a result you did not expect. How did you handle it?
  5. Tell me about a time you had to present a finding that leadership did not want to hear.

Strategic and case-style

  1. Team8 invests in early-stage companies. If you joined a portfolio company on day one, how would you help them define their first key metrics?
  2. Explain cohort analysis to a product manager who has never worked with data before.

03 Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Sample Answers (STAR Format)

Q: Walk me through a dashboard or report you built end to end.

*Situation:* My previous employer's sales team tracked performance in separate spreadsheets, with no shared view of which product lines were converting fastest.

*Task:* The VP of Sales asked me to build a weekly dashboard she could use to run Monday reviews.

*Action:* I pulled data from three sources: the CRM, the billing system, and a product usage table. I wrote SQL to join them on customer ID, cleaned mismatched date formats, and built a Tableau view broken down by product, region, and rep. I shared an early draft with the VP to confirm the layout matched how she thought about the business before finalizing.

*Result:* Prep time for the weekly review dropped from a few hours to minutes. Within a month, the team spotted that one region was consistently underperforming and launched a targeted training sprint.

---

Q: A stakeholder says your numbers are wrong. How do you handle it?

*Situation:* A marketing manager at my previous company insisted our campaign attribution numbers were undercounting conversions compared to her own spreadsheet.

*Task:* I needed to find the source of the discrepancy quickly, without damaging a relationship I relied on for requirements.

*Action:* I asked the manager to walk through both data sources with me side by side. I explained the SQL logic and she walked me through her spreadsheet formulas. We found that her sheet was counting users who clicked multiple ads more than once.

*Result:* We aligned on a single source of truth and updated the attribution logic together. The manager became an advocate for centralizing data rather than pulling manual exports.

---

Q: How would you analyze user churn for a fintech product with limited data?

*Situation:* A fintech startup I supported was seeing rising cancellations but had no structured view of when or why users were leaving.

*Task:* I was asked to surface churn patterns the product team could act on within that quarter.

*Action:* I segmented users into cohorts by signup date and plan type, then identified where each cohort dropped off in the product lifecycle. I ranked churn predictors using a simple logistic model and found that users who did not complete onboarding in their first week had markedly higher cancellation rates. Industry surveys on SaaS retention commonly cite early onboarding drop-off as a leading churn signal, which gave the team confidence the finding was structural.

*Result:* The product team prioritized an onboarding checklist in their next sprint, and stakeholders reported that publicly reported retention metrics trended upward after launch.

04 Answer Frameworks

Answer Frameworks

STAR for behavioral questions. Use Situation, Task, Action, Result. Keep each part to two to three sentences. The Action section should take the most time. Be specific about what you personally did, not what the broader team did.

PDAC for case and analytical questions.

  • *Problem:* Restate the question in your own words and call out assumptions upfront.
  • *Data:* Identify what data you would need, where you would find it, and any quality concerns.
  • *Analysis:* Walk through your approach. Name specific methods: cohort analysis, segmentation, regression, funnel analysis.
  • *Conclusion:* State what the output would look like and, critically, what business decision it would inform.

For live SQL questions, candidates report that talking through your logic before typing reassures interviewers that you understand the problem, not just the syntax. Read the output of each step before moving to the next.

05 What Interviewers Want

What Interviewers Want

Based on what candidates report, Team8 interviewers typically prioritize four things.

Business sense over pure technical skill. They want analysts who connect a metric to a decision, not analysts who produce correct queries with no actionable output. When you describe past work, always anchor it to what changed as a result.

Comfort with ambiguity. Team8 backs early-stage companies where data is often sparse or poorly structured. Interviewers tend to probe whether you ask good clarifying questions before diving in, or whether you freeze when requirements are unclear.

Clear communication. Expect at least one question that asks you to explain a technical concept to a non-technical person. Practice explaining cohort analysis, A/B testing, or funnel metrics in plain language before your interview.

Ownership. Early-stage companies cannot afford analysts who wait to be told exactly what to measure. Interviewers commonly look for examples of candidates who proactively identified a question the business had not thought to ask.

06 Preparation Plan

Preparation Plan

A four-step sequence candidates report working through:

Step 1: SQL fundamentals. Practice window functions, CTEs, and multi-table joins. Focus on writing readable queries, not just correct ones. Team8 interviewers typically ask you to explain your logic as you write, so practice talking aloud while coding.

Step 2: Case study readiness. Pick two or three fintech or cybersecurity products you use or know well. Think through what metrics you would track, what a useful dashboard would show, and what a churn analysis might reveal. Having a mental model for a familiar product gives you concrete material to reference in case questions.

Step 3: STAR story bank. Prepare five to six stories from your own work history that cover: finding an unexpected insight, disagreeing with a stakeholder, delivering a difficult finding, and building something from scratch with limited data. Rehearse them aloud so the structure feels natural, not scripted.

Step 4: Questions to ask. Candidates report that thoughtful reverse questions leave a strong impression. Ask how analytics work currently flows between Team8 and its portfolio companies, or what a successful first few months looks like in the role. It shows you are already thinking about impact.

07 Common Mistakes

Common Mistakes

Jumping straight to analysis. When given a case or open-ended question, many candidates dive into methods before defining the problem. Slow down. State your assumptions, confirm the goal, then proceed.

Vague STAR answers. 'We improved conversion' tells an interviewer nothing. Tie every result to something concrete and observable, even if it is qualitative: 'the team dropped the manual export process entirely' is stronger than a vague claim of improvement.

Not asking clarifying questions. Interviewers at venture-backed companies want to see how you handle uncertainty. Sitting in silence or assuming you know what is meant signals poor stakeholder habits.

Ignoring the 'so what.' A technically correct analysis that does not tell the business what to do next is an incomplete answer. Always close with a recommendation or a clear next step.

Over-indexing on tools. Listing every tool you know without connecting them to outcomes is noise. Pick the one or two you use best and talk about what you built with them and what changed because of it.
